# Chapter 11 — Roof Framing and Eaves (小屋組・軒 / koyagumi, noki)

**Scope.** This chapter sets the rules for the roof structure of traditional Japanese buildings: the roof forms and what they demand of the frame; the principal roof-framing systems (the Japanese roof 和小屋 *wagoya*, the rafter-truss 扠首組 *sasugumi* of thatched farmhouses, the king-post truss 真束小屋組 / 洋小屋 *yōgoya*, and the hidden-roof system 野小屋 *nogoya* of temples with its cantilevers 桔木 *hanegi*); rafters and their spacing; the members of the eave (茅負, 裏甲, 広小舞, 淀, 鼻隠し, 破風 …); how deep an eave can be and how it is carried; ridges and ridge ornaments; the structural consequences of the roofing material; hips and valleys in practice; roof curvature; and representative member sizes. **Geometry** (slopes, hip angles, cuts, curves) is in → Ch. 05. **Architectural styles** of temples and shrines and bracket sets (組物 *kumimono*) are in → Ch. 12. **Walls, posts, beams below the roof** are in → Ch. 10. **Erection sequence** (棟上げ *muneage*) is in → Ch. 15.

## 1. Roof forms and their framing implications

### 1.1 Basic forms

| Form | Japanese | Description | Framing consequence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gable | 切妻 *kirizuma* | two planes meeting at a ridge; triangular gable ends (妻 *tsuma*) | simplest; ridge (棟木) runs the full length; gable ends need a gable frame and support for the verge overhang (螻羽 *keraba*) |
| Hip | 寄棟 *yosemune* | four planes; two trapezoids and two triangles; ridge shorter than the building | four hips (隅木) from the eave corners to the ridge ends; jack rafters; support for the hip tails (§ 8) |
| Hip-and-gable | 入母屋 *irimoya* | hipped lower part, gabled upper part | hips on the lower part, and a gable triangle standing on the hip roof; the most complex common form |
| Pyramidal | 宝形 *hōgyō* (方形) | square (or polygonal) plan, all hips meeting at a point | no ridge; hips converge on a central post (心束 / 真束) carrying the finial (露盤 *roban*, 宝珠 *hōju*) |
| Mono-pitch | 片流れ *katanagare* | a single plane | the high wall is taller; the rafters span between two plates at different heights |
| Unequal gable | 招き屋根 *maneki-yane* | a gable with one slope much longer than the other ("beckoning" roof) | the ridge is off-centre; the long slope often has a break or a second, lower pitch |

### 1.2 Gable (切妻)

- **Ridge and purlins** run the length of the building and extend beyond the gable walls to carry the verge (螻羽). The projection of the extended purlins (母屋の出 / 棟木の出) is typically 1–2 shaku (≈300–600 mm) in houses.
- **Gable framing:** the gable end (妻) is framed by the end tie-beam (妻梁 *tsumabari*) with posts (束 *tsuka*) up to the purlins and ridge. In temples and shrines, the visible gable ornament (妻飾り *tsuma-kazari*: rainbow beam 虹梁 *kōryō* with a bottle-strut 大瓶束 *taiheizuka*, or a sasu truss 扠首組 in older work) is itself this frame (→ Ch. 12).
- **Bargeboards (破風 *hafu*)** close the gable edge (§ 4.7).

**R11-001** — *Must.* Support the verge overhang of a gable on extensions of the purlins, ridge and wall-plates, which must continue **over at least one full interior support** (a gable post or the gable tie-beam). Never hang the verge from the bargeboard alone. *Why:* the verge carries roofing, snow and wind uplift. The bargeboard is a finishing board and has no cantilever capacity.

### 1.3 Hip (寄棟)

- Four **hips (隅木)** run from the plate corners to the two ends of the ridge (or, for a square plan, to a single apex: 宝形).
- The **ridge length** = building length − building width (for equal slopes all round). Where this is short, the hips crowd together and a 宝形-like apex results.
- The hip tails need support: at the ridge end (on the ridge-beam end, or on a post under it), and at intermediate points on **flying beams (飛び梁 *tobi-bari*)** that run from the corner or plate diagonally to the interior roof beams and carry posts (束) under the hips (§ 8.2).
- Hips are less efficient against wind at the gable end, but they shelter all four walls and brace the roof in plan. Hip roofs were preferred for farmhouses in many regions and for high-status buildings.

### 1.4 Hip-and-gable (入母屋)

Structurally an 入母屋 roof is a **gable roof (the upper part, 母屋 *moya* in the sense of "main body")** sitting on a **hip roof skirt**:

- The lower part is framed like a hip roof: eave all round, four hips from the corners.
- The **gable** of the upper part stands **set back** from the end eave. Its plane lies somewhere between the end wall line and the ridge end. The gable wall (妻壁), with its bargeboards (入母屋破風), stands on the lower roof. Its bottom member (the gable sill, often on top of the hips or on a beam spanning between them) carries the gable triangle.
- The hips of the lower part stop at the gable plane. They are received on the gable-sill beam, or on posts, or they pass under it to the purlins behind.
- The junction of gable and lower roof is covered by a small ridge (降棟 *kudarimune* along the bargeboards, and a horizontal ridge where the gable wall meets the lower roof). In tiled roofs these are built up with tiles (→ § 6).
- The **setback of the gable** (how far in from the eave) is a design proportion that changed over time and varies by region and style (→ Ch. 12). It fixes the lengths of the hips and the height of the gable.

**R11-002** — *Must.* In an 入母屋 roof, provide a positive structural seat for the gable triangle: a gable-sill beam (or equivalent) on posts or on the hips, tied back to the interior roof beams. Do not rest it on the lower roof's rafters. *Why:* the gable wall with its bargeboards, ornaments (懸魚) and ridge tiles is heavy and exposed to wind. Rafters are neither placed nor sized to carry it.

### 1.5 Pyramidal (宝形) and polygonal roofs

All hips meet at a **central post (心束 *shinzuka* / 真束 *shinzuka*)** at the apex. The hips are tenoned or housed into it, and it carries the finial. In small buildings the central post is short and stands on a cross of beams. In octagonal halls (八角円堂, e.g. the Yumedono of Hōryū-ji) the arrangement is the same with eight hips. Hip geometry for polygonal plans is in → Ch. 05 § 8.3.

**R11-003** — *Must.* The central post of a 宝形 roof must be supported by beams that carry it to the walls, or it must be designed to hang from the hips as a compression ring. The hip tails must be positively fixed to it. *Why:* the hips push outward at their feet and inward at the apex. The apex joint holds the whole roof together, and a loose apex lets the hips slide and the eaves spread.

### 1.6 Shrine roof forms (framing notes)

The architectural definitions are in → Ch. 12. Framing points:

| Style | Japanese | Roof type and entry | Framing notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Shinmei | 神明造 *shinmei-zukuri* | straight gable, entrance on the long side (平入 *hira-iri*); thatched | **free-standing ridge posts** (棟持柱 *munamochi-bashira*) outside the gable walls carry the ridge directly; the bargeboards extend through the roof as chigi (千木); log-like ridge weights (鰹木 *katsuogi*) on the ridge |
| Taisha | 大社造 *taisha-zukuri* | gable, entrance on the gable side (妻入 *tsuma-iri*); nearly square plan | central post (心御柱 *shin-no-mihashira*) and ridge posts (宇豆柱 *uzubashira*) at the gable centres; large, steep, curved roof |
| Nagare | 流造 *nagare-zukuri* | gable, 平入, front slope extended to cover a porch (向拝 *kōhai*) | the front rafters continue from the main body across to the porch posts (a long front slope, often with a change in pitch at the body–porch junction); the commonest shrine form |
| Kasuga | 春日造 *kasuga-zukuri* | gable, 妻入, with a small lean-to roof (庇 *hisashi*) over the steps on the gable front | the porch roof meets the main gable with small hips and valleys; small scale, curved bark roofs |
| Hachiman | 八幡造 *hachiman-zukuri* | two parallel gabled buildings, front and rear, joined | an internal **valley gutter** (樋) between the two roofs; the joined eaves must be framed and drained |
| Gongen | 権現造 *gongen-zukuri* | main hall and worship hall joined by a connecting hall (石の間 *ishi-no-ma*) | complex intersections of 入母屋 roofs, many valleys and 千鳥破風 / 唐破風; extensive hip-and-valley geometry |

**R11-004** — *Must.* Wherever roofs of different buildings or parts meet (八幡造, 権現造, porch-to-body junctions), design the valley and its gutter first: framing, falls, the gutter lining, and the overflow route. Only then frame the rafters. *Why:* concealed valley gutters are the leading cause of decay in joined shrine roofs. The framing must give them adequate fall and access.

## 2. Roof structural systems

### 2.1 Load path and the basic choice

Japanese roofs are **heavy** (tile, mud, bark, thatch; § 7) and the eaves are **deep** (§ 5). Two fundamentally different ways of carrying them have been used:

- **Beam systems (和小屋 *wagoya*):** roof load → rafters → purlins → **short posts (小屋束)** → **roof beams (小屋梁) in bending** → wall-plates and posts. The rafters and purlins can follow any roof shape because the posts can be any height.
- **Triangulated systems (扠首組 *sasugumi*, 洋小屋 *yōgoya*):** the roof slopes themselves (sasu, or principal rafters) form a triangle with a tie. The members work mainly in axial force, and the tie resists the outward thrust.

**R11-005** — *Must.* Identify for every roof which system carries it, and follow its load path through to the ground. Beam systems need deep beams and stiff posts. Triangulated systems need tension-capable ties and joints at the feet. *Why:* mixing the logic (for example, cutting the tie of a sasu truss to gain headroom, or removing posts from a wagoya because "the rafters will carry it") is a major cause of failure in altered old buildings.

### 2.2 The Japanese roof (和小屋 wagoya)

The dominant roof structure of Japanese houses from the early modern period onward, and of the hidden roofs of many temples.

```
         棟木 munagi (ridge)
              ▄
             /█\          垂木 taruki (rafters) on 母屋 moya (purlins)
       母屋 ▄/ █ \▄ 母屋
          / █  █  █ \
   母屋 ▄/  █  █  █  \▄ 母屋
       / │  █  █  █  │ \
  軒桁▄/  │  █  █  █  │  \▄ 軒桁 nokigeta (wall-plate)
  ════╪═══╪══╪══╪══╪══╪═══╪════   小屋梁 koyabari (roof beam)
      │                   │        █ = 小屋束 koyazuka (roof posts)
      柱 hashira           柱
```

**Components:**

| Member | Japanese | Function | Representative spacing |
|---|---|---|---|
| Roof beam | 小屋梁 *koyabari* | spans between wall-plates (or posts); carries the roof posts; works in bending | 1 ken (6 shaku, ≈1,820 mm) apart |
| Roof post | 小屋束 *koyazuka* | stands on the roof beam; carries a purlin | along the beam, under each purlin (≈3 shaku, 910 mm) |
| Purlin | 母屋 *moya* | runs along the building; carries the rafters | 3 shaku (≈910 mm) apart, measured horizontally |
| Ridge beam | 棟木 *munagi* | the top purlin | — |
| Wall-plate (eave plate) | 軒桁 *nokigeta* | the lowest purlin, on the wall posts | — |
| Rafters | 垂木 *taruki* | span purlin to purlin, cantilever at the eave | 1.5 shaku (≈455 mm) apart in houses (§ 3) |
| Sway bracing | 振れ止め *furedome* | horizontal member tying the roof posts along the building | one line along the roof at mid-height of the posts, more in tall roofs |
| Roof braces | 小屋筋かい *koya-sujikai*, 雲筋かい *kumo-sujikai* | diagonal boards between roof posts (in the vertical plane along the building, or from post to purlin) | every few bays, at both ends |
| Tie-through | 小屋貫 *koyanuki* | penetrating ties through roof posts (older practice, instead of or with sway bracing) | — |
| Double beam | 二重梁 *nijūbari* | a second, shorter roof beam higher up on posts, in large roofs | where the post heights would be excessive |
| Longitudinal beam | 牛梁 *ushibari* | a heavy beam along the building that supports the roof beams at mid-span (minka) | as required |

**Joints (summary; details in → Ch. 07 and → Ch. 08):**
- roof post to roof beam: short tenon (短ほぞ), often with a staple (かすがい) or modern plate;
- roof post to purlin: short tenon or housing;
- purlin splices: over a post, with the lap-dovetail on a seat (腰掛蟻継ぎ) or the lap-sickle on a seat (腰掛鎌継ぎ) (→ R07 series), placed just off the post;
- roof beam to wall-plate: see § 2.3 (京呂 / 折置);
- roof beam splices (in long buildings with interior posts): over a support, with the pursuing splice with draw-pins (追掛大栓継ぎ) or the pursuing splice on a seat (台持継ぎ).

**Characteristics.** The wagoya is simple to set out and to erect, and it tolerates irregular plans because the purlins can be supported wherever a post can stand on a beam. Its weaknesses are:
1. the roof beams work in **bending**, so they must be deep, and spans much over 2 ken (≈3.6 m) need either very large timbers (logs) or intermediate supports;
2. it has **little inherent stiffness** in the longitudinal direction, since the posts are nearly pinned at both ends. Bracing (振れ止め, 小屋筋かい) is essential.

**R11-006** — *Must.* Brace every wagoya longitudinally with sway bracing (振れ止め) and diagonal roof braces (小屋筋かい) at both ends and at intervals. *Why:* a row of short posts pinned top and bottom is a mechanism. Without bracing, earthquake or wind racks the roof along its length. Japanese building law requires 振れ止め in roof framing (Enforcement Order Art. 46; → Ch. 17).

**R11-007** — *Should.* Place roof beams at 1-ken (≈1,820 mm) centres, roof posts at the purlin positions (≈910 mm), and purlins at ≈910 mm. Depart from this grid only with a check of the purlin and rafter spans. *Why:* this grid matches the standard member sizes (§ 10) and the post/wall module (→ Ch. 02). Wider spacings need larger purlins and beams than the stock sizes.

**R11-008** — *Must.* Stand roof posts square on the beam and directly under the purlin they carry. Never stand one on a beam at mid-span where it is not accounted for in the beam's sizing, or offset from the purlin line. *Why:* point loads at mid-span are the governing case for roof-beam bending, and an offset post introduces eccentric loads the short tenons cannot resist.

### 2.3 Beam-on-plate (京呂組 kyōrogumi) and plate-on-beam (折置組 oriokigumi)

The two ways of meeting the roof beam and the wall-plate on top of the wall posts are among the most basic choices in Japanese framing.

```
    京呂組 kyōrogumi                      折置組 oriokigumi
    (beam sits ON the plate)             (plate sits ON the beam; beam sits on the post)

        小屋梁 ────────                          桁 ═══════
       ═══╪═══════ 桁 keta                  ──────╪────── 小屋梁
          │                                       │
          │ 柱                                    │ 柱
          │                                       │
    beam ends may fall anywhere           a post is needed under
    along the plate                       every roof beam
```

**京呂組 (kyōrogumi): beam on the plate.**
- The wall-plate (軒桁) is supported on the posts. The roof beams are laid **on top of the plate**, crossing it, and are joined to it with a **cogged lap (渡りあご *watari-ago*)** or a **helmet dovetail (兜蟻 *kabuto-ari*)** (→ Ch. 08).
- **Advantage:** roof beams can be placed independently of post positions, so the plan (window and door openings, room layouts) is freed from the roof grid. The wall-plate is continuous and can be spliced anywhere between posts.
- **Disadvantage:** the roof-beam load reaches the post through the plate in bending and shear. The plate must be sized for the point loads between posts. The beam end is only lapped over the plate and depends on the joint (and today on metal straps) for uplift and pull-out.
- **Use:** the norm in houses from the Edo period onward in most regions, and in almost all modern post-and-beam work.

**折置組 (oriokigumi): plate on the beam.**
- The roof beam is set **directly on the post top**. The post tenon passes through the beam (often a long or double tenon into the plate above as well). The wall-plate is laid **on top of the beam ends**.
- **Advantage:** a direct load path (beam → post) with no bending in the plate. The joint stack is very robust. It is the older form, and suited to heavy thatched and tile roofs.
- **Disadvantage:** a post is needed under **every** roof beam, so the wall is fixed to the beam grid and openings are restricted. The plate is interrupted by or notched over each beam end.
- **Use:** older minka and some regional traditions. It is common where the roof beam grid and the post grid coincide anyway (regular bays).

**R11-009** — *Must.* In 京呂組, size the wall-plate for the roof-beam reactions as point loads between posts, and place each roof beam either directly over a post or within the span the plate can carry. *Why:* in 京呂組 the plate is a beam, not merely a tie.

**R11-010** — *Should.* Choose 折置組 when the posts are regular and the roof is heavy, and 京呂組 when the plan requires freedom of openings. In repair, **retain the original system**. *Why:* each has its own logic of load path and joints. Converting 折置 to 京呂 in repair (or vice versa) changes the loads on every member at the wall head (→ Ch. 16).

**R11-011** — *Must.* In both systems, fix the roof beam to the plate or post against **uplift and pull-out**: traditionally with the joint geometry (dovetail, through tenon with pin), today additionally with metal straps (羽子板ボルト *hagoita* bolts, etc.; → Ch. 09, → Ch. 17). *Why:* in earthquakes and typhoons the wall-head joint is where roofs separate from walls.

### 2.4 Sloping beams (登り梁 noboribari)

A **登り梁** is a roof beam that is itself inclined, following the roof slope, from the wall-plate up to a ridge beam or to an interior beam or post. It replaces the horizontal roof beam with its posts.

- **Use:** to gain headroom in an attic storey (町家 *machiya* upper floors, storage lofts), and to express the roof structure in an exposed ceiling (sukiya, modern work).
- **Behaviour:** the beam works in bending (like a big rafter) and pushes outward at its foot unless the ridge is a true support (a beam carried on posts or gable walls) or the feet are tied.
- **Joints:** at the foot, a birdsmouth seat on the plate (or housed into the plate) with a pin or bolt against sliding; at the top, a seat on the ridge beam or tenoned into a post.

**R11-012** — *Must.* A sloping beam must either bear at its upper end on a **structurally supported** ridge beam or post, or have its feet tied against spreading. *Why:* a pair of sloping beams leaning against an unsupported ridge is a three-hinged arch whose thrust pushes the walls out.

### 2.5 The sasu truss of thatched farmhouses (扠首組 sasugumi; 合掌造 gasshō-zukuri)

In the **thatched farmhouse (民家 *minka*)** of most of Japan, the roof above the beam level is not a wagoya but a **pair-rafter truss**:

- **扠首 / 叉首 *sasu*:** pairs of heavy inclined poles (usually unsawn logs or split timbers, 3–6 sun in diameter or more) set at the roof pitch. Their **feet** rest on the ends of the tie-beams (梁 *hari*) or on the wall-plates directly above the tie-beams. Their **tops** cross at the apex.
- **Apex:** the two sasu cross (or one is tenoned through the other). A **ridge pole (棟木)** is laid in the crotch above the crossing, and the joint is lashed with rope (縄) or pinned.
- **Feet:** traditionally the sasu foot is **pointed** and set into a **socket** (a hollow or a mortise) on the beam end, so that it can rotate slightly like a pin but cannot slide. The beam acts as the **tie** of the triangle.
- **Purlins and rafters:** horizontal poles (in some regions called 屋中 *yanaka* or 母屋) are lashed across the sasu. Light rafters of small poles or bamboo (垂木) run down the slope. Horizontal bamboo or split reed laths (小舞 *komai*, えつり) are lashed on, and the thatch is sewn to these.
- **Bracing:** diagonal poles in the roof plane (in Shirakawa-gō usage called ハガイ *hagai*; the term is flagged as regional) brace the roof along its length.
- **合掌造 *gasshō-zukuri*** (Shirakawa-gō, Gokayama): a very steep (about 60°) sasu roof enclosing several storeys of attic floors used for silkworm culture. Its sasu are called 合掌 *gasshō*. It is the best-known example of the system. Descriptions of Shirakawa practice state that the pointed 合掌 feet are set into a socketed member on the beam and that lashings use rope and ネソ *neso* (twisted witch-hazel shoots). The regional names of the parts are flagged. The principle (pointed foot in a socket, lashed apex, no nails) is general to sasu roofs.

**Characteristics.** The sasu truss is **statically efficient**: a triangle with the beam as tie, members in compression, carrying a heavy, steep thatched roof over wide spans with modest timbers. It needs no roof posts and leaves the attic open. Its demands are:
1. the tie (the beam) must be continuous and its joints capable of **tension** between the two sasu feet;
2. the feet must be held in their sockets;
3. the lashings must be maintained, since rope decays and loosens.

**R11-013** — *Must.* Never cut, lower or splice without tension capacity the beam that ties the feet of a sasu pair. *Why:* it is the tie of the truss. Without it the sasu spread, the walls are pushed out and the ridge drops.

**R11-014** — *Should.* Set sasu feet in sockets (pointed foot, or tenon in a mortise) that allow slight rotation but prevent sliding. Do not rigidly fix them with modern plates that impose bending. *Why:* the traditional detail is a pin. A rigid foot connection attracts bending moments that the pole and the beam end are not shaped to carry, and it splits the beam end.

**R11-015** — *Should.* Inspect and renew sasu lashings together with re-thatching (typically every few decades; → Ch. 16). *Why:* the lashings carry the apex and the purlins. Their decay is invisible under the thatch until the roof deforms.

The **sasu** also appears in ancient temple architecture as a visible gable truss (扠首組 in the gable ornament, e.g. in Nara-period buildings), where it is combined with a rainbow beam (虹梁) and a central strut (扠首束 *sasuzuka*) (→ Ch. 12).

### 2.6 King-post and Western trusses (真束小屋組, 洋小屋 yōgoya)

Western timber trusses were introduced in the Meiji period (1868–1912) for government buildings, schools, factories and warehouses. The Japanese name is **洋小屋 *yōgoya*** ("Western roof"):

- **King-post truss (真束小屋組 *shinzuka-koyagumi*):** tie-beam (陸梁 *rokubari*), principal rafters (合掌 *gasshō*, not to be confused with the farmhouse term), king post (真束 *shinzuka*), struts (方杖 *hōzue*), with purlins on the principal rafters. The king post **hangs** from the apex and supports the tie-beam at mid-span against sagging. It does not stand on the tie.
- **Queen-post truss (対束小屋組 *tsuizuka-koyagumi*):** two posts, for longer spans.
- **Joints** are Western in logic but executed with Japanese joinery plus iron: principal rafter to tie-beam with a step (傾ぎ大入れ *katagi-ōire*) and a bolt; king post to tie with a stirrup strap (箱金物 *hako-kanamono*); rafter to king post with a housing and straps.

**Comparison with wagoya:**

| | 和小屋 wagoya | 洋小屋 yōgoya |
|---|---|---|
| Main action | bending in beams, compression in posts | axial forces in a triangulated frame |
| Typical economical span | up to ≈2–2.5 ken (3.6–4.5 m) per beam without interior support (logs allow more) | 6–20 m and more |
| Member sizes | large beams, small posts | modest, uniform members |
| Joints | compression and bending joints (tenons, laps) | tension joints at heel and tie: need bolts, straps |
| Adaptability to plan | high: posts anywhere on beams | low: trusses at regular spacing, uniform plan |
| Attic space | cluttered with posts | cluttered with struts |

**R11-016** — *Must.* In a king-post truss, the king post must hang from the apex, with a gap under its foot or a strap connection that allows the tie to be lifted rather than propped. The heel joints (principal rafter to tie) must be designed for the full horizontal thrust. *Why:* a king post bearing on the tie turns the tie into a loaded beam. An inadequate heel joint lets the truss spread (→ Ch. 17).

### 2.7 Hidden roof and display roof (野小屋 nogoya / 化粧屋根 keshō-yane), and the cantilever (桔木 hanegi)

**The problem.** The early temples of the Asuka and Nara periods had a single roof structure: the rafters visible from below under the eaves (化粧垂木 *keshō-daruki*) were the same rafters that carried the roof tiles. The eave pitch, the roof pitch and the visible geometry were all bound together. A shallow, elegant eave meant a shallow roof (bad for rain on tile). A deep eave meant long, heavy cantilevers on the bracket sets, which sagged.

**The solution: the double roof.** From the mid-Heian period, Japanese carpenters separated the two functions:

- **Display roof (化粧屋根 *keshō-yane*):** the visible eave rafters (化粧垂木), the visible ceiling boards on them (化粧裏板 *keshō-uraita*), and inside the building the ceilings. These are shaped for appearance (low pitch, fine rafters, proportions per the kiwari).
- **Hidden roof (野小屋 *nogoya*, 野屋根 *noyane*):** above the display roof, a separate structure of rough timbers, invisible from below. It carries the real roof covering at a steeper, weather-proof pitch: hidden rafters (野垂木 *no-daruki*), hidden purlins (野母屋 *no-moya*), a hidden ridge (野棟木), roof posts, sheathing (野地 *noji*).
- The space between the two is the **roof void (小屋裏 *koyaura*)**.

The **Great Lecture Hall (大講堂) of Hōryū-ji, rebuilt in 990 (Shōryaku 1)**, is regarded as the oldest surviving building with a hidden roof. According to repair studies, it did not originally have hanegi. The ones it has now were added later.

**The cantilever (桔木 *hanegi*).** The roof void made room for a new structural element: the **桔木**, a large log or squared beam laid in the roof void as a **lever**:

- **Outer arm:** from a fulcrum near the outer wall-line (the column line or the outer plate) outward, under the hidden rafters, to support the eave near its tip (via the eave-end purlin or the 茅負 zone).
- **Fulcrum:** the **桔木枕 *hanegi-makura*** ("hanegi pillow"), a transverse timber on the plates or bracket-set tops at the outer column line on which the hanegi pivots.
- **Inner arm (tail, 桔木尻 *hanegi-jiri*):** extends inward and is **held down**, by the weight of the hidden roof bearing on it and by positive anchorage (a hold-down beam 桔木押さえ over the tail, fixed to the roof beams or posts, or iron straps).

The lever principle: if the eave load at the tip is *P* at distance *a* outside the fulcrum, and the tail is held down at distance *b* inside, the hold-down force is *T* = *P*·*a*/*b*, and the fulcrum carries *P* + *T*. The longer the tail relative to the projection, the smaller the hold-down force. A tail at least about twice the outer arm (*b* ≥ 2*a*) is a representative proportion. **Flag:** actual ratios vary widely with the building. They must be checked, together with the hold-down, for each case.

**History.** Hanegi appear from the **late Heian period** and became general in the medieval period. Early examples carried the hanegi tip on a purlin at the eave end (鼻母屋受け *hanamoya-uke*). Examples cited in the literature include the Shōryōin of Hōryū-ji (1284) and the Ōtataneko-jinja shrine building of the Ōmiwa shrine complex (1285). The structural effect was large. Literature on the Yumedono of Hōryū-ji reports that its eave projection was increased by roughly 40% when hanegi were introduced in a Kamakura-period rebuilding. **Flag:** these figures come from secondary sources and should be confirmed against the repair reports (→ Ch. 01, → Ch. 16).

**Consequences for design.**
1. The visible bracket sets (組物) and display rafters no longer carry most of the eave load. They become partly ornamental, which allowed the refined, lighter wayō eaves of the medieval period (→ Ch. 12).
2. Eave depth grew greatly.
3. The roof pitch and the roof curve are shaped in the hidden roof, independently of the visible eave (§ 9).
4. Hidden-roof members can be rough, round or reused timber (→ Ch. 16).

**R11-017** — *Must.* Every hanegi must have (a) a solid fulcrum (桔木枕) bearing on the column-line structure, not on the display rafters, and (b) a positively anchored tail that can resist the hold-down force without relying on roof weight alone. *Why:* roof weight varies (re-roofing, lighter replacement materials such as copper for tile) and wind uplift reverses it. A tail held only by weight can lift, and the eave then drops onto the bracket sets.

**R11-018** — *Must.* Where a hidden roof exists, the display rafters must not be loaded by the hidden roof except where designed to be. The hidden rafters should bear on their own purlins and on the hanegi, and connections between the two roofs must be deliberate. *Why:* uncontrolled bearing of the hidden roof on display members causes the visible eave to sag and the display rafters to crack. This is a common finding in repairs.

**R11-019** — *Should.* When replacing a heavy roof covering with a lighter one (e.g. tile with copper) on a building with hanegi, re-check the tail anchorage and the counterweight. *Why:* the lever balance was based on the original dead load. A lighter roof reduces the counterweight on the tail and can let the eave drop under snow or lift under wind.

## 3. Rafters (垂木 taruki)

### 3.1 Functions and classes

Rafters carry the roof sheathing (野地) and the covering from purlin to purlin and cantilever beyond the wall-plate to form the eave. They are classified:

- by **visibility:** **化粧垂木 *keshō-daruki*** (display rafters, planed and proportioned, seen under the eave or inside) and **野垂木 *no-daruki*** (hidden rafters, rough, in a hidden roof or above a ceiling);
- by **tier:** in a **single eave (一軒 *hitonoki*)** one tier of rafters runs out to the eave. In a **double eave (二軒 *futanoki*)** the **lower rafters (地垂木 *ji-daruki*)** run from the interior over the plates out to the **lower eave member (木負 *kioi*)**, and the **flying rafters (飛檐垂木 *hien-daruki*)** sit on the 木負 and project further to the upper eave member (茅負 *kayaoi*). The tail (尻 *shiri*) of each flying rafter extends back over the lower rafters and is fixed to them. The 木負 acts as its fulcrum. Nara-period buildings often have **round lower rafters and square flying rafters (地円飛角 *jien-hikaku*)**. Later wayō buildings generally have square rafters in both tiers;
- by **plan arrangement:** **平行垂木 *heikō-daruki*** (parallel; wayō) and **扇垂木 *ōgi-daruki*** (fan rafters; Zen style, and at the corners in the Great Buddha style; → Ch. 05 § 10.4, → Ch. 12);
- by **spacing** (§ 3.2).

### 3.2 Spacing systems

Traditional rafter spacing is measured in **枝 *eda*** ("branches"). One **一枝 *hito-eda*** is the centre-to-centre spacing of rafters, i.e. rafter width + clear gap. In temple and shrine work the whole proportional system of bays and bracket sets is keyed to the rafter spacing (枝割 *edawari*; → Ch. 12). Named spacings:

| Spacing | Japanese | Clear gap between rafters (one common definition) | Use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Full-dense | 本繁 *hon-shige* | equal to the rafter depth (成 *sei*); some texts: equal to the rafter width | temples, shrines, high-status display eaves |
| Half-dense | 半繁 *han-shige* | equal to rafter width + rafter depth | secondary buildings, some shoin |
| Sparse | 疎垂木 *mabara-daruki* (疎割り) | wide; in houses commonly ≈1 shaku to 1.5 shaku centres (≈300–455 mm) | houses, farmhouses, service buildings |
| Paired | 吹寄垂木 *fukiyose-daruki* | rafters grouped in twos (or threes) with a wide gap between groups | sukiya, shoin, tea houses (→ Ch. 13) |

The definitions of 本繁 and 半繁 vary between sources (whether the gap is measured against the rafter's width or its depth). The book gives the reading found in current glossaries and flags the variation.

**R11-020** — *Must.* In temple and shrine work, fix the rafter spacing (一枝) **before** fixing bay widths and bracket-set dimensions, and make every bay an integral number of 枝. *Why:* in the 枝割 system the bays, the bracket-set widths and the corner geometry are all multiples of the rafter spacing. A bay that is not a whole number of 枝 leaves a rafter off-centre over a bracket, which is considered a serious fault (→ Ch. 12).

**R11-021** — *Should.* In houses with sparse rafters, set the rafters at 1.5-shaku (≈455 mm) centres (or 1 shaku, ≈303 mm, under heavy covering or deep eaves), aligned with the post module so that a rafter falls over each post line at the gable and eave. *Why:* 455 mm divides the 910 mm module evenly, allows standard sheathing lengths, and keeps the rafter layout symmetrical on the elevation.

### 3.3 Rafters on the plate: seating and fixing

- **Seating.** In good work the plate top is **bevelled to the rafter slope** and the rafter bears on it full-width at the 峠 (→ Ch. 05 § 5.2). Alternatively the rafter is notched with a small seat (垂木欠き). In temple work the display rafters are often also let slightly into the 茅負 at their tips (茅負の垂木欠き, notches in the 茅負 underside).
- **Nailing.** Traditionally each rafter is nailed down to the plate with a long forged nail (和釘 *wa-kugi*, "rafter nail" 垂木釘; → Ch. 09), driven from the top (脳天打ち *nōten-uchi*) through the rafter into the plate or purlin. The head is later covered by the sheathing. Alternatively it is toe-nailed (斜め打ち) from the sides. Nails at intermediate purlins are usual as well.
- **Modern fixing.** Current Japanese practice (e.g. the Japan Housing Finance Agency's construction specification) calls for a twisted metal strap (ひねり金物 *hineri-kanamono*) or similar connector at each rafter–plate crossing against wind uplift (→ Ch. 17).
- **At the ridge,** rafters from the two sides meet over the ridge beam: butted with plumb cuts, lapped side by side, or halved together (相欠き). They are nailed to the ridge beam. They are paired (opposite each other) unless the spacing system dictates otherwise.

**R11-022** — *Must.* Fix every rafter to the wall-plate against uplift (nail and/or strap). Where rafters are spliced, splice them only over a purlin. *Why:* the eave is the part of the roof most exposed to wind suction. A rafter lifted at the plate starts the progressive stripping of a roof in a typhoon. A splice between supports is a hinge.

**R11-023** — *Should.* Drive rafter nails from the top in traditional work, with nail length at least about 2.5 times the rafter depth (so that more than half the nail is in the plate), and pre-bore hardwood and near the rafter end. *Why:* the top nail acts in withdrawal and needs embedment. Pre-boring avoids splitting the rafter's end, which is the part of the rafter that carries the eave. (Representative; → Ch. 09 for wa-kugi sizes.)

### 3.4 Rafter ends (鼻 hana)

- The rafter tip (垂木鼻 *taruki-bana*) is cut either **plumb** (垂直) or **square to the rafter** (直角), matching the eave member it meets (→ Ch. 05 § 6.7). In houses the ends are usually hidden by a fascia (鼻隠し). In temples and shrines the display rafter ends are exposed.
- **Exposed rafter ends** are finished with care: the end grain is planed or sawn clean, arrises chamfered. In painted buildings the ends are often painted white or yellow ochre, or capped with decorative metal (垂木先金具 *taruki-saki kanagu*) in high-status buildings. The metal also protects the end grain.
- In wayō double eaves, the **flying rafters are commonly tapered** toward their tips in depth and sometimes in width (a refinement that lightens the eave line). **Flag:** the amount varies with period and school.
- Under **curved eaves** the rafter tips follow the eave curve (sweep) and near the corners are lifted and twisted (→ Ch. 05 § 10.3).

**R11-024** — *Must.* Protect exposed rafter ends from end-grain water uptake: with a sound cut surface, paint, or a metal cap, and with the eave covering projecting beyond them. *Why:* rafter ends are the first members of a roof to decay. In repair records, eave rafter tips are among the most frequently replaced parts (→ Ch. 16).

### 4.1 Eave member at the rafter tips — 茅負 (kayaoi)
- **Class:** eave member (軒付け材).
- **Typical use:** laid across the tips of the outermost rafters (the flying rafters in a double eave, the single tier in a single eave) in temples, shrines and high-grade buildings.
- **Load behaviour:** ties the rafter tips together and distributes local loads (snow, a person on the eave) between rafters. It carries the 裏甲 and the roof edge. It is not a primary beam: it spans only between rafters.
- **Proportions:** section roughly trapezoidal. The depth is of the order of the rafter depth to about 1.5 times it, and the width about the same (representative; set by the kiwari, → Ch. 12). The underside is notched to seat the rafter tips.
- **Orientation:** the front face is plumb, or inclined per school. It follows the eave curve (反り) in elevation and plan.
- **Marking:** from the full-size eave curve (→ Ch. 05 § 10.2). Rafter positions come from the 枝割.
- **Assembly:** laid after the rafters, nailed down into each rafter tip. Splices (scarfs) fall over rafters. At the corner the two 茅負 meet on the hip in a mitre (→ Ch. 05 § 6.8).
- **Rules:** R11-025, R11-026.
- **Common errors:** a curve taken from the rafter tips as installed (which carries their errors) instead of from the full-size curve; splices between rafters.

### 4.2 Lower-tier eave member — 木負 (kioi)
The equivalent of the 茅負 at the tips of the lower rafters in a double eave. It carries the flying rafters, which bear on its top, and it acts as their fulcrum. Its curve is related to (but not identical with) that of the 茅負. It is notched on top for the flying rafters.

### 4.3 Eave edge board — 裏甲 (urakō)
- Board(s), commonly about 1 sun (≈30 mm) thick or more, laid **on top of the 茅負** and projecting slightly beyond it. It forms the actual roof edge on which the first course of the covering starts. Its underside is visible from below (hence 裏, "back").
- **切裏甲 *kiri-urakō*:** short boards with the grain running **across** the eave (end grain to the front). **布裏甲 *nuno-urakō*:** long boards with the grain **along** the eave. The choice follows period and building type, and 切裏甲 is associated with tiled roofs.
- It follows the eave curve. In 布裏甲 the boards are bent or cut to the curve. In 切裏甲 the curve is built up piece by piece.

### 4.4 Eave boards of houses — 広小舞 (hirokomai) and 淀 (yodo)
- **広小舞 *hirokomai*:** a board laid on the rafter tips along the eave, on top of the rafters (in houses, where there is no 茅負). It stiffens the rafter tips and carries the first course of the roof. Representative: 0.6–0.8 sun thick × 3–5 sun wide (≈18–24 × 90–150 mm).
- **淀 *yodo*:** a thin strip laid on the 広小舞 (or on the 裏甲) along the outer edge, which raises the first course of tiles or shingles to the correct angle (the "kick" at the eave). On gable verges the corresponding member runs up the slope as **登り淀 *nobori-yodo*** on top of the bargeboard.

### 4.5 Fascia — 鼻隠し (hanakakushi)
- A board nailed across the rafter ends ("nose-hider"), in houses and utilitarian buildings. It is set **plumb** or **square to the rafters**. The rafter ends are cut accordingly (→ Ch. 05 § 6.7–6.8).
- Representative: 0.8–1.2 sun thick × 5–8 sun wide (≈24–36 × 150–240 mm), jointed over rafters, mitred at corners.
- It protects the rafter end grain and carries the gutter hangers in modern work.

### 4.6 Roof boarding and laths — 野地板 (nojiita), 化粧裏板 (keshō-uraita), 木舞/小舞 (komai)
- **野地板 *nojiita*:** rough boards (typically 4–5 bu, ≈12–15 mm, in houses; today often plywood) nailed across the rafters as the base for the roofing.
- **化粧裏板 *keshō-uraita*:** planed boards laid on the display rafters and seen from below between them (in a double roof they are not the weather surface).
- **木舞 / 小舞 *komai* (roof lath):** spaced slats or bamboo battens on the rafters instead of close boarding. They are used under thatch (lashed), and in some traditions under shingles. (Wall laths of the same name are in → Ch. 14.)

**R11-025** — *Must.* Take the curve of every eave member (茅負, 木負, 裏甲, 広小舞 under a curved eave) from the single full-size eave curve. Never take it from the installed rafters. *Why:* → R05-037. Installed rafters carry the accumulated errors. The eave line is the most visible line of the building.

**R11-026** — *Must.* Joint eave members (茅負, 裏甲, 広小舞, fascia) only over a rafter, never between rafters, and stagger the joints of successive layers. *Why:* a joint between rafters is unsupported and opens under snow or foot traffic. Aligned joints in successive layers form a weak line.

### 4.7 Bargeboards and gable ornaments — 破風 (hafu), 懸魚 (gegyo), 螻羽 (keraba)

- **螻羽 *keraba* (verge):** the part of the roof projecting beyond the gable wall. It is carried by the extended purlins, ridge and plates (R11-001). In houses the projection is commonly about 1–2 shaku. In temples and shrines it is proportioned with the eave.
- **破風 *hafu* (bargeboard):** the board, often massive in temples and shrines, that closes the verge along the slope, fixed to the ends of the purlins and ridge. The two boards meet at the apex in a joint called **拝み *ogami*** ("hands joined in prayer"). The bargeboard is usually curved (反り) to follow or exaggerate the roof curve.
  - **切妻破風 / 入母屋破風:** on gable and hip-and-gable roofs.
  - **千鳥破風 *chidori-hafu*:** a small triangular gable set **on** a roof slope, as a dormer-like ornament. It is framed on the rafters of the main roof, with its own small ridge and valleys.
  - **唐破風 *kara-hafu*:** a bargeboard with an undulating curve, convex (起り) in the centre and turning concave (照り) toward the ends. It appears as the **eave form** of a porch (向唐破風 *mukai-karahafu*, a karahafu roof facing forward) or as a **bump in a straight eave** (軒唐破風 *noki-karahafu*). The karahafu requires its own curved rafters and a curved ridge.
- **懸魚 *gegyo*:** an ornamental board hung under the apex (拝み) of the bargeboards, originally protecting the ridge-beam end. Main types: 猪目懸魚 *inome-gegyo* (with heart-shaped cut-outs), 梅鉢懸魚 *umebachi-gegyo*, 蕪懸魚 *kabura-gegyo* ("turnip"), with side fins (鰭 *hire*). Smaller **降懸魚 *kudari-gegyo*** hang under the bargeboards at the purlin ends.

**R11-027** — *Must.* Fix bargeboards to the ends of every purlin, the ridge and the plates they cross, and join the apex (拝み) with a positive joint (tenon, dowel or concealed key) plus the 懸魚 fixing. Do not rely on nails into the roof boarding. *Why:* the bargeboard is exposed to wind on both faces at the most wind-sensitive part of the roof. Loose bargeboards are a common typhoon failure.

**R11-028** — *Should.* Keep the top edge of a bargeboard covered (by the verge tiles, a copper cap, or the 登り淀 and roofing) and ventilate its back. *Why:* its large face and horizontal-ish top edge collect water. Bargeboards and 懸魚 decay from the top edge and the back.

## 5. Eave depth and how deep eaves are carried

### 5.1 Eave projection (軒の出 noki-no-de)

The eave projection is measured horizontally, from the centre of the wall-plate or column line to the outer face of the eave (fascia, or 茅負, or the roof edge). Conventions differ, so always state which. Representative ranges:

| Building | Typical projection | Carried by |
|---|---|---|
| Ordinary house (sparse rafters, fascia) | 2–3 shaku (≈600–900 mm) | rafters cantilevering from the plate |
| House with deep eaves | 3–4 shaku (≈900–1,200 mm) | heavier rafters, or 出桁造 (§ 5.3) |
| Machiya / merchant house front (出桁造) | 4–6 shaku (≈1.2–1.8 m) | cantilever arms (腕木) and an outer plate (出桁) |
| Shrine (small, bark roof) | proportioned to column height and spacing | rafters, simple brackets |
| Temple hall (wayō, with hanegi) | often 2–4 m or more for large halls | bracket sets + hidden roof + hanegi |

**Proportion rule of thumb.** Houses aim for the eave to throw rain clear of the wall base and to shade the openings. A representative ratio of eave projection to eave height (from ground or foundation to eave) is about **0.3**. For example, a 3 m eave height with a 0.9 m eave. **Flag:** this is an approximation, not a traditional canonical rule.

**R11-029** — *Should.* In houses, give an eave projection of at least 2.5–3 shaku (≈750–900 mm) on the weather sides of walls with earthen plaster or exposed timber. *Why:* earthen walls (→ Ch. 14) and exposed timber rely on the eave for protection. Shallow eaves are the principal cause of wall erosion and sill decay in traditional houses.

### 5.2 Rafter cantilevers

For a plain rafter eave (no brackets, no hanegi):
- the rafter cantilevers from the plate (軒桁) and is held down by its **back-span** to the first purlin (and beyond);
- the eave cantilever should not exceed the back-span. Representative: with purlins at 3 shaku, an eave of up to about 3 shaku is carried by a continuous rafter over at least two interior purlin spans;
- deeper eaves need a larger rafter depth (e.g. 1.5 × 2 sun instead of 1.5 × 1.5 sun; § 10) or one of the systems below.

**R11-030** — *Must.* A cantilevered eave rafter must be continuous (unspliced) from its tip over the wall-plate and at least the first interior purlin. Its tail must be nailed down at the purlin. *Why:* the cantilever moment is maximum at the plate and is balanced only by the back-span. A splice or a loose tail turns the eave into a hinge.

### 5.3 Outrigger-plate construction (出桁造 degeta-zukuri) and cantilever arms (腕木 udegi)

- **腕木 *udegi*** (cantilever arms) pass **through** the wall posts (or are tenoned into them and wedged) and project outward. Their tips carry an **outer plate (出桁 *degeta*)**, on which the rafters rest near the eave. The rafter cantilever beyond the 出桁 is then short.
- The arm is a lever: the outer load is balanced by the post (through the tenon, with wedges or pins, → Ch. 09) and often by a back-span inside, tied to the structure.
- Common in Edo-period merchant houses (町家, 商家) and in temple subsidiary buildings. It allows 4–6 shaku eaves with slender rafters, and a crisp, exposed eave.
- A **持送り *mochiokuri*** (bracket or corbel) under the arm, fixed to the post, supports the arm at its root and adds a decorative element.

**R11-031** — *Must.* A cantilever arm (腕木) passing through a post must be wedged or pinned tight in the post mortise, with its shoulder bearing on the outer face. Where it does not continue inside as a back-span, the post and the wall frame must be designed for the resulting moment. *Why:* a loose through-tenon lets the arm rotate and the outer plate drop. The arm's moment is transferred to the post as a couple at the mortise.

### 5.4 Cantilevered beams (出し梁 dashibari)

The upper-floor beams (or roof beams) are extended beyond the wall line to carry the outer plate or an overhanging upper wall (出し梁造 *dashibari-zukuri*). This is robust because the beam's own back-span balances the cantilever. It appears in snow regions and in machiya with projecting upper storeys. **Flag:** regional naming varies.

### 5.5 Temple eaves: bracket sets, tail rafters and hanegi

In temples the eave is carried by a combination of:
1. **bracket sets (組物 *kumimono*)** on the columns, which step the support outward (→ Ch. 12);
2. **tail rafters (尾垂木 *odaruki*)** in the bracket sets: inclined levers whose outer ends carry the eave purlin (丸桁 *gangyō*) and whose tails are held down inside;
3. **hanegi** in the hidden roof (§ 2.7), which carry most of the eave load in medieval and later buildings.

**R11-032** — *Must.* In temple eaves, identify which elements actually carry the eave (bracket sets, tail rafters, hanegi) and design or repair each for its real share of the load. *Why:* in buildings with hanegi the bracket sets may carry little load. In buildings without them the bracket sets carry everything. Treating ornamental members as structural (or the reverse) misdirects repair.

### 5.6 Heavy rafters (力垂木 chikara-daruki)

The term 力垂木 ("strength rafter") is used for heavier rafters inserted into a run of ordinary rafters (at intervals, at the ends of a run, at corners or under concentrated loads) to stiffen the eave or carry loads the ordinary rafters cannot. **Flag:** the term's exact use varies between regions and between house and temple work. Treat it as a description of function rather than a fixed member type.

**R11-033** — *Should.* Where an eave carries concentrated loads (a gutter outlet, a snow-guard, the corner of a verge, an access point), provide a heavier rafter or a double rafter at that point instead of relying on the common rafters. *Why:* the common rafter is sized for distributed load. Local overloads crack rafters at the plate.
