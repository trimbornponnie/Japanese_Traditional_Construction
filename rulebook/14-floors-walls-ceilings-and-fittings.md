# Chapter 14 — Floors, Walls, Ceilings and Fittings (造作・建具 / zōsaku & tategu)

**Scope.** This chapter covers everything that is installed *inside and onto* the finished structural frame: the finish carpentry (造作, *zōsaku*) of floors, walls, ceilings, the tokonoma and the opening frames, and the movable fittings (建具, *tategu*) — shōji, fusuma, board doors, storm shutters, ranma — together with the lattice craft of kumiko. It assumes the frame of → Ch. 10 is standing, roofed and dried in (→ Ch. 15), and it borrows its joints from → Ch. 07 (splices), → Ch. 08 (connections) and its pins and wedges from → Ch. 09. Design-level rules for *where* a tokonoma goes, which room is formal, room heights and sukiya aesthetics belong to → Ch. 13; this chapter says *how* those elements are built, fitted and fixed. Floor *framing* (ōbiki, neda, yukazuka) is in → Ch. 10; here we start at the subfloor.

Dimensions in this chapter are representative of Kyoto/Kantō urban practice of the late Edo to Shōwa periods, which is the body of practice most carpenters and joiners (建具屋 *tateguya*) still work from. Individual workshops vary, sometimes by several bu, and every figure given should be read as "a common value", not a legal standard.

## 1. Zōsaku principles (造作の原則)

### 1.1 What zōsaku is
The Japanese building trade distinguishes **frame work** (軸組, *jikugumi*; 構造材 *kōzōzai*) from **finish carpentry** (造作, *zōsaku*; 造作材 *zōsakuzai*). Zōsaku members are those that are seen and touched but that carry little or no primary structural load: sills and lintels of openings (敷居 *shikii*, 鴨居 *kamoi*), tie-rails (長押 *nageshi*), ceiling rims and battens (回り縁 *mawaribuchi*, 竿縁 *saobuchi*), floor boards, skirting (畳寄せ *tatamiyose*, 幅木 *habaki*), window frames (窓枠 *madowaku*), tokonoma members and shelving. The fittings (建具 *tategu*) are made by a separate trade, the 建具屋 (*tateguya*), but their tolerances are dictated by the carpenter's zōsaku, so the two are treated together.

Three characteristics distinguish zōsaku from frame work:
1. **Appearance governs.** Zōsaku is made from the best, most straight-grained, knot-free material (無節 *mubushi*, 上小節 *jōkobushi*; → Ch. 03), planed to a finish that is not further coated in the best work (白木 *shiraki*).
2. **Movement governs.** Zōsaku is thin relative to its length and is fixed to a frame that is still drying, shrinking and settling. Every zōsaku joint must permit movement or hide it.
3. **Fixing is hidden.** In formal work no nail head, screw or glue line should be visible on a finished face.

### 1.2 Installation sequence
The general order is dictated by two principles: *wet trades before fine wood*, and *members that define a plane before the members that stop against it*.

| Stage | Work | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | Frame standing, roof covered, 荒壁 or sheathing | Building must be dried in (雨仕舞 *amajimai*). → Ch. 15 |
| 1 | Uchinori members (shikii, kamoi, mume, window frames) set where earthen walls will meet them | They form the "stops" for the plaster; in komai walls they are fixed *before* the lathing is complete. |
| 2 | Lathing (竹小舞) and 荒壁 on remaining walls; drying | Months (see §3). |
| 3 | Floor boards and 荒床 subfloor; 畳寄せ; 縁 (engawa) | Often after arakabe so that clay does not stain finish boards; boards protected (養生 *yōjō*). |
| 4 | Ceiling: mawaribuchi, nobuchi, saobuchi, ceiling boards | Ceiling before final plaster so the plaster meets the mawaribuchi. |
| 5 | 中塗り nakanuri | |
| 6 | Nageshi, tokonoma members (otoshigake, tokogamachi), shelving | Fine members fitted to the now-stable wall line. |
| 7 | 上塗り uwanuri | Last wet trade; fine members protected with paper/tape. |
| 8 | Tategu fitted (建付け *tatetsuke*) | After all wet work so that grooves have stabilised. |
| 9 | Tatami laid | Last; tatami are made to the as-built room. |

Regional and shop variations exist: in some Kansai practice the nageshi is fitted before nakanuri; in dry-walled (大壁 or board) interiors stages 2, 5 and 7 disappear.

**R14-001** — Zōsaku **must not** begin in a room until the building is roofed and the room can be kept dry. *Why:* finish members exposed to rain swell, stain (アク *aku* — tannin marks on sugi and hinoki) and later shrink away from their joints.

**R14-002** — Wet trades **should** precede fine carpentry in any given plane, and the finest members (tokonoma timbers, nageshi, tategu) **should** be installed last and protected. *Why:* plaster and clay stain white wood irreparably; water from wet trades raises grain.

**R14-003** — The carpenter **should** leave the frame to stand, roofed, as long as the schedule allows (traditionally at least one summer) before fitting uchinori members and tategu. *Why:* green posts shrink, rotate and settle in their first season; grooves cut into a moving frame bind or gape.

### 1.3 Movement allowance
Wood shrinks roughly 0.1–0.3 % along the grain, 3–5 % radially and 5–10 % tangentially from green to dry (species-dependent; → Ch. 03). Zōsaku design therefore:
- **places long members so their length runs with the grain** (always true), so length change is negligible;
- **allows width change** by letting boards float in grooves (小穴 *koana*), by overlapping (羽重ね *hagasane*, 相決り *aijakuri*) or tonguing (本実 *hon-zane*), and by never gluing a wide board across its width to a cross-grain member;
- **hides the gap that will open** where a member meets a post or wall: the joint is made *into* the post (大入れ *ōire* housing) so that shrinkage opens a shadow line inside the housing rather than a visible gap.

**R14-004** — Any finish member meeting a post **should** be housed into it (大入れ, typically 1–3 bu ≈ 3–9 mm deep) rather than butted against it. *Why:* when the member or the post shrinks, the end stays inside the housing and no daylight gap appears. (Butt joints are acceptable only where a moulding or plaster covers the line.)

**R14-005** — Boards wider than about 4 sun (≈120 mm) **must not** be fixed rigidly across their full width (e.g. nailed at both edges and glued); they **must** be allowed to move in grooves, under battens or via laps. *Why:* restraint across the grain causes splits or buckling.

### 1.4 Hidden fixing
The finish surfaces of formal zōsaku carry no visible fastener. The main methods are:

| Method | Japanese | How it works | Typical use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hidden nail under a lifted sliver | 隠し釘 *kakushi-kugi* (also 起こし釘) | A thin sliver of the surface is lifted with a narrow chisel, left attached at one end; the nail is driven and set in the hollow; the sliver is glued back. | Occasional fixing of a finish member that cannot be fixed from behind. |
| Blind (secret) nailing through the tongue | 隠し釘打ち / 実の上から斜め打ち | Nail driven obliquely through the tongue shoulder of a tongued board; next board's groove covers it. | Board floors, hameita. |
| Nailing from behind or from the hidden face | 裏から釘 | Nail or screw from the back, through a batten or through the frame. | Ceiling boards (稲子 *inago*), tokoita. |
| Loose tongue / spline | 雇い実 *yatoi-zane* | Separate strip in grooves in both pieces; carries alignment, nail driven through spline only. | Floor boards, panel edge joints, tokobashira–tokogamachi alignment. |
| Joint line in a reveal | 目地 *meji* | A deliberate recessed line (目透し *mesukashi*) that makes the joint read as intentional and absorbs movement. | 目透し天井, board walls. |
| Covered head | 釘隠し *kugikakushi* | A metal or wooden ornament that covers a nail head. | Nageshi at posts. |
| Hidden wedge / draw joint | 隠し蟻, 引き独鈷 *hiki-dokko* | Dovetail or bow-tie key concealed inside the joint; pulls parts together. | Tokogamachi, otoshigake, counter tops (see §5; → Ch. 09). |
| Plugged screw/nail | 埋木 *umeki* | Head counterbored and covered with a grain-matched plug. | Repairs, secondary work. |

**R14-006** — On the show face of formal zōsaku (tokonoma, shoin, nageshi, uchinori members in a zashiki) a fastener head **must not** be visible; it is either hidden in the joint, covered by the next member, fixed from behind, or plugged. *Why:* visual purity is the purpose of zōsaku; exposed nails also stain (iron–tannin reaction) on sugi and hinoki.

**R14-007** — Where iron nails must be used near finish surfaces of oak, chestnut, keyaki or sugi, they **should** be set below the surface and covered, or non-ferrous/traditional wa-kugi used (→ Ch. 09). *Why:* iron reacts with tannin to leave black stains that spread with moisture.

### 1.5 Fitting to irregular members — 光付け (*hikari-tsuke*)
Zōsaku members regularly meet posts that are not flat: natural-finish posts (面皮柱 *menkawa-bashira* — posts with the bark-edge left on the arrises), log tokobashira, bent 床柱 in sukiya work, and hand-hewn old posts. The method of scribing a member to such a surface is **hikari-tsuke** (光付け; the same word is used for scribing posts onto foundation stones, → Ch. 10).

Procedure (for, e.g., a tokogamachi meeting a menkawa post):
1. Set the member in its final position, held a known distance (e.g. 1–2 sun) short of the post, level and plumb.
2. Using a 光板 (*hikari-ita*, a thin scribing board) or a divider/compass (ぶんまわし), follow the post profile with one leg and transfer it with the other onto the end of the member.
3. Cut to the line, undercutting slightly (逃げ *nige*, a back-bevel of a few degrees) so that only the visible edge meets the post.
4. Test, blacken high spots (墨・チョーク), pare, and repeat until the line is continuous with no light visible — hence "light-fitting".
5. Add the housing or tenon *inside* the scribed outline so that the scribed edge covers any gap.

**R14-008** — A finish member meeting a natural-surface or irregular post **must** be scribed (光付け) to it, and the back of the scribed end **should** be undercut (逃げ) so that only the show edge bears. *Why:* a full-thickness contact is impossible to make tight on an irregular surface; undercutting leaves only a visible line to fit.

**R14-009** — On menkawa and log posts, zōsaku lines (nageshi, kamoi, mawaribuchi) **should** be set by level and plumb from the building reference (陸墨 *rokuzumi*, 心墨), never by eye from the post surface. *Why:* the post surface is irregular; lines derived from it wander.

## 2. Floors (床 *yuka*)

Floor framing — 大引 *ōbiki*, 床束 *yukazuka*, 根太 *neda*, 根太掛け *nedagake* — is in → Ch. 10 §Floor framing. This section begins at the subfloor.

### 2.1 Tatami floors (畳敷き *tatamijiki*)

#### Subfloor — 荒床 (*arayuka*)
Under tatami the floor is a rough board deck (荒床, also 下地板 *shitajiita*), traditionally of sugi or matsu boards, c. 4–5 bu (12–15 mm) thick, laid on neda at c. 1 shaku 5 sun (≈450 mm) centres, or on neda at 1 shaku for thinner boards. Boards are laid across the neda with small gaps (a few mm) for ventilation; tatami need to breathe from below, and in traditional houses the 荒床 is often unfixed in part so that boards can be lifted for underfloor inspection or for storing items (床下収納).

**R14-010** — The 荒床 surface **must** be set so that the finished tatami top is flush with the top of the tatamiyose and with the adjoining shikii top (or with the designed step). The subfloor level = shikii top − tatami thickness (traditionally c. 1 sun 8 bu ≈ 55–60 mm for a rice-straw 本畳; thinner, c. 1 sun 5 bu ≈ 45 mm or less, for modern insulation-board cores). *Why:* tatami are made to fill the room exactly and must finish flush with thresholds; the carpenter sets the subfloor for the tatami, not vice versa.

**R14-011** — The 荒床 **should** be laid with small open joints and, in humid regions, left unsealed. *Why:* the straw tatami core (藁床 *waradoko*) absorbs and releases moisture through the subfloor; sealing the underside promotes mould.

#### Tatamiyose — 畳寄せ
Where the tatami meet a shinkabe wall (a wall between posts), there is a gap between the tatami edge and the wall surface because the wall is recessed from the post face by the *chiri* (§3). This is filled by a thin finish strip, the **tatamiyose** (畳寄せ), set flush with the post face and with its top flush with the tatami surface. It is the floor equivalent of the mawaribuchi.
- Section: typically 1 sun 5 bu–2 sun high × a width equal to the chiri depth plus a housing (e.g. 5–8 bu).
- Housed (大入れ) into the posts at each end; the wall plaster is finished against its top edge.
- Material: matches the posts (hinoki, sugi); in sukiya work may be thin sugi or bamboo.

**R14-012** — The tatamiyose's face **must** be flush with the post faces, and it **must** be installed before the final plaster so the plaster is finished down onto it. *Why:* it defines the lower plaster stop; a tatamiyose fitted after plastering leaves a ragged joint.

#### Tatami sizes and room modules
The tatami module is treated in → Ch. 02 (京間 *kyōma* 6.3 × 3.15 shaku; 中京間 *chūkyōma* 6.0 × 3.0; 江戸間/田舎間 *edoma/inakama* 5.8 × 2.9 shaku, etc.). The key point for zōsaku is that in **kyōma (畳割 *tatami-wari*)** planning, the tatami size is fixed and the column grid adjusts, so tatami are interchangeable between rooms; in **inakama (柱割 *hashira-wari*)** planning, the column centre-lines are fixed and each room's tatami are made to measure. In both cases the traditional tatami maker (畳屋 *tatamiya*) measures the finished room (畳割り採寸) and makes each mat to fit its place; mats are marked with their position and **must** be returned to the same place after cleaning or re-covering.

**R14-013** — Tatami **must** be measured and made after all zōsaku of the room is complete, and each mat's position **should** be marked on its underside. *Why:* rooms are never perfectly square; a mat made for one position does not fit another.

#### Tatami layout rules (畳の敷き方)
The following layout rules are customary throughout Japan. They are social and ritual rules as much as technical ones, but a craftsman who ignores them is considered to have done wrong work.

1. **No four corners at a point (四つ目 / 十字目 avoidance).** In the ordinary, "auspicious" layout, **shūgi-jiki** (祝儀敷き), the joints of the mats never form a cross (+); at most three mat corners meet at a point, forming a T. The alternative, **bushūgi-jiki** (不祝儀敷き), in which mats are laid in a grid so that four corners meet, was used for funerals and memorial occasions (and in some temple halls and large gathering rooms). Houses were sometimes re-laid in bushūgi-jiki for a funeral and restored afterwards.
2. **Toko-zashi (床差し / 床刺し) avoidance.** In a room with a tokonoma, the mat in front of the tokonoma **should** lie with its long side parallel to the tokonoma front, so that no mat edge runs toward (points at) the tokonoma. Commonly cited reason: a mat edge pointing at the tokonoma would place the edge-binding (縁 *heri*) under the seat of honour (上座 *kamiza*) and "pierce" the tokonoma. (The same taboo governs ceiling battens, §4.)
3. **Half-mat placement.** In a 4½-mat room the half mat is placed in the centre (a pinwheel, 卍 layout around it); some sources also say the half mat should not be laid toward the 鬼門 (north-east), a geomantic custom that is regional and not universal. In tea rooms the placement is instead governed by the hearth (炉 *ro*) and the host/guest mats (→ Ch. 13).
4. **Start from the tokonoma.** Mats are laid beginning with the mat in front of the tokonoma (床前畳 *tokomae-datami*), then around.
5. **Grain of the mat (畳の目).** The weave of the rush cover (畳表 *tatami-omote*) runs along the long side of the mat. The mat edges along the long side carry the heri; the short ends are unbound (框 *kamachi* side). In the entrance-facing orientation, mats in the principal line of approach **should** have their weave running in the direction of walking where the layout permits, since the rush surface is smoother along the weave and wears less; this is a secondary preference, subordinate to rules 1–2.

```
 Shūgi-jiki, 8 mats (auspicious)          Bushūgi-jiki, 8 mats (grid)
 +-------+---+---+-------+                 +---+---+---+---+
 |       |   |   |       |                 |   |   |   |   |
 +-------+   |   +-------+                 |   |   |   |   |
 |       |   |   |       |                 +---+---+---+---+
 +---+---+---+---+---+---+                 |   |   |   |   |
 |   |               |   |                 |   |   |   |   |
 |   +---------------+   |                 +---+---+---+---+
 (schematic only; no + junctions)          (four-corner + junctions)
```

**R14-014** — Tatami in a dwelling **should** be laid in shūgi-jiki: no point where four mat corners meet. *Why:* customary; bushūgi-jiki is associated with funerals. (Variant: temple halls and large ceremonial rooms may use grid layouts.)

**R14-015** — The mat in front of a tokonoma **should** lie parallel to the tokonoma front (no toko-zashi). *Why:* customary respect for the tokonoma and the seat of honour.

**R14-016** — Tatami laying **should** begin at the tokonoma and proceed toward the entrance, and the last mat fitted **should** be one that can be lifted easily (usually a mat near the entrance). *Why:* errors accumulate toward the last mat; a mat far from the tokonoma is less conspicuous if slightly forced.

#### Tatami against the tokonoma (畳床 in the tokonoma)
In a formal tokonoma the floor of the alcove itself may be a tatami (畳床 *tatami-doko*, a mat made to the alcove, sometimes with 薄縁 *usuberi* — a thin bound mat) or a board (板床 *itadoko*, with a 床板 *tokoita*); see §5.

### 2.2 Board floors (板の間 *ita-no-ma*, 板張り *itabari*)

#### Materials
- **縁甲板 (*enkōita*)** — the traditional machined floor board for corridors and board rooms: narrow (3–4 sun ≈ 90–120 mm), 5–6 bu (15–18 mm) thick, tongued and grooved, in hinoki, sugi, matsu (akamatsu), tsuga, or for hard-wearing floors keyaki, nara or kuri. The name originally denoted engawa boards; it now refers to the narrow T&G board.
- **Wide boards (幅広板 / 一枚板)** — in old farmhouses (土間 edges, 板の間 of minka) floors of wide (8 sun–1 shaku+) matsu, keyaki or kuri boards, 1 sun or more thick, sometimes adzed (名栗 *naguri*), were laid on stout neda or directly on ōbiki with loose splines or plain edges.

#### Edge joints
| Joint | Japanese | Description | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tongue and groove | 本実 *hon-zane* | Tongue ≈ 1/3 of board thickness, length ≈ 1/3–1/2 thickness | Standard for enkōita; secret nailing through tongue shoulder. |
| Loose tongue / spline | 雇い実 *yatoi-zane* | Both edges grooved; separate spline (often hardwood or cross-grain-free straight stock) | Used for wide boards and where boards may be reversed or replaced. |
| Half-lap / shiplap | 相決り *aijakuri* | Each edge rebated to half thickness | Simpler; allows movement; used for sub-floors, board walls, rustic floors. |
| Butt (plain edge) | 突付け *tsukitsuke*, いも矧ぎ | Square edges | Subfloors, ventilated floors. |
| Glued edge (edge joint of wide panels) | 矧ぎ *hagi* | Used to make wide single panels (e.g. tokoita), *not* for floor fields | See §9. |

#### Board direction rules
- **Across the neda.** Boards must span across the joists (neda). Therefore the *neda* direction is decided (→ Ch. 10) with the desired board direction in mind.
- **Along the long direction of a corridor or engawa.** In corridors (廊下 *rōka*) and in kure-en engawa, boards run along the length of the corridor, so the joint lines lead the eye along the passage and the boards are cut from long stock with few end joints.
- **In rooms**, customary practice is to run boards along the long direction of the room or toward the principal light source (garden side), so that the edge joints, lit along their length, cast minimal shadow. This is a customary preference, not a fixed rule; practice varies.
- **End joints** (継ぎ *tsugi*) of boards are staggered (乱継ぎ *ran-tsugi*) and should fall over a neda; in the best work, boards are full-length (通し *tōshi*) in corridors.

#### 木表 up (*kiomote* up)
The face of a flat-sawn board that was toward the bark is **木表** (*kiomote*); the pith side is **木裏** (*kiura*). The customary rule for floor boards (enkōita, engawa boards) is **木表を上** — bark side up.

Reasons, as generally given:
1. On the kiura face the latewood of the rings is exposed at a shallow angle and tends to lift in slivers (目ぼれ *mebore*, ささくれ *sasakure*) as the surface wears and dries — a hazard to bare feet and to tabi and tatami edges. The kiomote face wears more evenly and polishes (木表は艶が出る).
2. The kiomote face has the more attractive figure (杢, straight 柾 lines) in most cuts.

A qualification: flat-sawn boards cup *toward* the bark side as they dry (the kiomote becomes concave), so a board laid kiomote-up tends to raise its edges. In practice this is controlled by using narrow, dry boards (enkōita are narrow for this reason), by tongue-and-groove edges which lock neighbours, and by fixing along both edges through the tongue. For wide boards that cannot be restrained, some carpenters reverse the rule; present practice in Japan overwhelmingly follows kiomote-up for floors. (Quarter-sawn 柾目 *masame* boards have no meaningful kiomote/kiura and cup very little; they are preferred for the best corridors.)

**R14-017** — Floor boards **should** be laid kiomote (bark side) up. *Why:* the kiura face slivers with wear; kiomote wears smooth. Cupping is controlled by narrow board width, dry stock and T&G edges.

**R14-018** — Board floors **must** run across the neda, and **should** run along the length of corridors and engawa. End joints **must** fall on a neda and **should** be staggered by at least one neda space between adjacent boards. *Why:* structural span; appearance; end joints aligned in a line create a weak, visually disturbing hinge line.

**R14-019** — Enkōita **should** be secret-nailed through the tongue shoulder at each neda (斜め打ち, c. 45°), with nail heads set so that the next board's groove closes over them. *Why:* hidden fixing (R14-006) and a floor that can be tightened (the next board is driven against the tongue with a 当て木 *ategi* block).

**R14-020** — Board floors **should** be laid with a small allowance (c. 1–3 mm per board in humid-season laying, less in dry) or with boards at equilibrium moisture content, and **must not** be driven so tight at walls that they cannot expand. The perimeter gap is hidden under the tatamiyose/habaki or inside the post housing. *Why:* seasonal swelling of a tightly laid floor causes arching (突き上げ).

### 2.3 Engawa (縁側)
The **engawa** is the board-floored strip along the garden side of a house, outside the paper-screened rooms and inside (or outside) the storm shutters. Its structure: the outer edge is a heavy finish beam, the **縁框** (*enkamachi*), carried on **縁束** (*enzuka*, short posts) on stones or on a 縁桁 (*engeta*); the inner edge rests on the room's shikii line (縁側敷居) or on a nedagake along the posts.

#### Kirime-en vs kure-en — definitions
| Type | Japanese | Board direction | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Kirime-en** | 切目縁 (切れ目縁) | Boards run **perpendicular** to the building line (from the house out to the enkamachi); board end grain (木口 *koguchi*) is visible at the outer edge | Older, formal form; standard on shrine and temple verandas (縁 around halls) and on nure-en. Needs thick boards spanning between the building-side beam and the outer 縁框/縁葛. |
| **Kure-en** | 榑縁 (くれ縁) | Boards run **parallel** to the building line (along the length of the veranda), supported on joists running out from the building | Characteristic of most Edo-period and later houses; thinner boards; long boards emphasise the length of the veranda. |

In kirime-en the boards are thick (1 sun–1 sun 5 bu and more in temple work) because they span between the building sill/縁葛 and the outer rim; ends are exposed and often protected by an end cap or simply chamfered. In temple architecture the outer rim member is the 縁葛 (*engazura*) and the boards may be jointed to it with a housing and 雇い実.

In kure-en the boards are typically 5 bu–8 bu thick, 4–6 sun wide, laid on 縁根太 (*en-neda*) spanning from the house sill to the enkamachi. Boards are often slightly wider than enkōita and may be jointed with 雇い実 or 相決り; the best are full-length hinoki or matsu.

**R14-021** — Kirime-en boards **should** be laid heart side (木裏) down / kiomote up and their exposed end grain **should** be protected (chamfered and set back under the eave, or capped). *Why:* end grain wicks water; exposed kirime-en board ends are the first place of rot on verandas.

**R14-022** — The enkamachi **should** be a single length of durable timber (hinoki, kuri, keyaki, or matsu in minka) wherever possible, with any splice made over an enzuka using a concealed splice (e.g. 金輪 or 追掛け, → Ch. 07). *Why:* it is the most visible and most weather-exposed finish member of the house.

**R14-023** — The engawa floor **should** fall very slightly (a few mm across its depth) toward the garden where it is outside the amado line. *Why:* driven rain drains outward, not into the house.

#### Nure-en (濡れ縁)
A **nure-en** ("wet veranda") lies outside the storm-shutter line and receives rain. It is built as a separate, lower platform (often a step down, 落ち縁 *ochi-en*) of durable timber (kuri, hinoki, sawara, or bamboo), with open joints (簀子 *sunoko*, gaps of 3–10 mm) so that water drains between boards. Boards typically run perpendicular to the house (a kirime form), which sheds water off the ends. Nure-en are expected to be replaced periodically (→ Ch. 16) and are made detachable from the main frame, often free-standing on their own stones.

**R14-024** — A nure-en **must** have open joints and **should** be structurally independent of (or easily detachable from) the house frame. *Why:* it will rot first and must be replaceable without disturbing the house; open joints drain water.

### 2.4 Shikii (敷居) — the floor-level track member
The shikii is treated with the uchinori in §6 because it is designed as a pair with the kamoi. Floor-specific points:
- In a tatami room, the shikii top is the datum for the floor: tatami finish flush with it (R14-010).
- Between a tatami room and an engawa or board corridor, the shikii is usually flush on both sides (同面 *dōzura*) or, in older houses, the engawa is one step lower.
- Shikii carrying heavy traffic or heavy doors (amado, 大戸) receive **umegashi** (埋め樫): strips of hard oak (樫 *kashi*, white or red evergreen oak) inlaid into the bottom of the groove, or a hard-wood insert (e.g. 桜) forming the running surface. In modern work, plastic or bamboo tapes (敷居すべり *shikii-suberi*) do the same job.
- The shikii is supported continuously: by a 敷居下 (*shikii-shita*) packing, by a 根太掛け or 足固め (→ Ch. 10), or, where it spans an open floor, by 敷居束 (small studs).

**R14-025** — A shikii **must** be supported continuously or at close centres (≤ c. 3 shaku) on the floor frame, never allowed to span freely. *Why:* a shikii that deflects binds the sliding fittings and opens gaps under them.

**R14-026** — Shikii carrying heavy doors or heavy traffic (entrance, amado track) **should** be fitted with umegashi hard-wood inserts or be made entirely of hard wood (樫, 桜, 欅). *Why:* the running surface wears; a worn groove lowers the door and it scrapes the kamoi groove bottom out of contact (and falls out).

### 2.5 Agarikamachi (上がり框) and shikidai (式台)
The **agarikamachi** is the heavy finish member at the edge of the raised floor at the entrance (玄関 *genkan*), where one steps up from the earthen floor (土間 *doma*). It is often the most prestigious single timber in a house: keyaki, kuri, or in sukiya taste a lacquered or natural-edged piece.

- **Proportions:** typically 4–6 sun (120–180 mm) high, 3–5 sun deep; in minka the doma edge member (上がり框 or 縁框 at the 土間 side) may be much larger.
- **Joints:** housed and tenoned into the posts at each end (大入れ + 短枘, sometimes 蟻掛け from below); the floor boards or 荒床 are carried on a rebate or a nedagake behind it; it is supported at mid-span on 束 from the ground or on a stone.
- **Show face:** planed, sometimes with a slight chamfer (面取り) on the top edge; the top face carries the most wear.

The **shikidai** (式台) is a lower board step between the doma and the agarikamachi in a formal entrance (originally in samurai residences, the place where visitors were formally received). It is a wide board (often 1 shaku–1 shaku 5 sun deep) in keyaki or hinoki, carried on its own frame, set at about half the rise.

**R14-027** — The agarikamachi **should** be fitted between posts with housings on both ends and supported at mid-span; its top face **must** be level and its riser face plumb, with the grain running along its length and the more wear-resistant face (usually kiomote) up. *Why:* it carries concentrated foot traffic and impact from footwear; unsupported spans sag and rock.

**R14-028** — In a formal entrance, the rise from doma to the floor **should** be divided so that no step exceeds a comfortable height (commonly c. 5–8 sun ≈ 150–240 mm per step) by adding a shikidai or 踏み石 *fumiishi* (stepping stone). *Why:* the traditional floor height (often 1 shaku 5 sun and more above the doma) is too high for a single step.

## 3. Walls (壁 *kabe*)

### 3.1 Shinkabe and ōkabe
| | **Shinkabe** 真壁 | **Ōkabe** 大壁 |
|---|---|---|
| Definition | Wall set *between* posts; posts and horizontal members exposed on the face | Wall finish covers posts and beams on at least one face |
| Typical use | Interiors of all traditional houses; exterior of minka and machiya where eaves protect; shrines, temples, tea houses | Storehouses (土蔵 *dozō*), fireproof machiya (塗屋 *nuriya*, 蔵造り *kurazukuri*), castle walls, most modern timber houses |
| Frame effect | Posts visible, can dry; nuki and posts inspectable | Posts hidden; fire protected; moisture trapped if not ventilated |
| Chiri | Present (wall recessed from post face) | None (or wall flush over frame) |
| Joinery implication | All zōsaku housed into posts; chirijakuri; frame must be finish grade | Frame can be rough; zōsaku fixed to a furring layer |

A building commonly combines both: ōkabe outside (for fire and weather) and shinkabe inside (片真壁 *kata-shinkabe*).

**R14-029** — In shinkabe construction all posts, nuki-concealing surfaces and exposed members **must** be treated as finish work from the moment of kizami: protected during tatemae and plastering, marked only on faces that will be hidden or planed. *Why:* the frame *is* the interior finish; stains, dents and pencil marks remain visible.

### 3.2 Earthen walls (土壁 *tsuchikabe*) — sequence

The traditional earthen wall is a multi-layer clay plaster applied onto a woven bamboo lath (竹小舞 *takekomai*) tied into the frame between the nuki. It is the work of the plasterer (左官 *sakan*), but the carpenter provides and prepares the frame and lath support. The sequence below is representative of Kyoto-area practice; names and number of coats vary regionally.

```
 Section through a shinkabe tsuchikabe (schematic)

   post        wall                           post
  |    |<-chiri->|  uwanuri  (finish)  ~1-3 mm
  |    |         |  nakanuri (middle)  ~5-10 mm
  |    |      ===|  arakabe  (rough, both sides of lath)
  |    |   ||||  |  komai lath around nuki
  |    |      ===|  uragaeshi (back coat)
  |    |         |
  |    |  chirijakuri groove in post to receive plaster edge
```

#### Step 1 — Frame preparation
- **Nuki** (貫) pass through the posts at regular heights (→ Ch. 10); they are the primary horizontal support for the lath.
- The carpenter bores **holes or slots** (間渡し穴 *mawatashi-ana*) in the posts, in the underside of the upper beam/keta or head member and in the top of the dodai/足固め/shikii for the ends of the main lath bamboos (see below). Alternatively, a small groove or the notch between nuki and post is used.
- **Chirijakuri** (散りじゃくり / ちり決り) — a small groove, c. 2–3 mm wide × 2–5 mm deep (representative; varies by plasterer), cut along the post face at the chiri line, into which the edge of the plaster is pressed. As the post shrinks away from the wall, the plaster edge remains hidden in the groove rather than opening a visible crack (散り切れ *chiri-gire*).

#### Step 2 — Mawatashi-dake (間渡し竹)
Main lath members: whole or halved **madake** or **hachiku** bamboo of c. 15–25 mm diameter (or split), set both vertically and horizontally across the bay with their ends inserted into the pre-bored holes in the frame members. Representative spacing: c. 1 shaku (≈300 mm), with the first bamboo c. 1–2 sun from each post. The mawatashi-dake are nailed or tied to the nuki where they cross.

#### Step 3 — Komai-dake (小舞竹) lashing
Split bamboo laths (小舞竹 *komai-dake*, c. 15–20 mm wide) are tied to the mawatashi-dake with **komai-nawa** (小舞縄): straw rope (藁縄 *waranawa*), hemp (麻), or hemp-palm fibre (棕櫚縄 *shuro-nawa*). Verticals (竪小舞 *tate-komai*) and horizontals (横小舞 *yoko-komai*) are placed on opposite faces of the mawatashi and woven with a continuous rope that zigzags between laths.
- Representative clear spacing between komai laths: c. 1–1.5 sun (30–45 mm), i.e. roughly two fingers; wider spacing loses clay grip, closer spacing prevents the clay from keying through.
- The komai net stops a few mm short of posts and beams (the clay bridges the gap).

#### Step 4 — Arakabe (荒壁) and uragaeshi (裏返し)
Rough clay (荒壁土 *arakabe-tsuchi*) — a local clayey soil mixed with chopped rice straw (藁すさ *wara-susa*, c. 5–10 cm lengths) and water, and traditionally **fermented/aged** (寝かせ *nekase*) for weeks to months so that the straw rots partly and the clay becomes more plastic and cohesive — is thrown and pressed onto one face of the lath so that it squeezes through the gaps (表塗り *omote-nuri*). When the front coat has set enough to be firm (days to weeks), the back face is coated (**uragaeshi**, 裏返し "turning over"), filling and locking the key.
- Thickness of arakabe incl. lath: c. 40–60 mm (varies with post size).
- **Drying** (乾燥) of arakabe: traditionally left as long as possible — at least several weeks in summer, commonly one to several months, and in fine work over a full season — until all shrinkage cracks have formed. Cracks are then filled.

#### Step 5 — Nukibuse (貫伏せ) and chiri-mawari
- **Nukibuse**: the face of each nuki is thinner than the arakabe around it (the clay is thin over the nuki), so a crack forms along it. The plasterer covers the nuki line with a strip of hemp cloth (寒冷紗 *kanreisha* or 麻布), or with fibre-rich clay, pressed into fresh clay — "burying the nuki".
- **Chiri-mawari** (散り廻り): along post and beam edges, hemp fibre tufts (のれん *noren*, ひげこ *higeko*) are nailed to the post in the chirijakuri zone and spread into the clay so that the plaster edge is anchored to the post and does not separate (the fibres span the shrinkage gap).
- **斑直し** (*muranaoshi*): levelling coat to correct unevenness before the middle coat.

#### Step 6 — Nakanuri (中塗り)
A finer clay with shorter straw fibre (中塗りすさ) and sand, c. 5–10 mm, trowelled flat to the chiri line. In rustic sukiya work the nakanuri itself may be the finish (中塗り仕上げ, *nakanuri-shiage*) or the arakabe left visible (荒壁仕上げ).

#### Step 7 — Uwanuri (上塗り) — finishes
| Finish | Japanese | Composition / character | Typical use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lime plaster | 漆喰 *shikkui* | Slaked lime + seaweed glue (角叉 *tsunomata*, 布海苔 *funori*) + hemp fibre; white, hard, fire-resistant | Storehouses, castle and temple walls, formal interiors; exterior. |
| Jūraku | 聚楽 (聚楽壁 *jūraku-kabe*) | Fine brown clay originally from the Jūrakudai area of Kyoto, with fine fibre; soft, matte, warm | The classic shoin and sukiya interior wall. |
| Ōtsu (polished) | 大津壁, 大津磨き *ōtsu-migaki* | Coloured clay + lime + fibre, trowelled and polished to a hard sheen | Formal interiors; tokonoma walls; kura interiors. |
| Sand wall | 砂壁 *sunakabe* | Coloured sand bound with glue | Economical interior finish (widespread in 20th c.). |
| Hand-polished lime | 漆喰磨き / 本磨き | Shikkui polished to mirror finish | Storehouse doors, prestige kura. |

**R14-030** — Arakabe clay **should** be aged (寝かせ) with its straw for weeks or longer before use. *Why:* aging increases plasticity and cohesion and reduces shrinkage cracking.

**R14-031** — Each earthen coat **must** be allowed to dry and crack before the next is applied, and arakabe **should** dry for at least several weeks (ideally a season). *Why:* shrinkage must occur in the lower coat; an upper coat laid over wet clay cracks with it.

**R14-032** — The nuki line and the post/beam edges **must** be reinforced (nukibuse; chiri-mawari with hemp fibres) before the middle coat. *Why:* these are the lines where clay thickness changes and where frame shrinkage opens cracks.

**R14-033** — Posts in shinkabe tsuchikabe work **should** have a chirijakuri groove at the plaster edge. *Why:* hides the edge gap when the post shrinks (散り切れ).

**R14-034** — Plastering **should not** be done in freezing weather, nor finished coats applied in direct summer sun or drying wind without shading and dampening; spring and autumn are the preferred seasons for final coats, while arakabe benefits from the long drying of summer. *Why:* frozen clay loses bond; rapid drying causes crazing (and lime needs slow carbonation).

#### Chiri dimension and wall thickness
The **chiri** (散り) is the setback of the wall surface from the post face. It determines both the visible "shadow" of the post and the wall thickness:

 wall thickness = post width − 2 × chiri (for a wall centred in the post and exposed on both sides).

Representative values for traditional tsuchikabe (these vary widely by region, post size and period; treat as indicative):
| Post size | Wall thickness (approx.) | Chiri each side (approx.) |
|---|---|---|
| 3 sun 5 bu (≈106 mm) | 2 sun (≈60 mm) | c. 7 bu (≈20–25 mm) |
| 4 sun (≈121 mm) | 2 sun 4 bu–2 sun 8 bu (≈70–85 mm) | c. 6–8 bu (≈18–25 mm) |
| 4 sun 5 bu–5 sun | 3 sun and more | c. 8 bu–1 sun |

Modern shinkabe on plasterboard backing typically uses a much smaller chiri (c. 10–15 mm). A single-sided chiri may be unequal (e.g. a thicker wall toward the exterior).

**R14-035** — The chiri **must** be constant along a given post face in a room and **should** be at least about 1/6 of the post face width in traditional tsuchikabe. *Why:* a varying chiri reads as a crooked post; too small a chiri leaves the wall too thin for the lath and coats, or loses the post's shadow. (The 1/6 figure is a common rule of thumb, not a canonical kiwari value.)

### 3.3 Board walls (板壁 *itakabe*)
Board cladding is used on exteriors (especially in wet or snowy regions, and as protection of the lower part of earthen walls — 腰 *koshi*), for storehouse jackets, and for interior wainscots.

#### Shitamiita (下見板) — horizontal lapped boards
| Type | Japanese | Construction |
|---|---|---|
| **Nankin-shitami** (南京下見) | 南京下見 (also 箱目地下見, English-style weatherboarding) | Horizontal boards lapped (each upper board over the one below) and nailed directly; no battens. Introduced/popularised in the Meiji period with Western construction. |
| **Oshibuchi-shitami** (押縁下見) | 押縁下見 | Horizontal lapped boards held by vertical **oshibuchi** battens at intervals (c. 3 shaku, at posts and studs), nailed through the battens. The classic Japanese form. |
| **Sasarako-shitami** (簓子下見) | 簓子下見 | Like oshibuchi-shitami, but the vertical battens (簓子 *sasarako*) are notched on their back in a saw-tooth profile (簓 *sasara*) to fit the steps of the lapped boards, giving a tight, precise appearance. Seen on castles, temples, and high-grade houses. |
| **Yoroi-shitami** (鎧下見) | 鎧下見 | Deeply lapped with a strong shadow (armor-like); a variant name used regionally. |

The boards are sugi, hinoki or matsu, c. 4–6 bu thick, lapped c. 5 bu–1 sun. Often charred (焼杉 *yakisugi*, 焼板 *yakiita*) in western Japan, or coated with bengara/persimmon tannin (柿渋 *kakishibu*) or oil/soot mixtures.

**R14-036** — Lapped exterior boards **must** lap upper-over-lower (water-shedding), and the oshibuchi/sasarako battens **should** be fixed through into posts or studs, not only into the boards. *Why:* weather; the boards are held by the battens and allowed to move beneath them.

**R14-037** — Board cladding over earthen walls **should** be separated from the wall by an air gap or battens and **should** be detachable. *Why:* the clay wall must dry; the boards are a sacrificial rain screen renewed every few decades (→ Ch. 16).

#### Tate-hameita (竪羽目板) and hameita (羽目板)
**Hameita** are boards set flush in a frame or between posts (the word implies fitting into a plane, 羽目 *hame*). **Tate-hameita** are vertical boards, usually T&G (本実) or 相決り or with loose splines, fixed top and bottom into grooves or onto 胴縁 (*dobuchi*, horizontal furring), and sometimes with 目板 (*meita*, cover battens) over the joints (目板張り *meita-bari*). **Yoko-hameita** are horizontal equivalents (as in corridor wainscots).

#### Koshikabe (腰壁)
The lower part of a wall (up to c. 2.5–3 shaku) exposed to feet, furniture, splashing and weather is often finished differently — boards (腰板 *koshiita*), a harder plaster, or tile (海鼠壁 *namako-kabe* on storehouses). A horizontal rail (腰長押 *koshi-nageshi* or 見切縁 *mikiri-buchi*) separates koshi from the upper wall.

**R14-038** — Exterior walls below c. 3 shaku, and all walls within splash range of the ground, **should** have a protective koshi (board, tile, or hard plaster). *Why:* splash-back and snow degrade clay rapidly.

## 4. Ceilings (天井 *tenjō*)

### 4.1 Structure common to hung ceilings
Traditional Japanese ceilings in houses are almost always **suspended** (吊り天井) from the roof or floor structure above, so that they are independent of the roof's movement and can be adjusted.

| Member | Japanese | Function | Representative size |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ceiling rim | 回り縁 *mawaribuchi* | Perimeter moulding fixed to posts; carries the ends of saobuchi and ceiling boards; defines the ceiling height | 1 sun 5 bu–2 sun square (≈45–60 mm), often with 面 on the lower show edge |
| Ceiling battens | 竿縁 *saobuchi* (also 棹縁) | Exposed battens spanning between opposite mawaribuchi, supporting the boards from below | c. 1 sun × 1 sun 2 bu, at c. 1 shaku 5 sun (≈450 mm) centres (varies with room) |
| Ceiling boards | 天井板 *tenjō-ita* | Thin boards, sugi (杉柾, 杉杢, 秋田杉, 屋久杉 in fine work), c. 2–3 mm to 6 mm | Width 1 shaku+; laid across the saobuchi |
| Hidden joists | 野縁 *nobuchi* | Unseen battens above, parallel to the saobuchi, fixing the board backs | c. 1 sun 2 bu–1 sun 5 bu square |
| Hangers | 吊木 *tsurigi* | Vertical hangers from above to nobuchi / saobuchi | c. 1 sun square, at c. 3 shaku (≈900 mm) centres |
| Hanger supports | 吊木受け *tsurigi-uke* | Horizontal members spanning between roof beams/ joists to carry the tsurigi | c. 2–3 sun square |
| Board clips | 稲子 *inago* | Small wooden cleats glued/nailed to board backs at laps, holding adjacent boards together and to the saobuchi | — |

### 4.2 Saobuchi-tenjō (竿縁天井) — the standard domestic ceiling
Thin ceiling boards are laid across the saobuchi, each board overlapping the next (**羽重ね** *hagasane*, a lap of c. 1–2 sun, the thin edges often bevelled), and are held together by inago on the back. The saobuchi and boards are carried by the mawaribuchi at the room perimeter and by tsurigi above.

**The tokozashi rule.** The saobuchi **must run parallel to the tokonoma front** (the plane of the tokonoma opening). Saobuchi running *toward* the tokonoma, i.e. perpendicular to its front, are called **床差し / 床刺し** (*tokozashi*, "piercing the tokonoma") and are universally avoided. The ceiling boards therefore run perpendicular to the tokonoma, toward it. In rooms without a tokonoma, saobuchi customarily run parallel to the long side of the room, or parallel to the main opening (garden side) — but some practice runs them across the short span for structural reasons; the former is more common.

**Board lap direction.** The direction of the hagasane laps is also customary. The principle is that the visible step of each lap should not face the principal viewpoint (the seat of honour at the tokonoma, or the view on entering). Sources formulate the practical rule differently — some say "boards are laid starting from the tokonoma side", others "from the entrance the lap edges must not be visible" — and workshop practice varies. Treat the principle as firm and the exact wording as shop-dependent. **Flagged uncertainty:** the author could not confirm a single canonical formulation.

**R14-039** — Saobuchi **must** run parallel to the tokonoma front; tokozashi (battens pointing at the tokonoma) **must not** be used. *Why:* long-standing customary taboo; the same logic as R14-015.

**R14-040** — Ceiling boards in a saobuchi ceiling **should** be lapped (hagasane) with inago on the back rather than butt-jointed, and **should not** be nailed through their faces except through the saobuchi with hidden or very fine nails. *Why:* thin boards shrink visibly; a lap hides the shrinkage; inago keep laps aligned.

**R14-041** — Where ceiling boards are figured (杢 *moku*), boards **should** be selected and sequenced so that figure flows continuously across the room (板割り *itawari* planned on the floor before hanging). *Why:* appearance; ceiling boards are often consecutive cuts from one log (共木 *tomogi*) to allow this.

### 4.3 Other ceiling types
| Type | Japanese | Description | Use |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Coffered ceiling** | 格天井 *gōtenjō* | Heavy lattice of square ribs (格縁 *gōbuchi*) with square panels (鏡板) set in; joints of the ribs are half-lapped (相欠き) and the panels rest in rebates | Shoin audience halls, temples; the most formal flat ceiling |
| **Coved coffered ceiling** | 折上格天井 *oriage-gōtenjō* | Gōtenjō raised at its perimeter by a concave cove (折上げ, 支輪 *shirin*) | Rooms of highest status (e.g. jōdan-no-ma); double coved = 二重折上格天井 |
| **Small-lattice ceiling** | 小組格天井 *kogumi-gōtenjō* | Gōtenjō with a fine lattice inside each coffer | Formal shoin |
| **Saruboo ceiling** | 猿頬天井 *saruboo-tenjō* | Saobuchi-tenjō in which the saobuchi have a "monkey-cheek" profile — the lower corners heavily chamfered (猿頬面) | Refined variant of the saobuchi ceiling |
| **Wickerwork ceiling** | 網代天井 *ajiro-tenjō* | Woven thin strips of wood, bamboo or bark (杉皮, 檜皮, へぎ板) in patterns, held by battens | Sukiya, tea rooms, eaves soffits |
| **Boat-bottom ceiling** | 舟底天井 *funazoko-tenjō* | Ceiling rising to a central ridge in two slopes (like an inverted boat hull) | Tea rooms, corridors, sukiya |
| **Sloping (built-in) ceiling** | 掛込天井 *kakekomi-tenjō* | Ceiling following the rafters — the underside of the eave carried into the room, rafters exposed (化粧屋根裏 *keshō-yaneura*) | Tea rooms (esp. over the guest entrance area), sukiya |
| **Open-joint ceiling** | 目透し天井 *mesukashi-tenjō* | Boards laid with even open joints (目透し) over rebated edges, no saobuchi | Modern and sukiya rooms; with plywood in 20th c. |
| **Mirror ceiling** | 鏡天井 *kagami-tenjō* | Flat ceiling of wide boards with no visible battens, joints invisible, a single smooth plane | Temples (Zen halls — often with painted dragons), shrines |
| **Exposed roof underside** | 化粧屋根裏 *keshō-yaneura* | No ceiling; rafters and underboards finished | Temples (wayō), farmhouses (no ceiling at all over the doma: 天井なし) |

**R14-042** — In gōtenjō the ribs (格縁) **must** be cross-halved (相欠き) with the lap directions alternating consistently (e.g. all long-direction ribs on top) and the panels **must** be free to move in their rebates. *Why:* the grid must remain flat; panels restrained on four sides split.

**R14-043** — A room with a ceiling of higher status (oriage, gōtenjō) **should** be the room of higher status in the sequence; ceiling type encodes hierarchy (→ Ch. 13). *Why:* shoin-zukuri rank rules.

### 4.4 Suspension and height
**R14-044** — Hung ceilings **must** be hung from members that do not deflect with snow/roof loading (tsurigi-uke spanning between beams, not hung from rafters alone), and tsurigi **should** be fixed so their length can be adjusted (e.g. nailed through a slot, or wedged). *Why:* roof members move with load and seasons; a ceiling fixed rigidly to rafters sags or cracks along the mawaribuchi.

**R14-045** — A slight upward camber (起り *mukuri*) of a few mm per ken **should** be built into the centre of large flat ceilings. *Why:* a perfectly flat ceiling appears to sag (optical correction), and any real sag is absorbed.

Ceiling height rules are design rules and are treated in → Ch. 13. A widely quoted rule of thumb gives the ceiling height above the tatami as the uchinori height plus a figure proportional to the room size (e.g. uchinori + number of mats × c. 3 sun); present such formulas as approximate and school-dependent.

### 4.5 Mawaribuchi joints
The mawaribuchi is housed into the posts (大入れ, 1–2 bu), and at room corners without a post (rare) mitred (留め *tome*). Where two mawaribuchi meet at a post they are each housed. Joints along a long wall are made with a concealed splice (e.g. 目違い入り 殺ぎ継ぎ) placed over a post or at least over a hanger. At the tokonoma, the mawaribuchi runs above the otoshigake and is set at the same level as elsewhere, the tokonoma ceiling usually being higher (see §5).

**R14-046** — Mawaribuchi **must** be set dead level from the building datum (陸墨), not from the tops of the posts or the kamoi. *Why:* the ceiling plane is the most visible horizontal reference in the room; any out-of-level reads immediately.

### 5.1 Tokobashira (床柱)
- **Class:** finish post (化粧柱), usually also structural.
- **Typical use:** the post dividing the tokonoma from the adjacent tana or shoin bay; the symbolic centre of the formal room.
- **Materials:** in shoin style, square hinoki or sugi of the finest grade (四方柾 *shihō-masa* — straight grain on all four faces); in sukiya, natural-surface timbers: 北山杉 磨丸太 (*Kitayama-sugi migaki-maruta*, polished log), 絞丸太 (*shibori-maruta*, fluted), 赤松皮付 (red pine with bark), 黒柿, 鉄刀木, 紫檀, 桜皮付, 面皮柱 etc.
- **Orientation rules:** moto (butt) down — a reversed tokobashira (逆柱 *sakabashira*) is a severe taboo (→ Ch. 03). The best face (見付 *mitsuke*) faces the room/seat of honour. The core crack for drying is cut on the hidden face (背割り *sewari*, → Ch. 03) — on the wall side.
- **Assembly:** the tokobashira is usually erected with the frame at tatemae and must then be wrapped and protected (養生) through all later work. In some sukiya work, a purely decorative tokobashira is inserted later, fixed with hidden tenons at top and bottom (and a 雇い枘).

**R14-047** — The tokobashira **must** be set moto-down with its best face toward the room, its sewari toward the wall, and **must** be protected from tatemae to completion. *Why:* sakabashira taboo; appearance; the tokobashira is irreplaceable once the room is finished.

### 5.2 Tokogamachi (床框)
- **Class:** finish sill/rail (framing the front edge of the tokonoma floor).
- **Typical use:** front edge of a raised tokonoma (本床). Height above the tatami typically 3–5 sun in shoin, less (1–2 sun) or omitted (踏込床) in sukiya.
- **Materials:** shoin — black lacquered (真塗 *shin-nuri*) hinoki or a lacquered/ plain keyaki; sukiya — natural log (面皮, 皮付), 黒柿, 桑, 松.
- **Proportions:** height 3 sun 5 bu–4 sun 5 bu; depth (見込み) 2–3 sun (representative).
- **Joints:** housed (大入れ) into the tokobashira and the opposite post (相手柱 *aite-bashira*); where the post is irregular, scribed (光付け, R14-008). Fixed with concealed tenons (雇い枘) or a hidden draw-dovetail/bow-tie key (引き独鈷 *hiki-dokko*, → Ch. 09) that pulls the kamachi into the post housing; on its back it carries a rebate or 決り to receive the tokoita, and is supported from below by blocks/束 (框受け). No face fixing.
- **Assembly:** set after the floor frame, before the toko walls' final coat; protected with paper.

**R14-048** — The tokogamachi **must** be fixed without visible fasteners and **must** be supported continuously or at close intervals from below. *Why:* R14-006; it is sat against and leaned on and must not rock.

### 5.3 Tokoita (床板) and jiita (地板)
The **tokoita** is the floor of a board-floored tokonoma: a single wide board (一枚板) of keyaki, matsu, tochi, or sugi, planed and often lacquered (拭き漆 *fuki-urushi*), set in a rebate behind the tokogamachi and carried on bearers. If a single board cannot be had, boards are edge-joined (矧ぎ) with loose tongues and battened from below with **sliding dovetail battens** (吸付き桟 *suitsuki-zan*, §9) that allow movement while keeping the board flat.

The **jiita** (地板) is the base board at floor level (or slightly raised) in the tana (shelf) bay beside the tokonoma, under the chigaidana and jibukuro. It is typically a wide board flush with or a little above the tatami, bordered by a thin 畳寄せ-like edge.

**R14-049** — Wide tokoita and jiita **must** be restrained against cupping only by sliding battens (suitsuki-zan) or battens fixed through slotted holes, never by battens glued/nailed rigidly across their width. *Why:* R14-005.

### 5.4 Otoshigake (落掛け)
- **Class:** finish head member of the tokonoma opening.
- **Typical use:** spans between tokobashira and aite-bashira across the top of the tokonoma, set higher than the room's kamoi line (commonly by about the height of the nageshi or more, so that the toko opening appears taller), below the tokonoma ceiling.
- **Materials:** sugi or kiri straight-grain (柾), or in sukiya, bamboo, pine or other characterful woods; typically light-looking.
- **Proportions:** representative 見付 (face height) 1 sun 5 bu–2 sun 5 bu, 見込み 2–3 sun.
- **Joints:** housed into the tokobashira and aite-bashira; commonly fixed with a hidden tenon and a key/wedge from the concealed upper side (e.g. 目違い + 込み栓 from above, hidden by the toko wall), or with hiki-dokko. The toko-kabe (wall above the otoshigake, 小壁 *kokabe*) is plastered down onto its top.
- **Rules:** below.

**R14-050** — The otoshigake **should** be set higher than the room's kamoi/uchinori line and **must** be level; the toko ceiling behind it **should** be higher than or equal to the room ceiling. *Why:* spatial hierarchy — the tokonoma is the highest space; a low otoshigake crowds the hanging scroll (掛軸 *kakejiku*).

### 5.5 Chigaidana (違い棚), tenbukuro (天袋), jibukuro (地袋)
The tana bay (床脇 *tokowaki*) beside the tokonoma contains: an upper cupboard (**tenbukuro** 天袋) at ceiling level, staggered shelves (**chigaidana** 違い棚), and often a low cupboard (**jibukuro** 地袋) above the jiita.

**Chigaidana:** two (or more) shelves at different heights, the upper overlapping the lower, joined by a short vertical strut, the **ebizuka** (海老束, "shrimp strut" — referring to its curved or bent-back form in some variants, though usually straight). The upper shelf's free end often carries a raised curled lip, the **fudegaeshi** (筆返し, "brush-stopper"), to stop brushes rolling off.
- **Shelf boards (棚板):** c. 8 bu–1 sun 2 bu thick, housed into the walls/posts at their outer ends (大入れ into the post; into a 間柱 or 棚受け hidden in the wall), usually lacquered in shoin style.
- **Ebizuka:** c. 8 bu–1 sun square; tenoned (short tenons, 短枘) into the upper and lower shelf; the tenon on the upper shelf is often blind (not seen from above).
- **Fudegaeshi:** a separate carved piece joined to the shelf end by a concealed tongue (雇い実) and hidden tenons, or carved from the solid in the best work.
- **Order:** the shelves and ebizuka are assembled as a unit and slid into their housings before the finish coat of the surrounding walls; the housings are cut in the posts during the zōsaku stage, and the wall-side supports are built into the wall framing before lathing/plastering.

**Tenbukuro/Jibukuro:** small cupboards with small fusuma (袋戸 *fukuro-do*), framed by their own shikii (無目 or grooved) and kamoi, set flush with the tana posts, and with a top board (天板) — the jibukuro top board is often a fine single board.

**R14-051** — In chigaidana, the upper shelf **should** be on the side away from the tokonoma or toward it according to the design convention of the school followed (both occur), but the fudegaeshi **must** be on the free (open) end of the upper shelf; the shelves **must** be housed into posts, not merely supported on cleats. *Why:* the fudegaeshi's function is at the free edge; housing hides movement and supports the shelf along its end.

**R14-052** — Tana shelves and supports **must** be built into the wall framing before the wall is plastered. *Why:* they cannot be inserted afterwards without breaking the plaster.

### 5.6 Tsuke-shoin (付書院)
The **tsuke-shoin** (built-in writing desk bay; → Ch. 13) projects outward from the room beside the tokonoma, with a desk board (地板 or 机板) at c. 1 shaku 2 sun–1 shaku 5 sun above the tatami, a shōji window (書院障子, typically with kumiko and a small ranma above: 書院欄間), and its own small kamoi and shikii. Its desk board is a wide single board fixed as for the tokoita; its shōji run in shallow grooves in the desk board or on a separate shikii. It is framed as a small projecting box (出書院 *de-shoin*) supported by the frame's cantilevered members or its own posts.

### 5.7 Nageshi (長押)
Originally a structural horizontal member binding posts (on the outside of the posts in ancient temple architecture, → Ch. 12), the **nageshi** in shoin-zukuri became a purely decorative (and status-marking) moulding running around the room over the kamoi, at the uchinori level (内法長押 *uchinori-nageshi*). Other nageshi: 天井長押 (*tenjō-nageshi*, under the ceiling), 腰長押, 地長押/切目長押 (at floor level, temples).

- **Section:** a characteristic triangular-trapezoid section (cut from a square log diagonally, often two nageshi from one square, 長押挽き *nageshi-biki*), with the thin top edge against the wall and a vertical show face.
- **Proportions:** representative face height 0.8–0.9 × the post width (e.g. 3 sun 2 bu–3 sun 6 bu for a 4-sun post), the exact ratio being a matter of kiwari (→ Ch. 02, Ch. 13); depth at the bottom c. 1 sun+.
- **Fixing:** nailed through into the posts (長押釘), with the nail heads covered by decorative metal **kugikakushi** (釘隠し) at each post in formal rooms — the origin of the kugikakushi as an ornament. In sukiya, the nageshi is thin, often a half-round log or omitted entirely (sukiya frequently eliminates the nageshi).
- **Joints:** along long walls, splices over a post or with a concealed mitred splice; at room corners, mitred (留め *tome*).

#### Nageshi at the tokobashira — hina-dome, kata-sabaki, makura-sabaki
Where the nageshi meets the tokobashira (which projects into the tokonoma), the end must be terminated. Three standard forms, ranked in the 真・行・草 (*shin–gyō–sō*, formal–semi–informal) scheme:

| Rank | Form | Japanese | Description (one common reading) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Shin (most formal) | **Makura-sabaki** | 枕捌き | The nageshi wraps around the tokobashira and returns along its side face into the tokonoma, terminating in a small mitred return; the end grain is fully concealed. |
| Gyō | **Kata-sabaki** | 片捌き | Wraps only partly (one side returns). |
| Sō (least formal) | **Hina-dome** | 雛留め | The nageshi stops at the tokobashira with a small mitred stop that hides the end grain on the room face; the visible return on the post face is small (sources cite a return about 1/6 of the post width). |

Terminology and exact geometry differ between schools and sources; the above follows common modern glossary definitions. **Flagged uncertainty:** the ranking and exact return proportions vary.

**R14-053** — Nageshi **must not** show end grain (木口) on a show face; ends at posts, the tokonoma or at openings **must** be closed by a mitre, return (捌き) or stop (留め). *Why:* exposed end grain is regarded as unfinished work in formal zōsaku.

**R14-054** — Nageshi nails at posts **should** be covered by kugikakushi in formal (shoin) rooms; in sukiya rooms nageshi **may** be omitted or reduced, in which case no kugikakushi is used. *Why:* the kugikakushi is both cover and status mark; its presence in a sukiya room is a stylistic error.

## 6. Opening frames — uchinori (内法)

The **uchinori** (内法) is the zone of the wall defined by the openings: the shikii at the bottom, the kamoi at the top, and the clear height between them (the **uchinori height** 内法高 — traditionally 5 shaku 7 sun–5 shaku 8 sun ≈ 1,730–1,760 mm in houses; → Ch. 02, Ch. 13). Uchinori members are the carpenter's most precise zōsaku because sliding fittings run in them.

### 6.1 Shikii (敷居) and kamoi (鴨居)
- **Class:** zōsaku sill (shikii) and head (kamoi) of sliding openings.
- **Typical use:** all openings with sliding tategu: between rooms (fusuma), room/engawa (shōji), engawa/outside (amado, garasu-do).
- **Materials:** shikii — hinoki, sugi (赤身), matsu, or hard wood (sakura, kashi) for wear; kamoi — hinoki, sugi, straight-grained and stable. Heartwood preferred.
- **Section (representative):** shikii 見付 (top width) = post width (e.g. 4 sun for 4-sun posts) × height 1 sun 5 bu–1 sun 8 bu; kamoi width = post width × face height 1 sun 5 bu–1 sun 8 bu (the kamoi is usually set flush with the post faces or slightly recessed).
- **Grooves (溝 *mizo*) and hibata (樋端):**
  - Number: **ippon-mizo** (一筋, one groove — e.g. for a single-track door or amado), **nihon-mizo** (二筋, two grooves — the standard for a pair of sliding panels), **sanbon-mizo** (三筋 — three tracks, e.g. glazed door + amado + screen in engawa openings).
  - The ridges between/beside grooves are **hibata** (樋端): the central one **naka-hibata** (中樋端), the outer ones **soto-hibata** (外樋端).
  - Standard groove widths and naka-hibata (the "four-seven" and "three-seven" rules):

| Name | Naka-hibata | Groove width | Use |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Shi-shichi** (四七) | 4 bu (≈12 mm) | 7 bu (≈21 mm) | Shōji (shōji c. 1 sun ≈ 30 mm thick, edge rebated to run in 7-bu groove) |
| **San-shichi** (三七) | 3 bu (≈9 mm) | 7 bu (≈21 mm) | Fusuma and cupboard doors (fusuma c. 9 bu ≈ 27 mm or less thick) |

  With a 1-sun shōji and 4-7 grooves, the two panels are on a 1-sun-1-bu pitch, leaving c. 1 bu between them — a well-known self-consistency of the system.
  - **Groove depth (representative):** shikii 1 bu 5 rin–2 bu (≈4.5–6 mm); kamoi c. 5 bu (≈15 mm), i.e. considerably deeper. Modern standard practice uses c. 3 mm for shikii and 15 mm for kamoi.

**R14-055** — Kamoi grooves **must** be deeper than shikii grooves by more than the depth of the shikii groove plus a clearance (e.g. kamoi 5 bu vs shikii 1.5–2 bu). *Why:* a sliding panel is installed by lifting it up into the kamoi groove and dropping it into the shikii groove; it must be able to rise clear of the shikii without leaving the kamoi groove.

**R14-056** — Grooves in shikii and kamoi **must** be cut on the **kiomote** (bark side) face: shikii kiomote up, kamoi kiomote down. *Why:* on drying, a flat-sawn member bows with the kiura side becoming convex; with grooves on the kiomote face, the member curves *away* from the fitting (the groove side becomes slightly concave), keeping the panel free. Grooves on the kiura face would bulge toward the panel and bind it.

**R14-057** — Standard groove sets **should** follow shi-shichi (4-bu hibata / 7-bu groove) for shōji and san-shichi (3-bu / 7-bu) for fusuma unless the tategu maker specifies otherwise, and the carpenter and tategu maker **must** agree the groove set before the kamoi and shikii are grooved. *Why:* panel thickness, meeting gap and groove set form a system; a mismatch is not correctable after installation.

**R14-058** — Kamoi and shikii of a pair **must** be marked, grooved and installed so that their grooves are in exact vertical alignment (same 心 *shin* line from a common reference), checked with a plumb bob (下げ振り) at both ends. *Why:* misaligned grooves make the panel lean and bind.

### 6.2 Installation between posts
A kamoi or shikii spans between two posts that are already standing, so it cannot be dropped in from above with a tenon at both ends. Common methods (representative):
1. **One end housed and tenoned, the other end slid in (遣り返し *yarikaeshi*):** a deeper housing is cut in one post; the member is inserted into this deeper housing, swung into line, and then slid back into the shallower housing of the other post. The first post's housing is then packed or wedged so the member cannot slide back. *(Terminology varies; "yarikaeshi" is one common name.)*
2. **Dropped in from above with a 目違い (*megai*, stub tongue) in a vertical slot:** the post is slotted from above (落とし込み *otoshikomi*) for a short tongue on the member end; the member is dropped in and fixed with a hidden nail or a 横栓 (*yokosen*, cross-pin) from the concealed side.
3. **Hidden wedged tenon (地獄枘 *jigoku-hozo*):** for shikii set during frame assembly.

The kamoi is typically also fixed at mid-span to the structure above.

### 6.3 Tsurizuka (吊束) and kamoi deflection
A kamoi spanning 1 ken or more (especially 2 ken openings of four fusuma) will sag under its own weight, the weight of the kokabe plaster above it, and the upward pull of the fittings. It is therefore hung at mid-span (or at the fusuma meeting points) from a beam above by a **tsurizuka** (吊束, "hanging strut"): a small stud dovetailed (蟻 *ari*) into the top of the kamoi and nailed or tenoned into the beam or 天井 structure above, concealed in the kokabe or above the ceiling.

Some carpenters also build in a slight upward camber (**起り** *mukuri*) of a few mm at mid-span of long kamoi, or adjust the tsurizuka to lift the kamoi slightly before plastering, anticipating settlement. This is a shop practice rather than a universal rule. **Flagged:** not universally documented.

**R14-059** — Any kamoi spanning more than about 1 ken **must** be hung from the structure above by tsurizuka (dovetailed into the kamoi top), or be replaced by a structural 差鴨居 (*sashigamoi*, → Ch. 10). *Why:* a sagging kamoi pinches the tategu and closes the meeting gaps.

**R14-060** — Tsurizuka **should** be installed so that their length can be adjusted (wedged or nailed after the kamoi is set level), not so rigidly that roof settlement drags the kamoi up or down. *Why:* the beam above deflects seasonally; the kamoi must remain level.

### 6.4 Mume, tsukegamoi, and related members
- **Mume** (無目): a shikii or kamoi without grooves — e.g. a 無目敷居 where there are no sliding panels but the floor line is continued, or a 無目鴨居 above a fixed ranma. It keeps the uchinori line continuous.
- **Tsukegamoi** (付鴨居): a false kamoi fixed flush to a wall face where there is no opening, continuing the kamoi line around the room so that the uchinori reads as a continuous band. Fixed to the posts with housings and to the wall framing.
- **Sashigamoi** (差鴨居): a deep, structural kamoi (often 1 shaku+) tenoned through the posts, acting as a beam — a frame member, → Ch. 10.
- **Kamoi on the engawa side:** grooves for amado are cut in a separate outer kamoi (雨戸鴨居) or in the engawa's head member (一筋鴨居).

**R14-061** — In a formal room, the uchinori line (kamoi or tsukegamoi, and nageshi) **should** run continuously around the room at one level, interrupted only by the tokonoma (where the otoshigake is higher). *Why:* the continuous band is the organizing horizontal of shoin interiors.

### 6.5 Tatetsuke (建付け) — fitting sliding panels
**Tatetsuke** is both the act of fitting tategu to their openings and the quality of that fit. Good tatetsuke means: the panels slide with a light touch, stand plumb, meet their neighbours and posts without light gaps, and do not rattle.

Key features:
- **Meshiawase** (召し合わせ): the meeting stiles of two panels that overlap when closed. In shōji the meeting stiles are often slightly thicker or rebated so that the overlap is tight; in amado and glass doors a lapped or rebated meshiawase (召し合わせ決り) prevents draughts.
- **Todatari** (戸当たり): the stop — the edge of the post (or a planted strip) against which a panel closes. For shōji, the stile closes into a shallow groove or against the post face; the post is sometimes grooved (戸当たり決り) to receive the stile edge so no light passes.
- **Clearance:** panels are made so that at the top they rise into the kamoi groove with a clearance of c. 1–2 mm below the groove bottom when resting in the shikii (the "lift" gap), and they are planed to fit the actual opening, which is rarely perfectly rectangular.
- **Adjustment:** tategu makers measure each opening (採寸) individually, fit each panel in place, and plane the top and bottom edges and stiles to the as-built opening.

**R14-062** — Tategu **must** be made to the as-built opening (measured after zōsaku is complete) and each panel marked for its position; panels **must not** be assumed interchangeable. *Why:* as with tatami (R14-013), openings deviate from nominal.

**R14-063** — Meeting stiles (meshiawase) of paired sliding panels **should** overlap by the stile thickness or more when closed, and the panels **should** close against a todatari groove or stop on the post. *Why:* light- and draught-tightness; a panel that closes only against a flat post leaves a visible light line.

## 7. Tategu — the fittings (建具)

Tategu are made in the tategu maker's workshop (建具屋) to the carpenter's openings. They are light, precise, and nearly all joinery is dry-fitted or minimally glued so that they can be taken apart and repaired (→ Ch. 16). Species: hinoki, sugi (especially 秋田杉, 吉野杉 赤身 柾), sawara, hiba, kiri for light panels; for amado and heavy doors, sugi or hinoki with hard-wood inserts.

**R14-064** — Tategu frame members **should** be quarter-sawn (柾目) or rift stock of fully dried timber, and **must** be free of twist and spiral grain. *Why:* thin sliding frames must stay flat and straight for decades in a single groove; any twist makes the panel rock or bind.

### 7.1 Shōji (障子)
- **Class:** framed paper-covered sliding screen.
- **Typical use:** between rooms and engawa; windows (with 腰 or as 書院障子); ranma.
- **Parts:**
  - **竪框** *tate-gamachi* (stiles) — representative 見付 7–8 bu (21–24 mm) × 見込 1 sun (30 mm); Kyoto work tends to thinner 見付 (c. 6–7 bu) for elegance.
  - **上桟** *kami-zan* (top rail) — 見付 c. 8 bu–1 sun.
  - **下桟** *shimo-zan* (bottom rail) — 見付 c. 1 sun 2 bu–2 sun (wider, to resist wear and give a visual base).
  - **組子** *kumiko* (lattice bars): **竪子** *tatego* (vertical) and **横子** *yokogo* (horizontal); representative 見付 2–3 bu (6–9 mm) × 見込 4–6 bu (12–18 mm). Kumiko are set flush with the paper face of the frame (usually the outer/engawa face) and recessed from the room face, so the lattice is seen in relief from inside.
  - **腰板** *koshiita* (optional lower panel) — thin board in grooves (小穴) in stiles and rails, framed by a 腰桟 *koshi-zan* (mid rail).
- **Proportions:** height = uchinori height + groove engagement (top into kamoi groove, bottom rebated into shikii groove); width = opening ÷ number of panels + meshiawase overlap.
- **Edge rebates:** top and bottom rails (and stile ends) are rebated on one or both faces (決り *shakuri*) to a tongue that runs in the 7-bu groove, since the shōji (1 sun) is thicker than the groove.

**Shōji types by kumiko arrangement:**
| Type | Japanese | Description |
|---|---|---|
| Standard / horizontal-dense | 横組障子, 横繁障子 *yoko-shige shōji* | Many horizontal kumiko, few verticals; the traditional paper width governs horizontal spacing |
| Vertical-dense | 竪繁障子 *tate-shige shōji* | Many vertical kumiko |
| Coarse grid | 荒組障子 *aragumi shōji* | Wide spacing (few kumiko); formal and austere |
| Square grid | 枡組 / 本繁 | Equal spacing both ways |
| Snow-viewing | 雪見障子 *yukimi shōji* | Lower portion glazed (glass), sometimes with a sliding paper panel that lifts vertically (摺り上げ *suriage*) to reveal the glass |
| Cat-flap | 猫間障子 *nekoma shōji* | A small sliding paper panel (孫障子 *mago-shōji*) within the main shōji, sliding sideways, often over glass |
| With lower board | 腰付障子 / 腰高障子 *koshidaka shōji* | Koshiita of c. 1–2 shaku (腰付) or up to c. 2 shaku 5 sun+ (腰高, used on exterior and kitchen) |
| Ranma shōji | 欄間障子 | Small shōji in the ranma |

A regional tendency is sometimes stated that yoko-shige was preferred in Kantō and tate-shige in Kansai; this is a generalisation with many exceptions (**flagged**).

**Paper (障子紙):** traditionally 美濃紙 (*Mino-gami*) or other kōzo-fibre washi, in rolls whose width (c. 9 sun ≈ 27–28 cm, representative for traditional 美濃判) historically set the horizontal kumiko spacing so that each paper joint falls on a yokogo. The paper is pasted with starch paste (糊 *nori*) on the kumiko and frame.

**R14-065** — Shōji paper **should** be applied beginning at the bottom, each upper strip lapping over the one below, with laps falling on a yokogo. *Why:* the lap edge then faces downward and does not collect dust; laps on kumiko are invisible from inside.

**R14-066** — The kumiko face of a shōji (unpapered side) **should** face the interior room; the paper faces the engawa or exterior. *Why:* the lattice is the interior ornament; paper on the outside is protected by the engawa and amado and receives light evenly. (Ranma and interior shōji between rooms may be papered on either or both sides.)

**R14-067** — Kumiko **must not** be glued at their intersections; they **should** be dry-fitted with a tight friction fit. *Why:* allows repair (a broken kumiko can be replaced) and movement; glue lines in thin kumiko fail and stain.

### 7.2 Fusuma (襖)
- **Class:** opaque, paper-covered sliding panel with a light lattice core.
- **Typical use:** between rooms; cupboards (押入れ *oshiire*), tenbukuro/jibukuro (袋襖).
- **Core (骨 *hone*):** a light lattice of sugi strips — representative 縦骨 3 bars and 横骨 11 bars inside a perimeter frame (框, *kamachi*, of stronger section), plus a local reinforcing bar or block (**力骨** *chikara-bone*, 引手板 *hikite-ita*) at the pull position. Joints: half-lapped (相欠き) intersections, nailed with bamboo or fine nails; perimeter joints nailed/tenoned.
- **Underlayers (下張り *shitabari*) — representative sequence for a traditional 本襖 (varies by maker and region; 5 to 9+ layers):**
  1. **骨縛り** *hone-shibari* — strong paper pasted fully onto the lattice to bind it.
  2. **打ち付け張り** *uchitsuke-bari* — a full-pasted layer (e.g. on the perimeter).
  3. **蓑張り** *mino-bari* — several layers (often three) of small sheets pasted only along one edge and overlapped like a straw rain-cape (蓑 *mino*), creating a cushion that absorbs movement.
  4. **蓑縛り** *mino-shibari* — layer pasted over the mino layers to bind them.
  5. **下袋張り** *shita-fukuro-bari* / **上袋張り** *uwa-fukuro-bari* — "bag" layers pasted only at their edges, so that they float and tighten like a drumhead when the paste dries.
  6. **上張り** *uwabari* — the finish paper: 鳥の子 (*torinoko*), 奉書 (*hōsho*), 唐紙 (*karakami*, woodblock-printed), or cloth.
- **Fuchi (縁):** separate perimeter frame, lacquered (塗り縁 *nuri-buchi*), stained or plain (白木), attached to the core with **折れ合い釘** (*oreai-kugi*, bent interlocking nails) that hook into the core so the fuchi can be removed without damage for re-papering.
- **Hikite (引手):** recessed metal, lacquer, ceramic or wood pull, set in a mortise in the chikara-bone and fixed with tiny nails (引手釘) through its flange; centre height representative c. 2 shaku 5 sun–2 shaku 7 sun (≈760–820 mm) above the floor, set back from the meshiawase stile.
- **Proportions:** thickness c. 7 bu–9 bu (21–27 mm); fits the san-shichi groove set (R14-057).

**R14-068** — Fusuma **must** be built with a floating underlayer system (mino and fukuro layers) rather than a single glued paper. *Why:* the floating layers let the lattice move and let the finish paper tighten evenly; a single glued layer telegraphs the lattice and tears.

**R14-069** — Fusuma fuchi **should** be fixed with oreai-kugi (or equivalent concealed, removable fixing), never glued. *Why:* fusuma are re-papered many times in their life (→ Ch. 16); the fuchi must come off intact.

### 7.3 Itado (板戸) — board doors
A door of boards, either (a) **framed** (框組 *kamachi-gumi*) with a thin panel (鏡板 *kagami-ita*) in grooves, or (b) **boarded** — wide boards edge-joined (矧ぎ, with 雇い実 or 本実) and held flat by **端喰** (*hashibami*, breadboard ends) and/or **吸付き桟** (*suitsuki-zan*, sliding dovetail battens) on the back. Uses: storage rooms, kitchens, exterior doors, 杉戸 (*sugido* — painted cedar board doors in temples and shoin corridors, often with a single wide 杉 panel painted with figures). See §9 for the joints.

### 7.4 Mairado (舞良戸)
A framed board door whose face carries closely spaced thin horizontal battens (**舞良子** *mairako*), typically at 1–2 sun intervals, over a thin panel. The classic sliding outer door (遣戸 *yarido*) of shoin-zukuri and of temple sub-halls, placed outside the shōji. The mairako are fixed to the panel and let into the stiles with short tenons or housed.

### 7.5 Kōshido (格子戸)
A door or window of vertical (or vertical + horizontal) bars (格子 *kōshi*) in a frame. The machiya's 京格子/千本格子 (dense vertical bars) give privacy from the street while letting light and air through. Bars are tenoned into top and bottom rails; horizontal 貫 (*nuki*) bars pass through them. Often backed by glass or paper. For entrance doors, 格子戸 may be sliding (引戸).

### 7.6 Amado (雨戸) and tobukuro (戸袋)
- **Amado** (rain doors / storm shutters): board doors (sugi, c. 5–6 bu thick) on the outermost track of the engawa, closed at night and in storms. Construction: a thin frame with boards (or a single-panel 板戸 with 桟), light enough to handle one-by-one.
- **Track:** a single groove (一筋) in the outer shikii and kamoi; each door is slid out of the tobukuro along the track in sequence and the last is bolted.
- **Locks (猿 *saru*):** wooden sliding bolts — 上猿 (*kami-zaru*, into the kamoi), 落とし猿 / 下猿 (*otoshi-zaru*, dropping into a hole in the shikii), 横猿 (*yoko-zaru*, sideways into the post).
- **Tobukuro** (戸袋): a box at the end of the run, projecting outside the building face, into which the amado are stacked by day; its outer face is a board panel (戸袋鏡板), often decorated; it has its own small roof or is under the eave.
- **Tomawashi** (戸回し / 戸廻し): at a corner of an engawa, a pivoting fitting (a turning post or roller) that lets each amado be turned round the corner to continue along the next side. (Detail varies; **flagged** for exact mechanism.)

**R14-070** — The amado track **must** be a single continuous groove with no step at joints, and the tobukuro **must** be placed so that all doors can be stacked in it; its bottom **must** drain. *Why:* the doors are pushed one after another; any step jams them; water collects in a closed box and rots the doors.

### 7.7 Shitomido (蔀戸)
A heavy lattice-faced board shutter hinged at its top (from the nageshi or a head member) and swung upward and outward, then hooked to hangers (吊り金具) under the eave; used in Heian-period residences and later shrine/temple architecture. **Hajitomi** (半蔀) is the two-part version: the upper half hinged and lifted, the lower half fixed or removable. Construction: a grid of square battens (格子) on one or both faces of a board core, frame-and-panel. Hinges: iron straps or wooden pivots.

### 7.8 Sankarado (桟唐戸)
The paneled, pivoting double doors of Zen-style (禅宗様) and later temple architecture: a heavy frame of stiles and rails with raised or carved panels, often with lattice (格子) or open-work in the upper part, hung on pivots (軸 *jiku*) turning in sockets in the threshold (藁座 *waraza*) and head. See → Ch. 12 for style rules. Their frame joinery follows the kamachi-gumi principles of §9 at a larger scale (through tenons, wedged; 端喰 on panels).

### 7.9 Ranma (欄間)
Openings in the kokabe (small wall) zone between kamoi and ceiling or nageshi, for light and ventilation between rooms or above the engawa shōji.
| Type | Japanese | Description |
|---|---|---|
| Rod ranma | 筬欄間 *osa-ranma* | Fine vertical rods like the reed of a loom (筬 *osa*) between rails; shoin |
| Carved panel | 彫刻欄間 / 透彫欄間 *sukashibori* | Carved (often pierced) wooden panel |
| Kumiko ranma | 組子欄間 | Kumiko lattice (asanoha etc.) |
| Shōji ranma | 障子欄間 | Paper-covered lattice |
| Board ranma | 板欄間 | Board with pierced motif (透かし) |
| Shoin ranma | 書院欄間 | Above the tsuke-shoin shōji |
- **Fixing:** set in a frame consisting of the kamoi below (a mume top surface or a separate 欄間敷居 *ranma-shikii* with a shallow groove) and a 欄間鴨居 above, so that the ranma can be lifted out for cleaning and repair.

**R14-071** — Ranma **should** be installed removable (set in shallow grooves, not nailed) and fixed ranma frames **must** be installed after the kamoi is hung and levelled. *Why:* ranma are the most fragile and most valuable fittings; they are removed during repair and moving (→ Ch. 16); a ranma fixed before the kamoi settles cracks.

## 8. Kumiko craft (組子細工 *kumiko-zaiku*)

Kumiko is the assembly of thin, precisely cut wooden strips into geometric lattices, without glue or nails. It is used in shōji, ranma, and decorative panels. Its centres of production include Ōkawa (Fukuoka), Kiso and Kanuma (Tochigi), among others; the craft vocabulary below is shared broadly but varies in detail between shops.

### 8.1 Materials
Straight, fine-grained, quarter-sawn softwoods that cut crisply and do not split: hinoki (standard), sugi (赤身, 柾), sawara, hiba; for colour contrast, 神代杉 (*jindai-sugi*, buried cedar), 桂, 朴, 黒檀. Stock is prepared as long strips of exact section.

### 8.2 Jigumi (地組) — the ground lattice
All patterned kumiko begins with a **jigumi**, a ground lattice into which pattern pieces (組子 *kumiko*, 葉 *ha*, etc.) are fitted.
- **Square jigumi (角地組 *kaku-jigumi*):** vertical and horizontal strips half-lapped (相欠き). Pattern pieces (e.g. for 胡麻 *goma*, 角麻の葉 *kaku-asanoha*) are fitted into each square.
- **Diamond / 60° jigumi (菱地組 *hishi-jigumi*, 三角地組 *sankaku-jigumi*):** three families of strips at 60° to one another crossing at single points, using the **三つ組手** (*mitsu-kumite*, three-way lap). This forms equilateral triangles into which the classic patterns (麻の葉 *asanoha*, 桜 *sakura*, 竜胆 *rindō*, 胡麻 variations) are fitted.
- **Hexagon (亀甲 *kikkō*):** formed within the triangular jigumi or as a separate jigumi.

#### Aikaki (相欠き) in square jigumi
Each strip is notched to half its 見込 (depth) at every crossing, the notch width exactly equal to the 見付 (face width) of the crossing strip. All strips of a family are clamped together (束ね *tabane*) and notched in one operation (saw kerfs at each side then chisel or a dado jig), guaranteeing identical spacing.

#### Mitsu-kumite (三つ組手)
Three strips cross at one point at 60° to each other. At the crossing, each strip retains (in one common method) one third of its depth, the other two-thirds being cut away so the three strips stack into the full depth; the notch walls are cut at 60° so that the strips lock one another. The first two strips (notched one from above, one from below, in the common method) are assembled, and the third, which is notched on both faces, is pressed in at the crossing. Different shops use different cut geometries; the principle — three strips at one point, full-depth lock, no glue — is constant. **(Flagged: specific notch proportions vary.)**

### 8.3 Pattern pieces and angles
Pattern pieces are cut with angled ends to fit precisely into the jigumi cells:
- In a triangular cell (60°), asanoha requires three pieces running from the corners to the centre (meeting at 120°), with ends cut to fit the 60° corners (bisected: two 30° faces) — the classic "hemp-leaf" star.
- Square-cell patterns use 45° and 22.5°/67.5° angles.
- The end angles are cut using **angle jigs** (角度定規 *kakudo-jōgi*; also 留め定規, 組子定規) — hardwood guides in which a strip is held at the required angle while the end is planed on a shooting board with a very sharp plane (組子鉋) — so that every piece of a given kind is identical.
- Pieces are fitted in a set sequence: the "backbone" piece of each cell first, then the pieces that lock against it, the last piece being slightly bowed or pressed in so that it springs tight (the tension holds the whole cell).

| Pattern | Japanese | Ground | Character |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hemp leaf | 麻の葉 *asanoha* | Triangular | Six-pointed star; the most common; a traditional protective motif |
| Sesame | 胡麻 *goma* | Square (or triangular variants) | Criss-cross fine pattern |
| Cherry | 桜 *sakura* | Triangular / hexagonal | Five- or six-petalled flower forms |
| Gentian | 竜胆 *rindō* | Triangular | Flower of the gentian |
| Seven treasures | 七宝 *shippō* | Square | Interlocking-circle motif rendered in straight or (in some work) bent strips (**flagged**) |
| Tortoise shell | 亀甲 *kikkō* | Hexagonal | Hexagon grid, often with inset flowers |
| Square-hemp | 角麻の葉 *kaku-asanoha* | Square | Asanoha adapted to a square cell |

### 8.4 Fitting rules
**R14-072** — Kumiko **must** be fitted without glue or nails; all joints are friction fits cut to exact dimension. *Why:* glue-free kumiko can be disassembled for repair and does not stain; the lattice gains strength from its geometry and tension.

**R14-073** — All strips of one family in a jigumi **must** be notched together as a bundle (or with an indexed jig), never marked and cut individually. *Why:* cumulative spacing errors make the lattice skew and pattern pieces unfit.

**R14-074** — Kumiko 見付 (face width) **should** be proportioned to cell size: coarse grids (荒組) take a relatively wider 見付, fine patterns (asanoha in ranma) a thinner one (c. 1–2 mm for fine ranma pieces, 6–9 mm for shōji jigumi, representative). The depth (見込) **should** be at least about twice the 見付 for shōji kumiko. *Why:* visual balance; a deep, thin strip is stiff in the plane of the screen and resists warping.

**R14-075** — Kumiko joints **should** be cut marginally tight and assembled with a light compression of the fibres (木殺し *kigoroshi*, → Ch. 06) where needed, so that humidity swells them tight. *Why:* dry-season shrinkage would otherwise loosen the lattice.

**R14-076** — Pattern pieces **must** be cut using fixed angle jigs, and all pieces of one type **must** be cut against the same jig setting in one session. *Why:* angle accuracy of a fraction of a degree is required; re-setting between sessions introduces mismatch.

## 9. Tategu joinery details (建具の仕口)

### 9.1 Kamachi-gumi (框組) — frame corners
- **Class:** hozo (tenon) joints of frames.
- **Typical use:** shōji, fusuma cores, itado frames, glazed doors, sankarado.
- **Load behaviour:** racking resistance of the whole panel; the joint resists rotation by tenon cheek bearing and wedging.
- **Proportions (representative):** tenon thickness ≈ 1/3 of the 見込 (thickness) of the members; for wide rails, the tenon is divided into two (二枚枘 *nimai-hozo*, double tenon, side by side in the thickness) or into two stacked tenons across the width (to reduce the mortise and avoid shrinkage problems); a short 小根 (*kone*, haunch) fills the groove at the edge.
- **Orientation rules:** the **stile (竪框)** is the female (mortised) member and runs through at the corner; the **rails (桟)** are male (tenoned). Stiles are left long (**角** *tsuno*, horns) beyond the rails during assembly, to prevent splitting when wedging, and cut flush afterwards.
- **Joint face at the corner:**
  - **胴付き** (*dōtsuki*, square shoulder) — the rail shoulder butts square against the stile face; used where the members are plain.
  - **面腰** (*mengoshi*) — when the inner edges are moulded or chamfered (面), the shoulder is cut so that the moulding meets in a mitre (面腰 = "moulding-waist") while the rest of the shoulder is square.
  - **留め形** (*tomegata*) — the full face is mitred (as in fusuma fuchi, picture frames), with the tenon hidden inside.
- **Through or blind:**
  - **Through tenon, wedged (通し枘 割楔 *tōshi-hozo wari-kusabi*)** — the tenon passes through the stile and is expanded by wedges driven into saw kerfs from the outside, the mortise being flared slightly on the outside; strongest; the end grain shows on the stile edge (hidden in the groove or accepted in shōji).
  - **Blind tenon (止め枘 *tome-hozo*)** — does not show.
  - **Fox-wedged blind tenon (地獄枘 *jigoku-hozo*)** — blind tenon with wedges pre-set in its kerfs; as the tenon is driven home the wedges bottom out and spread it in a dovetailed mortise; cannot be withdrawn ("hell tenon"). Used where both faces and edge must be clean and the joint will never be taken apart (→ Ch. 08, Ch. 09).
- **Cutting sequence:** (1) dimension and square all stock; (2) mark with 罫引き (*kebiki*, marking gauge) from the face side (表 *omote*) only; (3) cut mortises (through from both faces, meeting in the middle, to avoid breakout); (4) saw tenon cheeks and shoulders; (5) cut mouldings and 面腰; (6) dry-fit; (7) cut kumiko mortises/tenons and fit lattice; (8) final assembly with wedges (glue only at wedges in traditional work, often none); (9) trim tsuno.
- **Rules:** below.
- **Common errors:** marking from both faces (gives a stepped frame); tenon too thick (splits stile); wedges driven parallel to the stile grain in a thin stile instead of perpendicular (splits the stile); cutting horns off before wedging.

**R14-077** — In tategu frames the stile **must** be the mortised member and **should** be left with horns (tsuno) until wedging is complete. *Why:* the stile runs full height and carries the sliding edges; horns prevent the end splitting under wedge pressure.

**R14-078** — Tategu wedges in through tenons **must** be driven so they spread the tenon *across the stile's length* (i.e. the wedge kerfs run parallel to the stile's face, wedging toward the ends of the mortise), never across the stile's thickness. *Why:* spreading across the thickness splits the thin stile along its grain.

**R14-079** — All marking on tategu **must** be referenced from one face (表) and one edge. *Why:* members of different thickness then still meet flush on the show face.

### 9.2 Panels in frames — kagami-ita (鏡板)
- A thin board panel (kagami-ita, "mirror board") sits in grooves (小穴 *koana*, c. 1/3 of the frame thickness wide) in stiles and rails; it is **not** glued, and is sized to leave clearance in the grooves (more across the grain than along it) to allow seasonal movement.
- Wide panels are made of boards edge-joined with **yatoi-zane** (雇い実, loose tongue) or **hon-zane**; in traditional work glue is used on edge joints (矧ぎ) of a panel but the panel floats in its frame.

**R14-080** — Kagami-ita **must** float in their grooves, unglued, with clearance across the grain. *Why:* R14-005; a restrained panel splits or breaks its frame.

### 9.3 Hashibami (端喰) — breadboard ends
- **Class:** end-cap / anti-warp member (反り止め *sori-dome*).
- **Typical use:** board doors (板戸), tokoita, tabletops, shelves, sankarado panels.
- **Construction:** a strip of wood the width of the board, with a groove (and often mortises), fitted over a tongue (and tenons) on the end of the board; pinned through the tenons, the pin holes in the tenons elongated (except at the centre) so the board can move across its width.
- **Load behaviour:** holds the board flat at its ends; resists cupping; hides end grain.

**R14-081** — Hashibami **must** be fixed to the board at the centre only (or by pins in elongated holes), not glued along the full width. *Why:* the board moves across its width; the hashibami does not.

### 9.4 Suitsuki-zan (吸付き桟) — sliding-dovetail battens
- **Class:** anti-warp batten (反り止め).
- **Typical use:** wide board doors, tokoita, ceiling panels in gōtenjō, 杉戸, table tops.
- **Construction:** a batten with a dovetail section (蟻形) slid into a matching dovetail housing (蟻溝 *ari-mizo*) cut across the back of the board. Both batten and housing are **tapered** along their length (c. 1–2 mm over the width of the board) so that the batten tightens as it is driven from the wide end; it is not glued, and in traditional work is fixed (by a pin or glue) only at one point, often the centre or the wide end, so that the board can move on it.
- **Proportions (representative):** batten width c. 1.5–2 × its depth; dovetail housing depth c. 1/3–1/2 of the board thickness; dovetail angle c. 1:5–1:6 (→ Ch. 08 for dovetail slopes).
- **Name:** "suitsuki" = "sucking on" — the batten holds the board as if by suction.

**R14-082** — Suitsuki-zan **must** be tapered and driven from the wide end, and **must not** be glued along their length. *Why:* the taper gives adjustable tightness (they can be re-driven as the board shrinks, → Ch. 16); gluing defeats the movement allowance.

### 9.5 Hozo in kumiko and small members
Kumiko ends enter the frame with **short tenons** (短枘 *tan-hozo*) into shallow mortises (or into a groove along the inner edge of the frame in cheaper work). In fine work each kumiko has an individual mortise; the tenon is cut to the full 見付 width and a reduced 見込, shouldered on the room side so the shoulder line is crisp.

**R14-083** — Kumiko **should** enter the frame by individual short tenons in individual mortises in quality work, not by a continuous groove. *Why:* individual mortises hold the kumiko against sideways slipping and keep the lattice square.

### 9.6 Ari (蟻) and jigoku-hozo in tategu
Dovetail joints in tategu appear in: tsurizuka-to-kamoi (§6.3, a carpentry joint), suitsuki-zan, and in some drawer/cabinet work of tokowaki cupboards (蟻組 *arigumi*, through or half-blind dovetails for box corners, → Ch. 08). Jigoku-hozo is used in tategu only where a joint must be invisible and permanent — its use conflicts with the principle that tategu be repairable, so it is used sparingly.

**R14-084** — Irreversible joints (jigoku-hozo, glued mitres) **should** be avoided in tategu except where no alternative exists. *Why:* tategu are expected to be dismantled and repaired repeatedly over the life of the building (→ Ch. 16).
