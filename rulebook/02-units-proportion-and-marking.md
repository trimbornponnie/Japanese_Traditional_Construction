# Chapter 02 — Units, Modules, Proportion & Marking (尺貫法・間・木割・墨付け / *shakkan-hō, ken, kiwari, sumitsuke*)

## Scope

This chapter covers everything that has to be settled **before any timber is cut**:

- the traditional units of length, area and volume;
- the planning modules (the *ken* and the tatami) and the two ways of laying out a building on them;
- the proportion system (*kiwari*) that derives member sizes from a base module, including the rafter-spacing module of temple architecture;
- the marking tools (carpenter's square, ink line, bamboo pen, marking gauge) and how to use them;
- the reference lines and marking symbols by which a carpenter tells the cutter what to do;
- the grid-numbering system (*bantsuke*) that tells the erector where each member goes;
- the story poles and plan boards that act as the controlling documents of a traditional job.

The geometry of roofs and hips (*kikujutsu*) is in → Ch. 05. Tools in general and their sharpening are in → Ch. 04. Timber orientation (heart/bark side, butt/top) is in → Ch. 03. Bracket-set proportions are in → Ch. 12.

## 1. Traditional units (尺貫法 *shakkan-hō*)

### 1.1 The length units

The Japanese traditional system of measures is called **shakkan-hō** (尺貫法), after its base units of length (*shaku*) and weight (*kan*). Carpenters work in a decimal series of length units built on the **carpenter's foot** (曲尺 / 矩尺, *kanejaku*):

| Unit | Kanji | Relation | Metric (kanejaku, since 1891) | Typical carpentry use |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| mō | 毛 | 1/10 rin | 0.0303 mm | Fine fitting; "hair" |
| rin | 厘 | 1/10 bu | 0.303 mm | Line width; fit allowances |
| bu | 分 | 1/10 sun | 3.030 mm | Chamfers, reveals (*chiri*), tenon shoulders |
| sun | 寸 | 1/10 shaku | 30.30 mm | Member sections (e.g. 4-sun post) |
| shaku | 尺 | base | 303.03 mm | Lengths, heights |
| ken | 間 | 6 shaku (legal, from the Meiji era) | 1818.2 mm | Bay, planning grid (→ §2) |
| jō | 丈 | 10 shaku | 3.030 m | Long lengths, ceiling heights in old texts |
| chō | 町 | 60 ken | 109.09 m | Site distances |
| ri | 里 | 36 chō | 3.927 km | Travel distances |

Notes:
- The system is **decimal from mō to shaku**. Ken, chō and ri are not decimal multiples.
- **分 (bu) has a second meaning in proportion texts: "tenths of the reference"** (→ §3.2, → R01-015). Always check which meaning is intended.
- Old texts and some modern carpenters write dimensions as, e.g., 「三寸五分」 (3 sun 5 bu = 3.5 sun ≈ 106 mm) or in shorthand 「3.5寸」, 「寸五」 (*sungo*, 1.5 sun), 「尺二」 (*shakuni*, 1.2 shaku).

### 1.2 Kanejaku and kujirajaku

Two different "feet" survived into modern times:

- **Kanejaku** (曲尺 / 矩尺, "square foot", the foot of the carpenter's square). This is the carpenter's and builder's unit. 1 shaku = 10/33 m ≈ 303.03 mm.
- **Kujirajaku** (鯨尺, "whale foot"). The name comes from the whalebone rulers once used by tailors. It is the measure for **cloth and kimono making** (和裁 *wasai*). 1 kujira-shaku = 1.25 kanejaku = 25/66 m ≈ 378.79 mm, and 1 kujira-sun ≈ 37.88 mm.
- Other historical cloth measures existed, e.g. the *gofuku-jaku* (呉服尺), about 1.2 kanejaku. They matter only for fabric-covered fittings (e.g. fusuma paper widths, noren) and for reading old inventories.

**R02-001** — In building work, *shaku* always means **kanejaku** unless the source explicitly says otherwise. Never apply kujira measures to timber. *(Must)*
*Why:* A 25 % error. Confusion arises mainly when reading household documents, and when fabric or paper widths for fittings are specified in kujira.

### 1.3 The historical length of the shaku

The shaku has **not been constant**. Its length drifted over the centuries and varied between regions and workshops:

- Nara-period (8th c.) buildings are usually found to have been laid out in a *shaku* somewhat **shorter** than the modern one, typically around 29.5–29.8 cm. The so-called Tang great foot (唐大尺) and Tenpyō foot (天平尺) are examples.
- A longer "Koma foot" (高麗尺 *Koma-jaku*), about 1.2 times the Tang foot (≈35 cm), has been proposed for some Asuka-period buildings. Its use is debated among historians.
- In the Edo period several "carpenter's feet" were in use, e.g. the *Kyōhō-jaku* (享保尺) and the *Matashirō-jaku* (又四郎尺). They differed by fractions of a percent. The Meiji standard foot was based on the *setchū-jaku* (折衷尺, "compromise foot"), an average of these two that had been used by the surveyor Inō Tadataka. [P]
- The **Weights and Measures Act of 1891** (度量衡法, Meiji 24, in force from 1893) fixed **1 shaku = 10/33 m** (so 1 m = 3.3 shaku exactly) and 1 kan = 3.75 kg.

This matters whenever an old building is surveyed or repaired. Conservation surveys therefore determine the **building's own foot** (造営尺 *zōei-shaku*, "construction foot") from its measured bays before interpreting any dimension.

**R02-002** — When surveying, repairing or copying a pre-Meiji building, **first derive its construction foot (zōei-shaku)** by dividing its principal measured bays by plausible whole-number shaku values, and express all its dimensions in that foot. Do not assume 303.03 mm. *(Must, for conservation)*
*Why:* An 8th-century bay of "10 shaku" may measure about 2.97 m, not 3.03 m. Reading it in modern shaku (9.8 shaku) hides the design intention and gives false fractional dimensions to every derived member.

### 1.4 Metrication and the continuing use of shaku

- The **Measurement Law** (計量法, 1951) made the metric system the only legal system for trade and certification. Use of shakkan units in transactions was prohibited from 1959. Land and buildings had a transition period that ended in **1966** (formally 31 March 1966) [P for exact dates]. After that, rulers and squares graduated in shaku could not legally be manufactured or sold.
- Carpenters, tatami makers and kimono makers, whose whole practice is built on shaku modules, resisted. A public campaign led by the broadcaster and writer 永六輔 (Ei Rokusuke) resulted in a **1977** ruling of the Measurement Administration Council. Instruments with **"shaku-equivalent graduations"** (尺相当目盛り *shaku-sōtō memori*) are treated as metric instruments and may be sold. Their graduations are spaced at shaku intervals, but they are formally labelled as metric fractions, typically **"1/33 m"**.
- So a modern Japanese carpenter's square may be marked in centimetres on one face and in "1/33 m" units (i.e. sun) on another. Squares with pure shaku markings are old stock, heirlooms, or sold under the shaku-equivalent rule.

In practice today:
- Many **timber sections are still thought in sun**, but supplied in round millimetres: 105 mm square (≈ 3.5 sun), 120 mm (≈ 4 sun), 150 mm (≈ 5 sun).
- **Lengths** come in metres that approximate shaku: 3 m (≈ 10 shaku), 4 m (≈ 13 shaku), 6 m (≈ 20 shaku).
- House plans are drawn on a **910 mm module** (3 shaku, *sanshaku*, the half-ken; 909.09 mm rounded) or on a "metre module" (1000 mm). Traditional hand-cut work stays in sun and shaku.

| Nominal (sun) | Exact (mm) | Common metric stock (mm) | Difference |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3.0 | 90.9 | 90 | −0.9 |
| 3.5 | 106.1 | 105 | −1.1 |
| 4.0 | 121.2 | 120 | −1.2 |
| 4.5 | 136.4 | 135 | −1.4 |
| 5.0 | 151.5 | 150 | −1.5 |
| 6.0 | 181.8 | 180 | −1.8 |

**R02-003** — **Choose one unit system per project and use it for all marking.** If the design is in shaku, set out every rod, gauge and square in shaku (or 1/33 m). If it is in millimetres, do the same in millimetres. Convert only at the interface (drawings for permits, orders to the timber yard). *(Must)*
*Why:* Rounding differences (e.g. 4 sun = 121.2 mm vs 120 mm stock) accumulate over a building. A 1.2 mm discrepancy per post width, repeated across mortise and tenon, beam seat and keta housing, becomes visible misfit.

**R02-004** — When metric stock is used for a shaku-module design, **mark joints from the actual measured member (centre lines, → §6), not from the nominal sun size.** *(Must)*
*Why:* A "4-sun" post is actually 120 mm. Joint positions derived from the nominal 121.2 mm would be off-centre by 0.6 mm per side.

### 1.5 Area and volume units

| Unit | Kanji | Definition | Metric | Use |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| tsubo | 坪 | 1 ken × 1 ken (6 × 6 shaku) | 3.3058 m² | Floor area of buildings and land |
| jō | 畳 | one tatami mat | ≈ 1.62 m² (Edo-ma) to ≈ 1.82 m² (kyō-ma) | Room size ("6-jō room") |
| se | 畝 | 30 tsubo | 99.17 m² | Land |
| tan | 反 | 300 tsubo | 991.7 m² | Land |
| koku (timber) | 石 | 10 cubic shaku (e.g. 1 × 1 × 10 shaku) | 0.2783 m³ | Timber volume (historical) |
| sai | 才 | 1 sun × 1 sun × 12 shaku (= 0.12 cubic shaku) | ≈ 0.00334 m³ | Timber trade; 1 m³ ≈ 300 sai |

- "Room size in jō" is an area count, not a dimension, and its real area depends on the tatami system (→ §2).
- The **sai** is still used in the Japanese timber trade. Regional and product-specific variants exist (for boards, for example), so check the local definition when ordering [P].

## 2. The ken, the tatami, and the two planning systems

### 2.1 From "bay" to "unit"

Originally 間 (*ken* / *ma*) did not denote a fixed length. It meant **the space between two columns: a bay**. A "three-ken hall" (三間堂) was a hall three bays wide, whatever the bays measured. In ancient temples bays of 10 shaku or more were normal, and bays within one building differed.

In residential architecture from the medieval period onward, bays became standardised, and *ken* came to mean a **length**. That length varied by region and period:

| Ken value | Name | Where / when | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| ≈ 7 shaku and more | — | Medieval residences | Bays gradually shrinking [P] |
| 6.5 shaku (≈ 1970 mm) | 京間 *kyōma* ("Kyoto ken") | Kyoto and western Japan, from the Momoyama/Edo period | Centre-to-centre usage |
| 6.3 shaku (≈ 1909 mm) | — | Toyotomi Hideyoshi's land survey (太閤検地 *Taikō kenchi*, 1580s–1590s) used a 6.3-shaku ken for measuring land | A land-survey value. Its connection with the kyō-datami length (6.3 shaku) is often noted |
| 6.0 shaku (≈ 1818 mm) | 田舎間 *inakama* ("country ken"), 江戸間 *Edoma*, 関東間 *Kantōma* | Edo (Tokyo) and eastern Japan. Later Edo-period land surveys used 6 shaku [P] | Became the **legal ken (6 shaku) in the Meiji era** |

Today, unless qualified, **1 ken = 6 shaku = 1818 mm** (in modern plans usually 1820 mm, with a half-ken of 910 mm).

### 2.2 Tatami sizes

A tatami mat has a length:width ratio of **2:1** and a thickness of about 5–6 cm (traditional straw core). There are four principal sizes:

| System | Also called | Region | Mat size (shaku) | Mat size (mm, approx.) | Planning basis |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Kyō-ma** | 京間, 本間 *honma* | Kansai, western Japan | 6.3 × 3.15 | 1909 × 955 (often 1910 × 955) | **Tatami-wari** (inner clear dimension) |
| **Chūkyō-ma** | 中京間, 三六間 *sanroku-ma* | Tōkai (Nagoya region); parts of Tōhoku, Hokuriku | 6.0 × 3.0 | 1818 × 909 (often 1820 × 910) | Usually tatami-wari |
| **Edo-ma** | 江戸間, 田舎間, 関東間, 五八間 *gohachi-ma* | Kantō, eastern and northern Japan; most modern houses | 5.8 × 2.9 (nominal) | 1757 × 879 (often 1760 × 880) | **Hashira-wari / shinshin-sei** (centre-to-centre) |
| **Danchi-ma** | 団地間, 五六間 *goroku-ma* | Post-war public housing (*danchi*) and apartments | ≈ 5.6 × 2.8 (varies) | ≈ 1700 × 850 (varies) | Mats cut to fit whatever room exists |

Note that "Edo-ma 5.8 × 2.9" is **nominal**. Under centre-to-centre planning every room's mats are made to measure (→ §2.3), so actual Edo-ma mats vary with the column size and the room.

### 2.3 The two planning systems

There are two fundamentally different ways of relating the structural grid to the rooms.

#### (a) Centre-to-centre planning (真々制 *shinshin-sei*; 柱割 *hashira-wari*)

- **What is fixed:** the **column centre lines**. They lie on a regular grid of whole or half ken (e.g. 6 shaku / 3 shaku; today 1820 / 910 mm).
- **What follows:** the **clear room dimension** = grid dimension − column width (half a column on each side). Tatami are made to the measured clear dimensions of each room after the frame is up.
- **Consequences:**
  - The structural members are **standardised**: a 2-ken beam is always 12 shaku between centres, whatever the column size. Beams, keta, sills, nuki and floor joists come in repeatable lengths. This suits mass production and the metric 910 module, which is why Edo-ma dominates modern practice.
  - **Rooms shrink as columns grow.** An 8-mat room with 4-sun columns is smaller than one with 3.5-sun columns.
  - **Tatami and fittings are not interchangeable** between rooms or houses. Each mat is cut for its place and must be labelled (→ §9.6).

#### (b) Clear-dimension planning (内法制 *uchinori-sei*; 畳割 *tatami-wari*)

- **What is fixed:** the **tatami** (and thus the clear room dimension). The room is an exact whole number of standard mats.
- **What follows:** the **column centre distance** = sum of the mat dimensions + column width. The column grid therefore **varies** with column size and with the number of mats a bay spans.
- **Consequences:**
  - **Tatami, shōji, fusuma and other fittings are interchangeable.** A standard kyō-datami fits any Kansai room of the right mat count. Historically this made a market in second-hand fittings possible, and it allowed tenants in Kyoto and Osaka to rent houses without fittings and bring their own (the *hadaka-gashi* 裸貸し practice) [P].
  - **Structural member lengths are not uniform.** Every bay must be computed and the rods marked accordingly. There is more layout work, and less room for prefabrication.
  - **Larger columns do not shrink rooms**; they widen the grid instead.

#### Worked comparison: an eight-mat room (八畳)

An 8-mat room is square: 2 mat-lengths by 2 mat-lengths, i.e. 2 mat-lengths × 4 mat-widths, whatever the laying pattern.

**Edo-ma, centre-to-centre, 6-shaku ken, columns 3.5 sun (≈106 mm):**
- Grid: 2 ken × 2 ken = 12.0 × 12.0 shaku (3636 × 3636 mm) between centres.
- Clear room: 12.0 − 0.35 = 11.65 shaku (≈ 3530 mm) each way.
- Mats: 11.65 / 2 = 5.825 shaku long × 2.91 shaku wide, which is the "5.8 × 2.9" of the nominal Edo-ma mat.

**Kyō-ma, clear dimension, kyō-datami 6.3 × 3.15, columns 4 sun (≈121 mm):**
- Clear room: 2 × 6.3 = 12.6 shaku (≈ 3818 mm) each way.
- Centre distance: 12.6 + 0.4 = 13.0 shaku (≈ 3939 mm), i.e. **2 × 6.5 shaku**. This is the arithmetical link between the 6.3-shaku kyō-datami and the 6.5-shaku kyō-ma ken.
- The same columns across a 1.5-mat-length span give 9.45 + 0.4 = 9.85 shaku, **not** 1.5 × 6.5 = 9.75. The grid is not regular.

**Chūkyō-ma, clear dimension, mats 6.0 × 3.0, columns 4 sun:**
- Clear room: 12.0 shaku. Centres: 12.4 shaku (≈ 3758 mm).

```
   CENTRE-TO-CENTRE (Edo-ma)             CLEAR-DIMENSION (Kyō-ma)
   grid fixed, room = grid - column      room fixed, grid = room + column

   |<------ 12.0 shaku c/c ------>|      |<------- 13.0 shaku c/c ------->|
   +--+-------------------------+--+    +--+--------------------------+--+
   |  |<--- 11.65 clear ------->|  |    |  |<---- 12.6 clear -------->|  |
   |  |  mats cut to fit        |  |    |  |  standard 6.3 x 3.15 mats|  |
   +--+-------------------------+--+    +--+--------------------------+--+
   col 3.5 sun             col 3.5 sun  col 4 sun                  col 4 sun
```

### 2.4 Dual meanings to watch

- **内法 *uchinori*** means (1) the **clear dimension** between two members (as in *uchinori-sei*), and (2) specifically the **clear height from the top of the sill (敷居) to the underside of the lintel (鴨居)**, the "door height" (内法高 *uchinori-daka*). The latter is traditionally about **5.7–5.8 shaku** (≈ 1730–1760 mm) and in modern houses often 1800 mm or more.
- **京間 *kyōma*** can mean the 6.5-shaku ken (a centre-to-centre measure) or the kyō-datami system (a clear-dimension measure).

**R02-005** — **Decide and record the planning system (shinshin-sei or tatami-wari) and the ken or mat size before any drawing or rod is made.** Write it on the itazu (→ §10). *(Must)*
*Why:* The two systems give different grids for the same room. A frame cut on one assumption and fitted with fittings of the other will not close.

**R02-006** — In **centre-to-centre** planning, all plan dimensions on the itazu and rods are **centre-to-centre**. Member lengths are derived from the grid. Tatami and fittings are measured from the finished frame, never pre-made. *(Must)*

**R02-007** — In **tatami-wari** planning, **fix the column width first**. Then compute every centre distance as (sum of mat dimensions + column width) and mark each bay individually on the rods. *(Must)*
*Why:* Changing the column size after the rods are made changes every grid dimension in the building.

**R02-008** — Never write "1 ken" in a specification without stating its value (e.g. "1 ken = 6.0 shaku c/c" or "kyō-datami 6.3 × 3.15, tatami-wari"). *(Must)*

**R02-009** — When **mixing traditional and modern work** (e.g. a new wing on a Kansai house), carry the original planning system and mat size into the extension unless there is a deliberate break (a corridor or a different room type) that hides the change. *(Should)*
*Why:* Fittings and mats are expected to match across adjacent rooms. A change of system inside a suite of rooms leaves non-standard mats and misaligned fusuma.

## 3. Kiwari: the proportion system

### 3.1 Definition

**Kiwari** (木割, "dividing the timber"; older 木砕 *kikudaki*) is the traditional system of determining the **cross-sectional size and position of every member as a proportion of a base module**. It was recorded in the kiwari books (→ Ch. 01 §2). A kiwari:

- starts from a **base module**, which is one of:
  - the **column width or diameter** (柱割 in the sense of "column-based"). This is typical of residential architecture (shoin, sukiya).
  - the **bay span**, especially the central bay (中の間). This is typical of early temple architecture.
  - the **rafter spacing** (枝 *shi*; 枝割 *shiwari*). This is typical of temple and shrine architecture from the medieval period onward (→ §3.4).
- derives the **first-order members** (e.g. lintels, tie-beams, bracket blocks) as fractions or multiples of the module;
- derives **second-order members** from first-order ones, and so on.

The result is a building whose parts are **mutually proportioned**: a large hall has correspondingly heavier lintels, brackets and rafters, and a small tea room is correspondingly finer throughout. It is also a **communication system**. With the base module and the school known, a carpenter could reconstruct the whole building from a few numbers.

### 3.2 Notation

In kiwari texts (→ Ch. 01 §8.2):

- **〜割 (*wari*)** = tenths of the reference. 「中の間の一割」 = 0.1 × central bay.
- **〜分 (*bu*), 〜厘 (*rin*)** = tenths and hundredths **of the reference**. 「柱の八分」 = 0.8 × column. 「柱の三分五厘」 = 0.35 × column.
- Absolute dimensions (寸, 尺) appear mainly in worked examples.

**R02-010** — **Write every kiwari rule as an explicit ratio to a named reference** (e.g. "kamoi face height = 0.40 × column width C") and compute absolute sizes only at the end. *(Must)* (→ R01-015)

### 3.3 Representative residential kiwari (column-based)

The table gives **representative ranges** found in shoin/sukiya kiwari as reported in modern handbooks and in surveys. The ratios **vary by school, period and building rank**. Sukiya work in particular deliberately departs from strict kiwari for lightness. Do not treat any single value as normative (→ R01-023).

Base: **C = column width** (square post; e.g. 4 sun ≈ 121 mm in a formal room, 3.5 sun ≈ 106 mm in an ordinary room, smaller in sukiya).

| Member | Japanese | Dimension governed | Representative ratio to C | For C = 4 sun |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Lintel-level decorative rail | 内法長押 *uchinori-nageshi* | face height (成/見付) | ≈ 0.8–0.9 C (formal shoin); smaller or omitted in sukiya | ≈ 3.2–3.6 sun |
| Ceiling-level rail | 天井長押 *tenjō-nageshi* | face height | smaller than uchinori-nageshi, ≈ 0.6–0.8 C | ≈ 2.4–3.2 sun |
| Lintel (grooved head) | 鴨居 *kamoi* | face height | ≈ 0.35–0.45 C | ≈ 1.4–1.8 sun |
| Sill (grooved) | 敷居 *shikii* | thickness | ≈ 0.4–0.5 C | ≈ 1.6–2.0 sun |
| Kamoi / shikii width | 見込 *mikomi* | depth into wall | C minus the reveals (*chiri*) on both faces | e.g. 4 sun − 2 × (2–3 bu) |
| Ceiling cornice | 回縁 *mawaribuchi* | face height | ≈ 0.5–0.7 C | ≈ 2.0–2.8 sun |
| Tatami edge strip | 畳寄 *tatamiyose* | face height | ≈ 0.3–0.4 C | ≈ 1.2–1.6 sun |

Heights:
- **Door height (内法高)**: about 5.7–5.8 shaku traditionally, fixed by custom rather than by C.
- **Ceiling height:** a frequently quoted rule of thumb scales it with room size, e.g. *ceiling height ≈ door height + 0.3 shaku × number of mats*. An 8-mat room then gets about 5.7 + 2.4 = 8.1 shaku (≈ 2.45 m). This is **one common reading**; it is not universal.

Worked example: a formal 8-mat room with 4-sun columns, taking mid-range values:
- nageshi 0.85 × 4.0 = 3.4 sun (103 mm);
- kamoi face 0.4 × 4.0 = 1.6 sun (48 mm);
- shikii thickness 0.45 × 4.0 = 1.8 sun (55 mm);
- mawaribuchi 0.6 × 4.0 = 2.4 sun (73 mm);
- door height 5.75 shaku (1742 mm);
- ceiling ≈ 5.75 + 0.3 × 8 = 8.15 shaku (≈ 2470 mm).

**R02-011** — In residential kiwari, **fix the column width C first and derive the room members from it**. If C changes, re-derive everything. *(Should)*
*Why:* This keeps a room internally proportioned. A heavy nageshi on a slender post, or a thin kamoi on a heavy post, looks wrong even to untrained eyes.

**R02-012** — **Reveals (散り *chiri*) must be consistent within a room.** The set-back of kamoi, shikii, nageshi and wall surfaces from the column face should follow one chosen value (commonly a few bu) throughout. *(Should)*
*Why:* The reveal is the visual line that makes the column read as the dominant member. Inconsistent reveals are the most visible symptom of careless kiwari (→ Ch. 14).

### 3.4 Shiwari (枝割): the rafter-spacing module

In temple and shrine architecture with closely spaced exposed rafters (繁垂木 *shige-daruki*), the controlling module from the medieval period onward is the **rafter spacing**.

- **1 shi (一枝)** = **one rafter width + one gap** (the gap is 小間 *koma*). Equivalently, the centre-to-centre distance of adjacent rafters.
- The gap is typically **equal to or somewhat larger than the rafter width**. Ratios of roughly 1:1 to 1:1.5 (rafter:gap) are found, varying by period, school and building [representative].
- **Bays are set as whole numbers of shi.** The central bay might be 20 shi and the side bays 16 shi. This guarantees that rafters fall in the same relation to every column line.
- **Eaves projection** and other horizontal dimensions are also often counted in shi.

#### Six-rafter bracket rule (六枝掛 *rokushi-gake*)

In a **three-bearing-block bracket set** (三斗 *mitsudo*; → Ch. 12), three small bearing blocks (巻斗 *makito*) sit on the bracket arm (肘木 *hijiki*). In *rokushi-gake*, which originated in the Kamakura period and later became the norm of wayō kiwari:

- each makito is exactly as wide as **two rafters plus the gap between them** (= 1 shi + 1 rafter width);
- the edges of the three makito line up with the edges of the rafters above;
- so the bracket set "carries" **six rafters**, and its overall width is **5 shi + 1 rafter width**;
- the column centre line falls on the **centre of a gap** (小間真), between the two rafters over the central makito.

The bracket parts are therefore **dimensioned from the rafters**, and the rafters from the bay. The whole front of the building becomes one interlocking module.

```
  rafters (cross-section)   R = rafter width, g = gap, 1 shi = R + g
  ..[R]g[R]g[R]g[R]g[R]g[R]..
    |<-mkt->|  |<-mkt->|  |<-mkt->|     makito width = 2R + g = shi + R
    [ makito]  [ makito]  [ makito]
    [=========== hijiki ===========]
               [  daito  ]              column centre = centre of the
                 |     |                middle gap (between rafters 3 and 4)
                 | col |
  overall bracket width = 6R + 5g = 5 shi + R
```

#### Worked example (illustrative, not from a specific text)

Take rafter width R = 2.2 sun and gap g = 2.8 sun (ratio ≈ 1:1.27). Then **1 shi = 5.0 sun** (≈ 152 mm).

| Item | Rule | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Central bay (中の間) | 20 shi | 100 sun = 10.0 shaku (3030 mm) |
| Side bays (脇の間) | 16 shi | 80 sun = 8.0 shaku (2424 mm) |
| Makito width | shi + R | 7.2 sun (218 mm) |
| Three-block set overall width | 5 shi + R | 27.2 sun (824 mm) |
| Clear gap between adjacent bracket sets (central bay) | bay − set width | 100 − 27.2 = 72.8 sun |
| Column diameter (if taken as ≈ 1/10 of central bay) | 0.1 × 100 sun | 10 sun = 1.0 shaku (303 mm) [representative] |

Checks:
- the next column is 20 shi away, so it also falls at a gap centre;
- the bracket set is symmetric about the column;
- if the rafter or gap size changes, **every** dimension above changes.

This is why in shiwari buildings the **rafter module must be fixed before anything else is drawn**.

Buildings with widely spaced rafters (疎垂木 *mabara-daruki*, e.g. many houses, shoin, sukiya, and some Zen-style or small buildings) do not use shiwari. Their rafter spacing is simply a division of the bay (e.g. rafters at about 1.5 shaku centres, or 4 per ken).

**R02-013** — In buildings with close-spaced rafters, **fix the rafter width and gap (the shi) first, set every bay and the eaves projection as whole numbers of shi, and derive bracket parts from the shi.** *(Must, for shiwari buildings)*
*Why:* If bays are not integer multiples of shi, rafters land differently over each column and the bracket blocks cannot align with them. This is a fault that cannot be corrected after cutting.

**R02-014** — Under *rokushi-gake*, **the column centre line falls on the centre of a gap, not on a rafter.** Check this at every column on the itazu before marking the keta. *(Must, for rokushi-gake)*

**R02-015** — **Corner bays and hip areas need special resolution** (e.g. adjusted rafter spacing near the corner, fan rafters). Resolve them by kikujutsu (→ Ch. 05) and the building's school (→ Ch. 12); do not force the regular shi into the corner. *(Should)*

### 3.5 Kiwari and structure

Kiwari gives members that look right and, for the building types and spans it was developed for, are generally adequate. It is **not** a structural check (→ R01-004, R01-024). Two cases in particular need independent checking:

- scaling up beyond the usual spans;
- using weaker or smaller-dimension timber than the tradition assumed.

**R02-016** — **Use kiwari to proportion, then check structurally.** Where the check requires a larger member than kiwari gives, increase the hidden dimension first (depth of a concealed beam, a hidden reinforcing member) before changing a visible, kiwari-controlled dimension. *(Should)*

## 4. The carpenter's square (曲尺 *sashigane*)

### 4.1 Anatomy

The **sashigane** (差し金; also *kanejaku* 曲尺 / 矩尺, *magarijaku*) is a thin, flexible steel L-square. It is the primary layout tool of Japanese carpentry, and the basis of kikujutsu (→ Ch. 05).

- **Long arm** (長手 *nagate*): traditionally about 1.5 shaku; modern metric versions are commonly 50 cm.
- **Short arm** (妻手 *tsumate*, also 短手): about half the long arm.
- **Width:** typically about **5 bu (≈ 15 mm)**. The width itself is used as a gauge for marking 5-bu offsets and parallel lines.
- **Thickness:** thin (about 1 mm or less) and slightly flexible, so that it can be sprung to a curve or pressed flat on a rough surface.
- **Outside corner** (外角) and **inside corner** (内角 / 入隅).
- **Blade profile:** many sashigane have a shallow groove along the centre of the arms, which stiffens the blade and helps lift it from a flat surface.

### 4.2 Scales

A traditional sashigane carries graduations on both faces (arrangements vary by maker):

**Front (表 *omote*), "front scale" (表目 *omote-me*):**
- on the **outer edge** of each arm, measured from the outer corner (外目 *sotome*);
- on the **inner edge**, measured from the inner corner (内目 *uchime*), for reading inside dimensions, e.g. the depth of a mortise or the inside of a frame.
- Units are sun and bu, or "1/33 m" (→ §1.4), or mm on metric squares.

**Back (裏 *ura*), "back scale" (裏目 *ura-me*):**
- **Kakume** (角目, "square scale"): graduations spaced at **√2 × the front unit**. One kakume "sun" = 1.414 sun ≈ 42.85 mm. It is usually on the outer edge of the long arm.
- **Marume** (丸目, "round scale"): graduations spaced at **1/π × the front unit**. One marume "sun" = 0.318 sun ≈ 9.65 mm. It is usually on the short arm.
- The inner edges often repeat the front scale, measured from the inner corner.

### 4.3 What the back scales do

**Kakume (√2):**

1. **Largest square timber from a log.** Measure the **small-end diameter** (inside the bark, the least diameter; → Ch. 03) with the kakume. The number read is the **side of the largest square that can be cut** (side = D/√2). For example, a log whose small end measures 6 kakume-sun (≈ 257 mm actual) will yield a 6-sun square.
2. **Diagonal of a square.** Laying off *a* kakume units gives a length of *a*√2 front units: the diagonal of a square of side *a*. This gives the **plan length of a 45° hip or valley** for a given run directly, without calculation (→ Ch. 05).
3. **Conversely**, measuring a diagonal on the kakume gives the side of the square.

**Marume (1/π):**

1. **Circumference from diameter.** Measure the **diameter** of a round member with the marume. The number read is its **circumference** in front units. For example, a round column 1 shaku in diameter reads about 31.4 on the marume, i.e. its circumference is about 3.14 shaku. This is used for ordering wrapping materials and bands, and for dividing a circle (e.g. laying out the facets of an octagonal post before rounding).
2. **Diameter from circumference.** Measure the circumference with a tape in front units, find that number on the marume, and the front-scale length to it is the diameter.

```
   FRONT FACE (omote-me)                      BACK FACE (ura-me)
   tsumate                                    tsumate
   +---+ 5                                    +---+ marume: 1 unit = 1/pi sun
   |   | 4                                    |   |   (~9.65 mm)
   |   | 3   outer edge: sotome               |   |
   |   | 2   inner edge: uchime               |   |
   |   | 1                                    |   |
   |   +--------------------------------+     |   +--------------------------------+
   |  0  1  2  3  4  5  6 ... 15 sun    |     |  0    1    2    3 ... kakume      |
   +------------------------------------+     +------------------------------------+
                nagate                        nagate: 1 kakume unit = sqrt(2) sun
                                                      (~42.85 mm)
   (schematic; the placement of scales on each edge varies by maker)
```

### 4.4 Basic operations

**(1) Squaring a line across a member.** Hold the inner edge of the tsumate hard against a **planed reference face** (never a rough face) and draw along the nagate, or vice versa. For lines around all four faces of a post, carry the line round from the same reference face each time.

**(2) Checking the square itself.** Draw a line along one arm against a straight reference edge. Flip the square over and draw again from the same point. If the two lines do not coincide, the square is out, by half the divergence.

**(3) Dividing a width into equal parts (tilting the rule).**
To divide a board of awkward width *W* into *n* equal parts:
1. Choose a round number *L* on the scale that is slightly larger than *W* and divisible by *n*.
2. Lay the square diagonally across the board with 0 on one edge and *L* on the other.
3. Mark at *L/n*, *2L/n*, and so on.
4. Repeat further along the board and join the marks.
Example: a board 107 mm wide into 3 parts. Set 0 and 120 on the edges and mark at 40 and 80. The same method finds the **centre of a board** (mark at *L/2*) without measuring its width.

```
   edge A ______________________________
          \  0
            \      . 40
              \          . 80
                \              . 120
   edge B _______\______________________
   (marks at 40 and 80 divide the width into three)
```

**(4) Laying out a slope (勾配 *kōbai*).**
Japanese slopes are stated as a **rise per 1 shaku (10 sun) of run**. "4-sun slope" (四寸勾配) means 4 in 10.
- On a **horizontal** reference edge: place the square so that the **10 mark of the nagate** and the **4 mark of the tsumate** both touch the edge. A line drawn along the nagate is the slope line; a line along the tsumate is perpendicular to it.
- On a **rafter** whose edge is already the slope: the same setting (10 on the nagate, 4 on the tsumate, both on the rafter edge) gives the **level cut** (水平 / 陸) along the nagate and the **plumb cut** (立水 *tachimizu*) along the tsumate.
- Hips, valleys, backing angles and all compound cuts are derived from this by kikujutsu (→ Ch. 05).

```
   reference edge (on a rafter: the sloping top edge)
   =======4==================================10=========
           \                              ___/
            \ tsumate                ___/   nagate
             \ (4 sun)          ___/        (10 sun = 1 shaku)
              \            ___/
               \      ___/
                \ ___/
                 +  <- corner of the square, lying on the face of the member

   The square lies flat on the face; the 4 mark of the tsumate and the
   10 mark of the nagate both touch the reference edge.
   On a horizontal board: line along the nagate = the 4-sun slope.
   On a rafter (edge = slope): line along the nagate = level cut,
                               line along the tsumate = plumb cut (tachimizu).
```

**(5) Parallel lines using the blade width.** Sliding the square along a reference edge with a pencil or sumisashi against the far side of the blade gives a line parallel to the edge at one blade width (≈ 5 bu). This is fast for chamfer lines and for reveals.

**(6) Large right angles on site (大矩 *ōgane*).** For site set-out, a large set-square is made from boards with sides in **3 : 4 : 5** proportion (e.g. 6, 8 and 10 shaku). It is used to square the batter boards (遣り方 *yarikata*) and the foundation lines (→ Ch. 15). The sashigane is too small for this.

**R02-017** — **Always square and measure from a planed reference face or from a snapped reference line**, never from a sawn, rough or waney surface. *(Must)*

**R02-018** — **Check the sashigane for square at the start of every marking job** (flip test, §4.4-2) and discard or correct a square that is out. *(Must)*
*Why:* A square out by 1 bu over the nagate throws every cross-cut line on a long member, and the error doubles across a mortise-and-tenon pair.

**R02-019** — Before reading a length, **confirm which face and which edge of the sashigane you are reading** (front outer, front inner, kakume, marume). Read back scales only for their intended purposes. *(Must)*
*Why:* Reading a kakume length as a front-scale length gives a 41 % error. It is a classic apprentice mistake.

**R02-020** — **State slopes in the sun-per-shaku convention** (e.g. 4-sun, 4.5-sun, 5-sun slope) on the itazu and rods, and lay them out with the sashigane at 10 : *k*, not with a protractor. *(Should)*
*Why:* The whole of kikujutsu is built on this convention. Degrees must be converted back, and the conversion introduces rounding.

## 5. Ink line, bamboo pen, marking gauge

### 5.1 The ink pot (墨壺 *sumitsubo*)

Parts:

| Part | Japanese | Function |
|---|---|---|
| Ink reservoir | 池 *ike* | Holds silk or cotton wadding (綿 *wata*) soaked with ink (墨汁 *bokujū*, or ink ground from an ink stick) |
| Reel | 糸車 *itoguruma* | Winds the line, with a crank handle |
| Line | 糸 *ito* | Traditionally fine silk; now usually a fine synthetic thread |
| End pin | 軽子 *karuko* | A small block with a needle, pushed into the wood at the start of the line |
| Guide hole | 壺口 *tsubo-guchi* | The hole through which the line leaves, straightening and wiping it |

The line passes from the reel through the wadding, so it is inked as it is pulled out.

Sumitsubo are often **carved from a single block of hardwood** (e.g. keyaki). Traditional forms (crane, turtle, heart-shaped *inome* motifs) were sometimes carved by the carpenter himself as a proof of skill. An apprentice's first sumitsubo was a mark of acceptance into the trade.

**Snapping a line (墨を打つ *sumi wo utsu*):**
1. Push the karuko needle into the start mark, exactly on the point.
2. Draw the line out through the wadding to the end mark and press it down with the thumb exactly on the point.
3. Lift the line with the other hand **perpendicular to the surface**, i.e. straight up, in the vertical plane through the two points.
4. Release. The line snaps down and leaves a fine straight ink line.
5. For long lines (more than about 2 ken), press the middle down and snap each half separately, or have a helper hold the middle.

On **round or irregular timber** (logs, bent beams), lifting the line **vertically** gives a line that lies in a vertical plane. Seen from above it is straight, although it follows the curve of the surface. This is exactly what is needed for the centre line (芯墨) and the crown reference (→ §6) on a log beam.

**R02-021** — **Snap lines by lifting the line exactly perpendicular to the surface** (vertically, on a horizontal member). *(Must)*
*Why:* A line lifted at an angle lands off-line by an amount proportional to the lift angle and the surface curvature. On a log this can amount to several bu.

**R02-022** — **Do not snap ink lines on finished visible surfaces** (化粧面 *keshōmen*) unless they will be planed off. On such surfaces use a marking knife (白書 *shiragaki*), a fine pencil, or mark only on faces and portions that will be removed or concealed. *(Should)*
*Why:* Ink penetrates softwood fibres and may not plane out cleanly without losing dimension.

### 5.2 The bamboo pen (墨差し *sumisashi*)

A **sumisashi** is cut by the carpenter from a strip of bamboo (usually *madake*):

- **One end** is thinned into a **flat spatula** with a sharp edge, split finely into many slits so that it holds ink. It is used **against the sashigane to draw lines**.
- **The other end** is shaped into a **rounded or pointed stick** for **writing symbols, characters and bantsuke**.

A well-cut sumisashi draws a line of roughly **0.3–0.5 mm** (about 1 to 1.5 rin), comparable with a snapped line. The carpenter re-trims it as it wears.

**R02-023** — **Keep all lines on one job to one line weight** (one sumisashi, re-trimmed; one sumitsubo line). *(Should)*
*Why:* The rules for leaving or splitting the line (→ §8) assume a known, constant line width. A thick line from a worn pen adds an unknown error of up to a bu.

### 5.3 Marking gauges (罫引 *kebiki*)

| Type | Japanese | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Scribing gauge | 筋罫引 *suji-kebiki* | Scribes a line parallel to the reference face: tenon cheeks, mortise sides, chamfer lines, housings. Often has two blades or two stems for marking both sides of a mortise at one setting. |
| Cutting gauge | 割罫引 *wari-kebiki* | A heavier blade that cuts through thin boards along the grain (ripping thin stock, kumiko strips). |
| Sickle gauge | 鎌罫引 *kama-kebiki* | A curved stem that reaches into or around obstacles, e.g. marking inside a groove or from an edge that a straight stem cannot follow [P for exact usage]. |

**R02-024** — **Gauge tenon and mortise at the same setting, from the corresponding reference faces** (the face side of the tenon member and the face side of the mortise member that will be flush with it). *(Must)*
*Why:* This makes the fit independent of small errors in member thickness. Measuring the two parts separately makes their errors add up.

**R02-025** — **Never reset a gauge in the middle of marking a set of identical joints.** Mark every instance of that joint at one setting, then reset. *(Must)*

### 5.4 Chalk lines

Modern **chalk lines** (チョークライン) are used on site for sheathing, roofing and slab marking, where fine accuracy is not needed and marks must not stain. **Joinery layout uses the ink line.** Laser levels are now common for site levels and for transferring the level datum (→ §6), but the rokuzumi on each member is still snapped or drawn.

## 6. Reference lines (基準墨)

Japanese marking is **reference-line based**. Every cut is measured from a small number of master lines, never from the edge of a member, because natural timber is never perfectly straight or true. The table defines the principal lines. The words are used somewhat differently in different trades and regions; the definitions below are the carpentry meanings used in this rulebook.

| Line | Japanese | Where | Meaning and use |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Grid line** | 通り芯 *tōri-shin* | Plan (itazu), site | The column centre lines of the building grid, named by the bantsuke (→ §9). The master reference of the whole building. |
| **Ground line** | 地墨 *jizumi* | Foundation, floor slab | The grid (or its offset) snapped on the foundation or the ground floor. |
| **Centre line** | 心墨 / 芯墨 *shinzumi* | Every member | The centre line of a member, on its faces and end grain. Posts: on at least two adjacent faces (usually all four) and on both ends. Beams: on the top face and the end grain; on logs, also on both ends as a cross (十字). Joints are laid out symmetrically about it. |
| **Level line** | 陸墨 *rokuzumi* (also 水墨 *mizuzumi*) | Posts, walls | A horizontal datum at a fixed height (e.g. 3 shaku or 1 m above the finished floor or the sill top), marked on every post. All vertical dimensions on the post are measured from it. |
| **Offset line** | 逃げ墨 *nigezumi*; also 返り墨 *kaerizumi*, 寄り墨 *yorizumi* | Site, members | A line parallel to a reference line at a stated distance, drawn where the true line cannot be marked (it would be cut away, covered, or blocked). **It is always labelled with its offset** (e.g. 「返り 1尺」, 「逃げ 500」). In site setting-out usage the three terms are synonyms. Some carpenters use *kaerizumi* specifically for a check line marked on the member before a cut, so that the true line can be re-established after the cut [P]. |
| **Pass line** | 峠墨 *tōge-zumi* | Keta, beams, roof members | The **峠 (*tōge*, "mountain pass")** is the point where a vertical reference (the wall/column centre line) meets the **underside of the rafters**. The 峠墨 is the horizontal line through that point, marked on the keta or beam. It governs the rafter seats and all roof heights (→ Ch. 05, Ch. 11). On **log beams** (丸太梁), whose tops are irregular, the tōge line is the datum from which every bearing and seat is measured ("○ sun below the tōge", 峠から○寸下がり). *(It is sometimes described loosely as the "crown line" of a log beam. Its proper meaning is the rafter-underside reference height.)* |
| **Top-face / bottom-face lines** | 上端墨 *uwaba-zumi* / 下端墨 *shitaba-zumi* | Beams, sills | Lines on the top or bottom face, used for joints laid out on those faces. The heights of beams are usually controlled by their **top faces** (上端), because floors and roofs sit on them. |
| **Cut line** | 切墨 *kirizumi* | Member ends | The line of a cross-cut: end cuts, tenon shoulders. |
| **Shoulder line** | 胴付墨 *dōzuki-zumi* | Tenons | The line of the shoulder (胴付) of a tenon or a housing. It determines the member's effective length between supports. |

### 6.1 Hierarchy of references

- **Plan position:** tōri-shin (grid) → shinzumi (member centre line) → joint lines. Plan positions of joints are measured **from the member centre line**, which is itself set on the grid.
- **Height:** rokuzumi (level datum) → top-face lines of beams and sills → joint lines. For roofs: the tōge line → rafter seats.
- **Length along a member:** from grid intersections, taken from the kenzao or the itazu (→ §10), to shoulder and cut lines.

**R02-026** — **Plan positions are measured from centre lines; heights from the level datum (or, for the roof, from the tōge line); lengths from grid intersections.** A face of the member is never the primary reference. *(Must)*
*Why:* Natural members vary in section, straightness and twist. Centre-line referencing keeps the grid true whatever the individual member does. This is the key difference between Japanese marking and the face-side/face-edge system of European joinery.

**R02-027** — **Beams whose tops carry floors or roofs are set by their top faces.** Mark bearings and joints from the top-face line (or the rokuzumi transferred to the member), so that members of different depth line up at the top. *(Should)*
*Why:* Floor boards and roof members need a common top plane. The bottoms of beams may differ with span and depth.

**R02-028** — **Every offset line must be labelled with its offset and its direction**, e.g. 「返り一尺 →」. An unlabelled parallel line must be treated as unknown and not used. *(Must)*

**R02-029** — **Snap the centre line on round or irregular members before anything else**: first on both end grains (with a plumb or level so that the cross is vertical and horizontal), then along the top by joining the end centres with a vertically lifted snap line. All further marking follows from these lines. *(Must)*

### 6.2 Choosing the faces

Before marking, each member is **oriented** by the rules of → Ch. 03 (butt down for posts, crown up for beams, bark side and heart side as appropriate, best face to the room). The orientation decides:

- the **appearance face** (見え掛かり *miegakari*, 化粧面): the side seen in the finished room. For boards and exposed members this is usually the bark side (木表 *kiomote*), chosen for grain and absence of knots;
- the **concealed faces** (見え隠れ *miegakure*): where bantsuke and other marks may be written freely;
- the **reference faces** used for gauging (→ R02-024). Usually two adjacent faces are planed flat and square first.

**R02-030** — **Orient and select the faces of each member before marking**, and mark the orientation on the member (e.g. 「上」 for top, 「内」 / 「外」 for room/exterior side, 「元」 / 「末」 for butt/top end) so that it cannot be reversed during cutting. *(Must)* (→ Ch. 03)

**R02-031** — **Write bantsuke, symbols and notes only on faces or portions that will be concealed or cut away.** Never write on appearance faces. *(Should)*

## 7. Marking symbols (墨付け記号) and matching marks (合印)

The tōryō or marking carpenter (墨付け職 *sumitsuke-shoku*) marks the timber, and other carpenters cut it. The marks must tell the cutter unambiguously **which side of each line is waste, how deep to cut, and what kind of joint it is**.

**A caution on variation.** No national standard defines these symbols for hand-cut work. Symbol sets differ between regions, schools and individual workshops, and vocational textbooks teach their own set. The symbols below are the **common core** that is widely recognised. Where a symbol is known to vary, this is noted. **Every job must have an agreed legend** (R02-032).

### 7.1 Common symbols

```
 (1) Centre line (shinzumi)          (2) Cut line, waste side marked
     ---------------+---------------      ||||||||||
                    芯 (or 心)            ----------------------  <- line
     A centre line is identified by      waste side hatched with short
     writing 芯/心 beside it, or by a     strokes; the unhatched side
     workshop-specific tick [varies].    is the piece that is kept.

 (3) Through mortise (tōshi-ana)      (4) Stopped / blind mortise (tome-ana)
     +-----------+                        +-----------+
     | \       / |                        | \         |
     |   \   /   |                        |   \       |
     |     X     |                        |     \     |
     |   /   \   |                        |       \   |
     | /       \ |                        |         \ |
     +-----------+                        +-----------+
     both diagonals = cut right          one diagonal = cut to a stated
     through the member                  depth only (depth written, e.g. 「深 2寸」)

 (5) Housing / shallow recess         (6) Cancelled line
     +-----------------+                  -----/-----/-----/-----
     |/////////////////|                  a wrong line is cancelled with
     |/////////////////|  depth written   short oblique strokes across it
     +-----------------+  (e.g. 大入 5分)  (or 「ケシ」 written beside it)

     ------------------ 陸                -------------------  返り 1尺 ↑
                                          the offset and its direction
                                          are always written
```

### 7.2 Additional conventions

- **Tenons** are not hatched; the waste around them (cheeks, shoulders) is. The tenon length and any haunch are written as numbers.
- **Nuki holes** (貫穴) through posts are marked as through mortises (X) and labelled 「貫」 with the nuki name or level (→ §9.4). Where the hole is cut larger than the nuki to allow wedges (楔 *kusabi*), the wedge side is marked (→ Ch. 09).
- **Peg and pin holes** (栓穴 *sen-ana*) are marked as a small circle or cross at the centre, with the diameter written. For draw-pinning (引き栓), the offset between the holes in tenon and mortise is marked on the tenon [variation; → Ch. 09].
- **Depth** is always written for anything that is not through: 「深 ○寸」 or just the number next to a single diagonal.
- **Joint type names** are often written in abbreviated form on the member (e.g. 「腰掛蟻」, 「鎌」, 「追掛」) near the joint, so the cutter can confirm the intention (→ Ch. 07, Ch. 08).
- **Top and orientation marks:** 「上」 (top), 「元」 (butt), 「末」 (top end), 「内」 (inside), 「外」 (outside), and arrows.

### 7.3 Matching marks (合印 *aijirushi* / 合番 *aiban*)

When two pieces are **fitted to each other individually** (scribed joints, pairs cut together, fittings made to a specific opening, a repaired member fitted to its original partner), both pieces receive the **same mark**, e.g. a kana, a number, or a sign straddling the joint line. The mark shows which pieces belong together and in which orientation.

**R02-032** — **Agree and write down a symbol legend for every job** (on the margin of the itazu or on a separate board), and make sure every carpenter who cuts from the marks has seen it. *(Must)*
*Why:* Symbols vary between workshops. A carpenter from another shop reading a single diagonal as "through" instead of "stopped" will cut through a visible face.

**R02-033** — **Waste is always indicated positively** (hatching, X, diagonal). An unmarked area is always "keep". *(Must)*

**R02-034** — **Every stopped cut must have its depth written at the joint.** Never rely on the cutter to infer depth. *(Must)*

**R02-035** — **A wrong line must be cancelled visibly at once**, not merely ignored or partly planed away. *(Must)*
*Why:* Half-removed wrong lines are a leading cause of mis-cuts, especially when the marking and the cutting are done by different people or on different days.

**R02-036** — **Give matching marks to every pair of individually fitted pieces**, placed where they remain visible until assembly (not on surfaces that will be cut away). *(Must)*

## 8. Cutting to the line and traditional tolerances

### 8.1 Leaving, splitting and removing the line

A snapped or drawn line has a width *w* of about 0.3–0.5 mm. The carpenter must decide, for every cut, **where the saw or chisel goes relative to the line**. Carpenters use three expressions, all stated relative to the **piece that is kept**:

| Expression | Japanese | Meaning | Kept piece is… |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Leave the line** | 墨を残す *sumi wo nokosu* (also 墨を生かす *ikasu*) | Cut on the waste side of the line, so the whole line remains on the kept piece (and is planed or pared off later, or left) | larger by ≈ ½ *w* per face than the line centre |
| **Split the line** | 墨を割る / 墨をまたぐ / 墨半分 *sumi-hanbun* | Cut down the centre of the line; half the line remains | exactly at the line centre |
| **Remove the line** | 墨を払う *sumi wo harau* (also 墨を取る; some say 墨を殺す *korosu*) | Cut on the kept side of the line, so the line goes with the waste | smaller by ≈ ½ *w* per face |

The terms are not used uniformly. In particular *sumi wo korosu* ("kill the line") is used by some for removing the line and by others more loosely. **Always state the intention in these explicit terms.**

### 8.2 The fit system

Suppose a tenon and its mortise are marked **at the same gauge setting** (R02-024). Nominal width *T* is the distance between the centres of the two lines. Then:

| Tenon | Mortise | Tenon width | Mortise width | Result |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| leave | remove | *T* + *w* | *T* + *w* | **Nominal (matched) fit.** The standard combination for a snug fit that assembles by hand pressure or light tapping. |
| leave | split | *T* + *w* | *T* | **Interference of about one line width.** A driven fit. Used in softwoods, often with compression of the tenon (木殺し *kigoroshi*; → Ch. 06). |
| leave | leave | *T* + *w* | *T* − *w* | **Interference of about two line widths.** Only for soft timber with kigoroshi, or where the joint will be dressed at trial assembly. |
| split | remove | *T* | *T* + *w* | **Clearance of about one line width.** For joints that must slide (e.g. sliding dovetails in long housings, wedged joints where the wedge supplies the tightness). |

So **the tenon (male, 男木 *ogi*) leaves the line** and the **mortise (female, 女木 *megi*) removes the line** for a nominal fit. The mortise is split or left instead when the fit must be tighter. This is the common teaching, but individual carpenters and schools vary; test it on offcuts (R02-039).

The same logic applies to **shoulders and lengths**. A shoulder line (胴付) is usually cut **leaving the line** and then pared exactly to it, so that the shoulder closes hard against its partner. Housing depths and seat cuts are cut to the line and checked with the square.

**R02-037** — **Saw kerfs always fall on the waste side of the line.** *(Must)*

**R02-038** — **For a matched fit, the male part leaves the line and the female part removes it** (both marked at one gauge setting). For a tighter fit, reduce the female part's allowance (split or leave the line), not the male part's. *(Should)*
*Why:* The male part (tenon, tongue) is the part that can be compressed (kigoroshi) or pared to adjust the fit. The female part (mortise, housing) is hard to enlarge accurately once cut, and impossible to make smaller.

**R02-039** — **Before cutting a series of joints in a new species or with a new team, cut a test joint on an offcut** with the agreed line rule, and adjust the rule (not the gauge) to get the intended fit. *(Should)*

**R02-040** — **When the marking and cutting are done by different carpenters, the line rule (leave / split / remove) must be stated for each joint type**, for example in the symbol legend (R02-032). *(Must)*

### 8.3 Traditional tolerance vocabulary

| Term | Japanese | Approximate size | Use |
|---|---|---|---|
| **One hair** | 一毛 *ichimō* | 0.03 mm | Rhetorical: the finest conceivable difference |
| **Paper thickness** | 紙一重 *kami-hitoe* | ≈ 0.05–0.1 mm (one sheet of Japanese paper) | A fit "tight by a sheet of paper"; the gap acceptable on a fine visible shoulder |
| **One rin** | 一厘 *ichirin* | 0.3 mm | About one line width; the unit of fit adjustment in joinery |
| **One bu** | 一分 *ichibu* | 3 mm | A coarse error in joinery; the scale of reveals (*chiri*) and chamfers |
| **One sun** | 一寸 *issun* | 30 mm | A layout-level error, i.e. a mistake |

Representative expectations (not normative; → Ch. 15 for assembly tolerances):
- **Visible joinery** (shoulders of nageshi, kamoi, fittings, sukiya work): gaps of the order of *kami-hitoe*.
- **Structural joinery** (tenons, splices): fits adjusted in *rin*, with kigoroshi to take up the rest.
- **Frame layout** (column positions on the grid, heights of datum lines): within about 1 bu over the building, controlled by the rods (→ §10).

**R02-041** — **Express fit tolerances in terms of line widths (rin), and layout tolerances in bu**, and do not mix the two scales. *(Should)*
*Why:* Fit is controlled by the line rule and the gauge. Layout is controlled by the rods and datum lines. Treating a layout error as a fit problem (or the reverse) leads to paring joints to absorb frame errors, which weakens the joints.

## 9. Grid numbering (番付 *bantsuke*)

### 9.1 Purpose

In a traditional frame every post, beam and tie is **unique**. Each is a natural member, and each carries joints cut for one position only. **Bantsuke** (番付, "numbering") is the address system that ties each member to its place on the grid. It is written on the itazu (→ §10) and on every member, and it lets a frame be cut in the workshop, carried to site and raised in the right order (→ Ch. 15). It is also what allows old buildings to be dismantled and re-erected: dismantling for repair begins by recording or re-marking the bantsuke (→ Ch. 16).

### 9.2 The grid

- **One axis** is labelled with the **i-ro-ha kana** in poem order: い ろ は に ほ へ と ち り ぬ る を わ か よ た れ そ つ ね な ら む う ゐ の お く や ま け ふ こ え て あ さ き ゆ め み し ゑ ひ も せ す.
- **The other axis** is labelled with **kanji numerals**: 一 二 三 四 五 六 七 八 九 十 十一 …
- A point on the grid is named by **kana + numeral**, e.g. 「い一」 (*i-no-ichi*), 「ろ三」 (*ro-no-san*), 「は十」.
- Grid lines are usually drawn at the **half-ken (3-shaku) pitch** in modern practice, so that every possible post position has a name. In older practice the lines followed the actual column lines of the building [P].
- The **i-ro-ha order is a kind of alphabet**. The expression **いの一番 (*i no ichiban*, "i-1, the very first")**, meaning "first of all", comes from the first post position on the grid, often the first post raised.

### 9.3 Direction and starting corner

The direction of the axes and the starting corner **are not uniform**. Two main conventions are reported:

| Convention | Japanese | Origin (い一) | Kana run | Numerals run | Where found |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Descending** | 下り番付 *kudari-bantsuke* | **Top right** of the itazu | right → left | top → bottom | Traditional hand-cut practice. It follows the order of vertical Japanese writing, which runs right to left, top to bottom. |
| **Ascending** | 上り番付 *nobori-bantsuke* | **Bottom left** of the plan | left → right | bottom → top | Pre-cut (machine-cut) factories and CAD, matching X/Y coordinates (X1, Y1). |

Further variations:
- which axis gets the kana (the frontage 桁行 or the depth 梁間);
- whether the plan is drawn with the front (entrance, street, or south) at the bottom;
- which compass corner the origin corresponds to on site. This follows from the two previous choices, and practice is reported to differ by region and workshop;
- whether the numeral or the kana is written first.

The regional differences (for example between Kansai and Kantō practice) are real, but they are not documented consistently enough to state a rule. **Treat the itazu legend as authoritative** (R02-043).

```
  DESCENDING (traditional)                 ASCENDING (pre-cut / CAD)
  kana right->left, numerals downward      kana left->right, numerals upward

        と  へ  ほ  に  は  ろ  い               七 +---+---+---+---+---+
     一 +---+---+---+---+---+---+ <-い一          |   |   |   |   |   |
        |   |   |   |   |   |   |                六 +---+---+---+---+---+
     二 +---+---+---+---+---+---+                   |   |   |   |   |   |
        |   |   |   |   |   |   |                ...
     三 +---+---+---+---+---+---+                一 +---+---+---+---+---+
        |   |   |   |   |   |   |                   い  ろ  は  に  ほ  へ
     四 +---+---+---+---+---+---+               い一 -> origin bottom left

   (each '+' is a grid point: e.g. the point in column は, row 三 is 「は三」)
```

### 9.4 Labelling each member

**Posts (柱):**
- Label with the grid point: 「ろ三」.
- Write the label on a **concealed face** (or a face that will receive a wall), **near the foot**, about 1–2 shaku above the base, where it can be read during raising. Some workshops also write it near the top.
- Write it **on a consistent face**, for example the face toward the origin or toward the front of the building, so that the label also shows the post's rotation. The convention is set by the workshop and recorded in the legend.
- Mark through posts (通し柱 *tōshi-bashira*) and two-storey positions distinctly, e.g. 「通」 added, or a circle round the label [varies].
- For upper-floor posts, add the floor: 「二階 ろ三」 or a workshop mark.

**Horizontal members (beams 梁, purlins and plates 桁, sills 土台, ties 貫, rails):**
- Label with the **grid line** they lie on and the **span** they cover: e.g. 「ろ通り 二〜四」 (on line *ro*, from 2 to 4). Alternatively, write at each end the grid point at which that end lands: 「ろ二」 at one end, 「ろ四」 at the other.
- For members at several levels on the same line, add the **level name**: 土台 (sill), 足固め (floor tie), 地貫 / 胴貫 / 内法貫 / 天井貫 (ties at ground, mid, lintel and ceiling level), 胴差し (floor beam at the second floor), 桁 (plate), 小屋梁 (roof beam), and so on.
- Write the labels on the **top or a concealed side face, close to the ends**, where they remain visible until the member is in place.

**Roof members:**
- Roof posts (小屋束) and purlins (母屋) are named from the grid of the roof framing plan (小屋伏図), in the same way as the floor frame, with a level prefix where needed.

**Fittings and tatami:**
- Under centre-to-centre planning, **mats and fittings are made to measure** and are labelled with their room and position. Tatami makers traditionally write the position on the underside of each mat, and fittings are marked on a concealed edge.

### 9.5 Reading bantsuke on old buildings

On old buildings bantsuke are often found **carved or inked on hidden faces**, sometimes in more than one set (original construction and later dismantlings). In repairs they are **recorded before dismantling**, and new labels are added in a clearly different form (e.g. on tags) so that the historic marks are preserved (→ Ch. 16).

**R02-042** — **Every structural member must carry its bantsuke before it leaves the marking bench.** No member is cut without a label. *(Must)*

**R02-043** — **The bantsuke convention (origin corner, axis directions, which axis is kana, face on which posts are labelled) must be drawn as a legend on the itazu.** Nobody on the job should have to assume a convention. *(Must)*

**R02-044** — **Write post labels on a consistent face that shows the post's rotation**, and write horizontal member labels so that both ends are identified. *(Should)*
*Why:* A post with joints on three faces can be raised rotated by 90° or 180° if the label does not fix its rotation. A beam with different joints at its two ends can be reversed.

**R02-045** — On repair work, **never overwrite or plane off historic bantsuke**. Add new identification in a distinguishable form and record the old marks. *(Must)*

**R02-046** — **Keep bantsuke on concealed faces**, and on the concealed parts of joint surfaces where they will be hidden after assembly. *(Should)*

## 10. Plan boards, story poles and section rods

In traditional practice the **tōryō** (棟梁, master carpenter) produces a small set of full-size or scaled **wooden documents** from the design. These, not paper drawings, are the **controlling documents** of the job. They are made from wood because a workshop is dusty and wet and paper does not survive; a board can be read by anyone at the bench, and it can be planed clean and reused.

### 10.1 The plan board (板図 *itazu*)

- A thin board, about 3 × 1.5 shaku to 6 × 3 shaku, traditionally of cedar or cypress, today often a sheet of veneer plywood. It is drawn with the sumisashi (sometimes with pencil for revisions).
- **Contents (typically):**
  - the **grid** with bantsuke labels (→ §9), plus the legend;
  - **post positions**, with their size and type, e.g. through posts distinguished from single-storey posts by a symbol such as a circle or a double mark [varies];
  - **walls, openings and nuki lines**;
  - the **floor framing plan** (床伏 / 土台伏): sills, floor ties, joists;
  - the **upper floor framing** (二階梁伏) and the **roof framing** (小屋伏): beams with direction, depth, and splice positions (→ Ch. 07). Usually these are on separate boards or on the back of the board;
  - key **heights** relative to the datum;
  - **splice and joint types** at critical points;
  - the **planning system** and ken value (R02-005).
- **Scale:** chosen to fit the board, often between about 1:20 and 1:50 [P]. The itazu shows **positions and relations**. Dimensions come from the rods, not from scaling the board.
- The tōryō draws it, and uses it to plan the **selection and orientation of each timber** (→ Ch. 03): which log becomes which beam, where the heavy members go, where the crown of a log beam points. On a hand-cut job the tōryō usually marks the key members himself.

### 10.2 The plan rod (間竿 *kenzao*)

- A straight, dry, stable rod (typically hinoki or sugi, square in section), longer than the largest bay, marked by the tōryō with the **plan divisions**: whole and half ken, and any non-standard bays. For tatami-wari buildings it is marked with the actual computed bay distances (R02-007).
- It is used to **transfer plan positions onto members**, e.g. column positions along a sill or plate, beam bearings, and onto the site.
- **Why a rod and not a tape:** a single rod used for every member gives the same measure everywhere. Tapes stretch, differ between one another, and are read with parallax. The rod's divisions are marked once, by one person.

### 10.3 The height rod (尺杖 *shakuzue*)

- A rod, usually square in section, somewhat longer than the tallest post. It is marked with **every height** in the building, measured from a datum at its foot, typically:
  - foundation top / stone top (礎石天端);
  - sill top (土台天端);
  - floor level(s) (床高), finished and structural;
  - the level datum (陸墨);
  - sill-rail top (敷居天端) and lintel underside (鴨居下端), so the door height (内法);
  - nageshi, nuki levels (足固め, 胴貫, 内法貫, 天井貫);
  - ceiling (天井高), second-floor beam (胴差), plate top (桁天端);
  - the tōge (峠) and the ridge height where applicable.
- **Different faces** of a square rod can carry heights for **different conditions**, e.g. one face for the main room, one for the veranda (縁側), one for the earth-floored area (土間), where floor levels differ.
- Every post is marked by **laying it beside the shakuzue** and transferring the marks with the square, not by measuring.

### 10.4 The section rod and section drawing (矩計 *kanabakari*)

- **矩計図 *kanabakari-zu*** is a detailed **section drawing** of the building, showing all vertical relationships: foundation, floor build-up, wall and opening heights, eaves and roof pitch, at a large scale (often 1:20 or larger).
- **矩計棒 *kanabakari-bō*** or **矩計竿** is the **rod version of the section**: a story pole carrying the heights of the section, including roof-related heights. In many workshops *shakuzue* and *kanabakari-bō* are the same object under two names; in others the *kanabakari* also carries the eaves and roof heights and the slope data.
- Chapter 04 discusses these rods (and the generic *bakabō* 馬鹿棒, any stick used for repeated measurement) as workshop tools.

### 10.5 Rules

**R02-047** — **On a hand-cut job, the itazu, kenzao and shakuzue (or kanabakari) made by the tōryō are the controlling documents for cutting.** Any change of design must be entered on them first, and then on the paper drawings (permit drawings, contract drawings), which must be reconciled with them. *(Must)*
*Why:* The cutters work only from the boards and rods. A change that exists only on paper will not reach the timber.

**R02-048** — **All plan lengths are transferred with the kenzao and all heights with the shakuzue. Tapes are not used for marking joints on a hand-cut frame.** *(Should)*
*Why:* One measuring instrument for the whole job removes instrument-to-instrument error. Transfer by laying the rod alongside the member removes reading errors.

**R02-049** — **Check the rods against each other and against the site before marking begins**: the kenzao against the site grid (set out with the ōgane and snapped on the foundation), and the shakuzue against the foundation levels. *(Must)*

**R02-050** — **Keep the itazu and rods on site, protected, until the building is complete**, and if possible keep them afterwards with the building's records. *(Should)*
*Why:* They are the fastest way to make a replacement member or to understand the building in a later repair. Old itazu kept in building archives are valuable historical documents.

**R02-051** — **Each rod carries its own identification** (project, rod type, datum definition, face meaning), written at its foot. *(Should)*

## 11. The marking workflow, start to finish

This is the standard order of work, connecting this chapter with the others.

1. **Design and planning system.** Fix the planning system, ken or mat size, column width, and (for temples) the shi module (§2, §3; R02-005, R02-013).
2. **Kiwari.** Derive member sizes and check them structurally (§3; R02-016; → Ch. 10, Ch. 17).
3. **Itazu.** The tōryō draws the grid, bantsuke legend, posts, walls and framing plans (§9, §10; R02-043).
4. **Rods.** Make the kenzao and shakuzue/kanabakari. Check them against the site (§10; R02-049).
5. **Timber allocation.** Assign each log or stock piece to a position on the itazu according to size, quality and growth orientation (→ Ch. 03).
6. **Preparation.** Plane two adjacent reference faces (or, for logs, clean the ends), and check the sashigane (R02-018).
7. **Centre lines and datum.** Snap the centre lines (R02-029), mark the level datum on posts or the top-face and tōge lines on beams (§6).
8. **Bantsuke and orientation marks** on concealed faces (R02-030, R02-042).
9. **Joint marking.** Lay out the joints from the centre lines and datum, with the kenzao and shakuzue, gauging mating parts at one setting (R02-024), and apply the symbols and depths (§7).
10. **Check.** The tōryō or a second carpenter checks the marks against the itazu before cutting. Fix and cancel errors visibly (R02-035).
11. **Cutting** by the agreed line rule (§8; → Ch. 04, Ch. 06, Ch. 07, Ch. 08).
12. **Trial assembly** where required, then **raising** in bantsuke order (→ Ch. 15).
