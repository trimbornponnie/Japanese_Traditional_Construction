# Chapter 13 — Building Types: Minka, Shoin and Sukiya (民家・書院造・数寄屋造 / *minka, shoin-zukuri, sukiya-zukuri*)

**Scope.** This chapter describes the three great families of Japanese domestic building and the construction rules that distinguish them: **minka** (民家, the houses of farmers, merchants and artisans, including farmhouses 農家 *nōka*, townhouses 町家 *machiya* and storehouses 蔵 *kura*); **shoin-zukuri** (書院造, the formal reception architecture of the warrior and aristocratic classes, Muromachi to Edo); and **sukiya-zukuri** (数寄屋造) with the tea house (茶室 *chashitsu*), whose deliberately rustic, light construction demands its own joinery. The chapter concentrates on plan types, structural systems, member sizes and the rules of each tradition. Frame members and foundations are treated generally in → Ch. 10, roof and thatch framing in → Ch. 11, units and the ken/tatami modules in → Ch. 02, and the detailed construction of floors, earth walls, ceilings, tokonoma and sliding fittings (tategu) in → Ch. 14. Religious architecture is in → Ch. 12.

As in the rest of the book, proportional figures are **representative** and vary by region, period and workshop; they are presented with ranges and the variation noted.

## 1. Three traditions and the hierarchy of formality

| | Minka | Shoin | Sukiya |
|---|---|---|---|
| Social origin | farmers, merchants, artisans | warrior and court elite, temples | the tea culture of elite and merchant patrons; later wealthy townsmen |
| Carpenter | village/town carpenter (町大工, 民家大工) | palace and mansion carpenters (e.g. the Heinouchi and Kōra lineages served the shogunate) | specialist sukiya carpenter (数寄屋大工) |
| Leading idea | shelter, work space, durability; large timbers used as found | order, rank and display; uniform, squared, planed members | refined rusticity; natural materials, lightness, restraint |
| Typical members | round logs, bent beams, heavy posts | square posts with chamfers, nageshi, coffered ceilings | small posts (menkawa, polished logs), bamboo, thin members |

The Japanese aesthetic hierarchy **真・行・草** (*shin, gyō, sō* — "formal, semi-formal, informal", borrowed from the three styles of calligraphy) runs across all three. It is used for everything from the tokonoma to stepping stones: *shin* is the regular, squared, complete form (the shoin), *sō* the free, natural, abbreviated form (the grass-hut tea room), *gyō* the intermediate.

**R13-001** — **Must.** Decide the formality level (shin, gyō or sō) of each room before selecting members, and keep the vocabulary consistent within the room: e.g. a *shin* room does not take bark-edged posts, and a *sō* tea room does not take a nageshi. *Why:* the three vocabularies are coded; mixing them within one room reads as an error to any trained eye. Mixing *between* rooms (a sukiya-style tea room attached to a shoin) is normal and intended.

**R13-002** — **Should.** Engage a carpenter trained in the relevant tradition: a sukiya carpenter for tea rooms, a shrine-and-temple or mansion carpenter for formal shoin, a minka carpenter for farmhouse repair. *Why:* the joinery skills differ in kind — heavy log framing, precise square framing, and scribing of irregular natural members are distinct trades (→ Ch. 04).

## 2. Minka: the structural system

### 2.1 Jōya and geya (上屋・下屋) — core and aisles
Most older Japanese farmhouses share a basic structural diagram:

- **上屋 *jōya*** — the structural core: a rectangle of tall posts (上屋柱 *jōya-bashira*) carrying the main tie beams (上屋梁 / 梁) and, above them, the roof frame (thatched *sasu* or a post-and-strut *wagoya*, → Ch. 11).
- **下屋 *geya*** — aisles (庇 *hisashi*) around one to four sides of the core, with lower posts (下屋柱 *geya-bashira*) and short tie beams (繋ぎ梁 *tsunagi-bari*) spanning from the geya posts to the jōya posts. The roof slope continues down over the geya, sometimes with a change in pitch.

```
   Cross-section of a jōya/geya farmhouse (schematic)

                 /\
                /  \        sasu (扠首) thatched roof
               /    \
              /      \
   _________ /________\ ________
  /         |  上屋梁   |        \      ← geya roof continues the slope
 /  繋ぎ梁  |          |  繋ぎ梁  \
|___________|  jōya    |__________|
|  geya     |  (core)  |   geya   |
|  下屋柱   |  上屋柱   |  下屋柱   |
```

Historical development (in broad terms): in older houses the jōya posts stand inside the rooms at intervals of about one ken, obstructing the floor; over the Edo period, heavier beams and the **差鴨居** (§2.4) allowed builders to omit jōya posts (柱抜き *hashira-nuki*) and open up large rooms. Many surviving houses show this in their repairs: a post removed and a deep lintel or beam inserted.

**R13-003** — **Must.** In repairing a jōya/geya house, identify which posts are jōya (carrying main beams) and which are geya (carrying tie beams and aisle roof) before removing or replacing any post. *Why:* removing a jōya post without inserting a transfer beam or sashigamoi of adequate depth drops the main beam and the roof ridge above it.

**R13-004** — **Must.** Tie beams (繋ぎ梁) from geya to jōya posts shall be tenoned and pinned (込栓) or dovetailed into the jōya post, not merely seated. *Why:* they tie the aisle posts back against the outward thrust of the geya roof and against lateral load; seated ties let the geya posts lean out.

### 2.2 Posts: daikokubashira, ebisubashira and foundations
- **大黒柱 *daikokubashira*** — the largest post of the house, usually at the junction of the earth-floored work area (doma) and the raised living floor, often 8 sun–1 shaku 2 sun square (≈24–36 cm) or more in wealthy houses, of keyaki, pine or cedar. It receives beams from several directions (a multi-member shiguchi, → Ch. 08).
- **恵比寿柱 *ebisubashira*** (also 小黒柱) — the second principal post, typically at the doma side or across from the daikokubashira.
- **Foundations.** Traditional minka posts stand on foundation stones — **石場建て *ishibadate*** — with the post foot scribed to the stone (光付け *hikaritsuke*, → Ch. 10). Floor-level ties (**足固め *ashigatame***) connect the posts just above the floor to stiffen the frame at its base; a continuous sill (土台 *dodai*) is typical in machiya and later houses. Earth-fast posts (掘立柱) survive in some old or minor buildings.

**R13-005** — **Must.** The daikokubashira shall be continuous from foundation stone to the highest beam it receives; beams framing into it from several directions shall enter at staggered heights so that no horizontal section of the post loses more than roughly one third of its area. *Why:* the daikokubashira is the node of the house; multiple mortises at one level neck it (same principle as R12-026).

**R13-006** — **Must.** In ishibadate construction every post foot shall be scribed to its stone so that it bears over most of its section, and ashigatame or a sill shall tie the post feet together. *Why:* without the tie, the post feet can walk on the stones in an earthquake; without full bearing, the post splits or rolls (→ Ch. 10, Ch. 17 on ishibadate performance).

### 2.3 Beams: round logs, bent beams and beam grids
- **丸太梁 *maruta-bari*** — minka beams are commonly round or only roughly squared logs (pine 松 and chestnut 栗 are common for beams in many regions; cedar in others), used to their natural size to save labour.
- **曲がり梁 *magari-bari*** — naturally curved logs used as beams, especially pine grown on slopes. They are **set with the crown (the convex side) upward**, like an arch; the rise gives extra depth at mid-span where bending is greatest, and allows beams at different levels to cross each other.
- **牛梁 *ushibari*** — a very heavy beam running along the ridge direction (or on the main axis over the doma) that carries the ends of several transverse beams; typical of eastern and northern Japanese farmhouses.
- **梁組 *harigumi*** — in the doma, beams cross in two directions in an exposed grid, the pride of the house; the lower ones are often the longest and largest.
- For *kyōrogumi* vs *oriokigumi* beam–plate relations see → Ch. 10 and Ch. 11.

**R13-007** — **Must.** A curved beam shall be set crown upward (背を上に) and its ends seated level; a curved beam set crown down is a structural error. *Why:* crown-up, the beam behaves partly as an arch and its greatest depth occurs where the bending moment is greatest; crown-down it sags visually and the bearing seats are uneven.

**R13-008** — **Should.** In a grid of round beams, adjacent logs of one direction shall alternate butt (moto) and top (sue) ends at each support (→ Ch. 03), and the heights of their seats shall be marked from a common level line struck on every log (the log is "squared" only at its seats). *Why:* alternating moto/sue balances the natural taper so the floor or roof above stays level; common level marking is the only way to frame irregular logs accurately.

**R13-009** — **Must.** Where a beam crosses and rests on another (渡り腮 *watariago* or 兜蟻 etc., → Ch. 08), the notch in the lower beam shall not exceed about one third of its depth at that point. *Why:* the lower beam is typically the principal member; notching it deeply at a point of load is the classic cause of cracked minka beams.

### 2.4 Sashigamoi (差鴨居) — the deep lintel beam

### Sashigamoi — deep lintel beam (差鴨居, *sashigamoi*)
- **Class:** shiguchi-connected beam/lintel (tenoned beam acting with posts as a portal frame).
- **Typical use:** minka and machiya, from the mid-Edo period especially in western Japan; spans between posts at lintel height, carrying the floor or beams above and receiving the sliding doors below in grooves cut on its underside. It allows intermediate posts to be omitted, creating wide openings between rooms.
- **Load behaviour:** bending (as a beam carrying load from above); with the posts it forms a semi-rigid portal whose tenon joints resist racking (a moment connection of limited stiffness). It does **not** replace the need for walls and nuki for lateral stiffness in the other direction.
- **Proportions:** depth (成) typically 8 sun to 1 shaku 5 sun (≈24–45 cm) depending on span and load, compared with an ordinary *kamoi* of about 1.5–2 sun; width = post width less the reveals (chiri) on both faces. Tenon: long (長枘) or through tenon of about ⅓ of the post width in thickness.
- **Orientation rules:** as for all kamoi and shikii, the grooved face is normally the **bark side (木表 *kiomote*)** — i.e. for a sashigamoi the bark side faces **down** (→ Ch. 03, Ch. 14). *Why:* a flat-sawn board cups so that its bark face becomes concave; with the grooves on that face, the groove walls tend to open slightly rather than pinch the doors. Moto/sue direction follows the conventions of → Ch. 03.
- **Marking:** the underside is the reference face (door grooves and their spacing); the top is marked for the floor joists or beams it carries.
- **Joinery at the posts:** (a) **through tenon with 込栓 *komisen*** (draw-bored pin) — most common; (b) where two sashigamoi enter a post from opposite sides at the same level, **竿車知継ぎ *sao-shachi-tsugi*** — one member's long tongue (竿 *sao*) passes through the post into the opposite member and is locked by 車知栓 *shachisen* (→ Ch. 08, Ch. 09); (c) **雇い枘 *yatoi-hozo*** (a loose tenon) where the post must be installed after the lintel.
- **Assembly:** post frames are assembled with sashigamoi on the ground or in the air as a portal (→ Ch. 15); pins are driven after the frame is plumbed.
- **Common errors:** cutting door grooves before the timber has stabilised (the grooves close); undersizing depth for a span that was designed with a post that was later removed; relying on the sashigamoi alone for racking resistance.

**R13-010** — **Must.** A sashigamoi that replaces a structural post shall be sized as a beam for the load it now carries, not as a lintel. Where the removed post supported a beam from above, the sashigamoi's seat for that beam must be positioned directly over its span's calculated support, and its depth checked for bending and shear. *Why:* this was the Edo builders' innovation, and it has also been the cause of many sagging minka where post removal outran the lintel's capacity.

**R13-011** — **Must.** Sashigamoi tenons shall be locked by draw-bored komisen or by sao-shachi through the post; tenons merely housed (大入れ) without pins are not acceptable. *Why:* the portal action depends on the tenon being unable to withdraw; an unpinned tenon slips out under repeated racking (→ Ch. 09 on draw-boring).

**R13-012** — **Should.** Cut the door grooves (樋端 *hibata* and 溝) of a sashigamoi at final fitting, after the timber has stood in the building through at least one season, or allow a trimming margin. *Why:* deep sections continue to move after installation; grooves cut at once may bind the doors (→ Ch. 14 tategu).

### 2.5 The thatched sasu roof (扠首組 *sasugumi*) and its maintenance
The typical minka roof is **thatched** (茅葺 *kayabuki*: susuki grass, reed *yoshi*, or rice/wheat straw) over a **sasu** frame, a pair of inclined poles meeting at the ridge like a pair of hands in prayer (hence 合掌 *gasshō*). (General roof framing and the wagoya alternative are in → Ch. 11.)

### Sasu frame — inclined principal poles (扠首, *sasu*)
- **Class:** triangulated roof frame (principal rafters without a king post).
- **Typical use:** thatched minka roofs over the jōya; the geya roofs are rafters leaning onto it.
- **Load behaviour:** the pair of sasu forms a triangle with the tie beam (梁) below: roof load produces compression in the sasu and **tension in the beam**, which holds the sasu feet together. The apex is lashed, not jointed.
- **Proportions:** sasu are round poles, commonly 4–6 sun (≈12–18 cm) in diameter at the foot for ordinary houses, larger in gasshō-zukuri; roof pitch is steep — about 45° to 60° (≈10/10 to 17/10 in kōbai terms), steeper where snow and thatch drainage demand it.
- **Orientation rules:** butt (moto) end down; the foot is shaped to a **point** (sasu-jiri) that sits in a socket or notch cut in the beam or plate (the socket is sometimes called *sasu-uke* or, in some regions, other local names).
- **Assembly:** sasu pairs are raised, crossed at the apex with a ridge pole (棟木) laid in the crotch; horizontal poles (屋中 *yanaka*) are tied across the sasu at intervals; secondary rafters (垂木, in Shirakawa called *kudari*) and a mesh of bamboo or small poles (小舞) are tied on; the thatch is laid in courses from the eaves upward and stitched to the frame. **All connections above the beam are lashed** — with straw rope (縄), wisteria vine (藤蔓), or in Shirakawa with *neso* (ネソ, twisted young witch-hazel shoots; several hundred to about a thousand are commonly quoted for one re-roofing).
- **Common errors:** seating the sasu foot on a flat bearing that allows it to slide; using a tie beam that is notched deeply at the sasu socket; replacing lashings with nails (which rust and split the poles, and prevent the necessary flexibility).

**R13-013** — **Must.** The sasu foot shall sit in a socket in the tie beam (or a plate directly connected to it) so that the tie beam resists the outward thrust; never rest the sasu feet on a member not tied across the building. *Why:* the sasu triangle is only stable if its base is closed by a tension member.

**R13-014** — **Should.** Sasu connections above the beam shall be lashed, not nailed or rigidly jointed. *Why:* the lashed frame accommodates movement of the thatch mass and settlement, can be re-tightened and is easily dismantled for rethatching; ropes and neso are part of the roof's maintenance cycle.

**R13-015** — **Should.** Where an irori (open hearth) was traditionally in use, maintain smoke exposure of the roof (regular fires or fumigation) or plan for more frequent thatch replacement. *Why:* smoke dries the thatch and frame and deters insects; it is commonly observed that thatch and lashings deteriorate faster once the hearth is abandoned.

**Thatch maintenance and community labour.** A thatched roof is renewed on a cycle (whole-roof re-thatching at roughly 20–40 years depending on material, pitch, climate and upkeep; Shirakawa-gō's gasshō roofs are commonly quoted at about 30 years), with patching in between by inserting new bundles (差し茅 *sashigaya*). The work was organised by **結 *yui*** — mutual labour exchange among households — and material came from communally managed thatch fields (茅場 *kayaba*). In Shirakawa-gō yui-based re-roofing continues and is a recognised part of the World Heritage site's conservation (inscribed 1995 with Gokayama).

**R13-016** — **Should.** A thatched building's maintenance plan shall include a supply of thatch (kayaba or purchase), a schedule for sashigaya patching and whole-side re-roofing, and fire precautions (water supply, discharge sprinklers as at Shirakawa-gō). *Why:* the roof is a consumable; buildings are lost when the material supply chain or the labour organisation lapses, not usually when the frame fails.

## 3. Farmhouse plans and regional types

### 3.1 Plan types
- **広間型 *hiroma-gata*** (large-room type): the living area beside the doma is one large room (広間 *hiroma*) with the hearth, with one or two smaller rooms (座敷, 寝間/納戸) behind or beside. Regarded as an older type in eastern Japan.
- **田の字型 *ta-no-ji-gata*** / **四間取り *yoma-dori*** (four-room type): the raised floor divided into four rooms in a 2 × 2 grid like the character 田; widespread by the late Edo period, allowing separation of daily and formal rooms (the best room, *zashiki* or *dei*, at the far corner from the entrance).
- **前座敷三間取り** and other variants, regionally named.
- **土間 *doma***: the earth-floored area — entrance, kitchen with stoves (竈 *kamado*), work and storage space, sometimes stabling. Floor of rammed earth (三和土 *tataki*: earth, lime and bittern) in better houses.
- **板の間 *itanoma***: board-floored living area with the **囲炉裏 *irori*** (sunken hearth, with a pot hook 自在鉤 *jizaikagi* hung from a beam or from a smoke shelf 火棚 *hidana*).

**R13-017** — **Should.** In a four-room (田の字) plan, the intersection of the room partitions shall be carried by a post on the ta-no-ji centre (or a beam designed to omit it), and the partitions shall align with the beam grid above. *Why:* the plan partitions are the structural lines of the house; misaligned partitions require transfer beams that minka frames rarely have.

**R13-018** — **Must.** The irori shall be framed within an opening in the floor structure trimmed by joists and a frame (炉縁 *robuchi*), lined with clay/stone, and kept clear of structural timbers by an adequate margin; the smoke shelf (hidana) shall be hung from beams, never from the thatch frame lashings. *Why:* fire safety and load path — the hidana with pot and kettle is a considerable hanging load.

### 3.2 Regional farmhouse types

| Type | Japanese | Region | Principal features |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gasshō-zukuri | 合掌造 | Shirakawa-gō (Gifu), Gokayama (Toyama) | very large steep sasu roofs, gable-ended; multi-storey attic (for sericulture) inside the roof |
| Magariya | 曲屋 | Nanbu region (esp. Iwate, e.g. Tōno) | L-shaped plan; the stable (厩 *umaya*) wing joined to the house under a continuous thatched roof |
| Chūmon-zukuri | 中門造 | Akita, Yamagata, Niigata (heavy snow) | L-shaped with a projecting wing (中門) containing entrance and stable |
| Honmune-zukuri | 本棟造 | central Nagano (Matsumoto–Shiojiri area) | large, near-square plan; gable entry; low-pitched board-shingle (later tile) roof; ridge ornament 雀おどし *suzume-odoshi* at the gable |
| Yamato-mune / takahe-zukuri | 大和棟 / 高塀造 | Nara basin, Osaka | steep thatched main roof flanked by gable walls (高塀) capped with tiles; a lower tiled roof (落棟 *ochimune*) over the kitchen side |
| Kabuto-zukuri | 兜造 | Yamagata, Fukushima, Yamanashi, Gunma and others (varieties) | thatched roof with gables cut open/raised (like a samurai helmet) to light and ventilate sericulture attics |
| Shihōbuta-zukuri | 四方蓋造 | Tokushima (Yoshino River basin), Kagawa | thatched hipped main roof surrounded on all four sides by broad tiled pent roofs (蓋 *futa*) |
| Kudo-zukuri | くど造 | Saga (northern Kyushu) | ridge forms a U (コ) shape around the plan |
| Buntō-gata | 分棟型 | southern Kyushu, Okinawa, parts of the Pacific coast | separate roofs (and often separate structures) for the main house (主屋 *omoya*) and the kitchen house (釜屋 *kamaya*) |

Among the oldest surviving minka are the **Hakogi family house** (箱木家住宅, Kobe) and the **Furui family house** (古井家住宅, Hyōgo), both called 千年家 (*sennen-ya*, "thousand-year houses") and generally dated to the Muromachi period (the precise dates are debated).

### 3.3 Gasshō-zukuri (合掌造)
- **Frame:** a lower house frame of posts and beams (with the living floor, doma and hearth) on which the enormous sasu roof stands. The sasu feet are pointed and seated in sockets on the beam ends (R13-013), acting as pins so that the roof can rock slightly relative to the house frame.
- **Attic:** two to four floors of slatted or board flooring inside the roof, used for silkworm rearing and storage; the smoke from the hearth rises through the slatted floors.
- **Roof pitch:** very steep (commonly described as about 60°) to shed heavy snow and drain the thick thatch.
- **Bracing:** long diagonal poles (筋交い-like braces) are lashed in the roof plane; the gable ends are open (boarded or with shōji) for light and ventilation.

**R13-019** — **Must.** In gasshō roofs the lashings at sasu apex, yanaka and rafters shall be inspected and renewed at every re-thatching; neso or ropes that have lost flexibility shall be replaced. *Why:* the lashings are the joinery of the roof; a gasshō roof fails at its ties long before its poles.

### 3.4 Magariya and chūmon-zukuri
- The L-plan joins the stable and the dwelling under one roof, so the valley at the junction (谷 *tani*) of the thatched roofs becomes the critical point for leaks.

**R13-020** — **Should.** Thatched valleys shall be built up with extra thickness and renewed more often than the plain slopes; the valley rafter (谷木) shall be of durable timber and adequately supported. *Why:* all the water from two slopes concentrates in the valley.

### 3.5 Honmune-zukuri and yamato-mune
- **Honmune-zukuri**: the low-pitch board roof (in the past weighted with stones, later tiled) requires a heavier post-and-beam frame and a wagoya-type roof rather than a sasu frame; the gable front with its lattice and *suzume-odoshi* is the formal face. A frequently cited example is the Horiuchi family house (堀内家住宅, Shiojiri).
- **Yamato-mune**: the gable walls (高塀) are thick earth walls rising above the thatch, protecting its edges from wind and fire; the thatch is laid between them; a tiled lower roof (落棟) covers the kitchen with its stoves.

**R13-021** — **Should.** Where a thatched roof meets a masonry-like earth gable wall (yamato-mune), the junction shall be flashed with the wall's tile coping and the thatch finished with a clear drip line away from the wall. *Why:* the junction of the flexible thatch and the rigid wall is where water enters and where the thatch rots first.

## 4. Machiya (町家): townhouses

### 4.1 Kyoto machiya (京町家)
Kyoto townhouses are the best-studied machiya. Plots are narrow and deep (a frontage of about 2–4 ken with a depth of many ken is common — the proverbial 鰻の寝床 *unagi no nedoko*, "eel's bed"). The popular explanation that frontage-based taxation produced this shape has some basis in historical levies on frontage but is often overstated; flag it as a partial explanation.

Principal elements:
- **通り庭 *tōriniwa*** — an earth-floored passage running from the street entrance through to the back of the lot along one side, serving as corridor, kitchen (走り庭 *hashiri-niwa*, with stoves おくどさん *okudo-san* and well) and service route.
- **火袋 *hibukuro*** — the tall open space above the kitchen part of the tōriniwa, rising to the underside of the roof, with the beams and struts (梁組) exposed and a high light/smoke window (天窓 *tenmado*). It ventilates smoke and heat and admits light.
- **Room sequence** on the other side of the tōriniwa: shop (店の間 *mise-no-ma*), middle room (台所 *daidokoro* / 中の間), and the formal back room (奥の間 *oku-no-ma*) facing the rear garden (奥庭 *okuniwa*); a **坪庭 *tsuboniwa*** (small courtyard) between front and back parts gives light and cross-ventilation. Larger houses separate the shop building and the main house with an entrance court — the **表屋造 *omoteya-zukuri***.
- **Facade:** **格子 *kōshi*** (vertical lattices, named by trade: 糸屋格子 *itoya-gōshi*, 米屋格子 *komeya-gōshi*, 酒屋格子 *sakaya-gōshi*, 炭屋格子 *sumiya-gōshi*, and others); **ばったり床几 *battari-shōgi*** (a fold-down bench/display board); **犬矢来 *inuyarai*** (curved bamboo splash guard at the wall base) and 駒寄せ *komayose*.
- **厨子二階 *zushi-nikai*** — a low second floor at the street front used for storage and servants' rooms, lit by **虫籠窓 *mushiko-mado*** — fixed windows with vertical bars formed of timber or bamboo cores wrapped and plastered (塗り込め), like an insect cage. Full-height second floors (本二階) became common from the Meiji period. (The idea that low second floors were required so that townspeople could not look down on passing samurai is widely repeated; its legal basis is uncertain.)
- **卯建 *udatsu*** — a raised fire wall with its own small tiled roof at the ends of the main roof/between neighbouring houses; in later periods, a status symbol (hence the expression うだつが上がらない "never raising one's udatsu" = never prospering). Udatsu townscapes survive notably at Mino (Gifu) and Wakimachi (Tokushima).

**Structure:** a timber frame of posts on a sill or stones, with continuous posts (通し柱 *tōshi-bashira*) at key points and storey posts (管柱 *kuda-bashira*) elsewhere; earth walls (土壁) on bamboo lath (→ Ch. 14); roofs of clay tile (桟瓦) on clay bedding (葺き土). Each house traditionally has its own side walls, even where neighbours stand almost touching.

**R13-022** — **Must.** The hibukuro beam grid shall be designed as a structural diaphragm tying the two side walls of the house at roof level; its beams are tenoned and pinned into the side-wall posts. *Why:* in a narrow, deep, open-fronted machiya, the hibukuro beams are among the few cross-ties between the long side walls; they are not decorative.

**R13-023** — **Should.** Lattice fronts (kōshi) shall be made as removable frames (建具 fixed between posts with grooves or pins), so they can be taken down and replaced. *Why:* machiya facades were designed to be opened (shop fronts) and repaired frequently; permanently fixed lattices rot at the sill and cannot be serviced.

**R13-024** — **Should.** Mushiko-mado shall be made with a rot-resistant core (bamboo or timber), wrapped (e.g. with rope) for key, and plastered in several coats like the walls, with a sloped sill for drainage. *Why:* the mushiko-mado is part of the plastered front and a fire barrier; a bare wooden grille would defeat the fireproof facade.

**R13-025** — **Must.** In a row of attached or nearly attached machiya, no member of one house shall be supported on or fixed to its neighbour's wall or frame. *Why:* each house is an independent structure (and property); interdependent framing transfers settlement and fire damage and complicates repair or demolition of either house.

### 4.2 Dashigeta-zukuri (出桁造) — Edo/Kantō shop-houses
- The eave plate (出桁 *dashigeta*) is carried outside the facade on cantilevered arms (腕木 *udegi*) projecting through or from the front posts, so that deep eaves shelter the shop front without columns at the street edge. The cantilever arms and the eave soffit (often with decorative rafters) are the display of the facade. Typical in Edo-period and Meiji shop-houses of Tokyo and the Kantō.

**R13-026** — **Must.** Udegi supporting a dashigeta shall pass through (or be deeply tenoned into) the front post and be counter-held inside by a beam, joist or the floor frame; the dashigeta sits on the udegi ends with a housing and pin. *Why:* the udegi is a cantilever carrying the whole eave; it needs a back-span anchor (→ Ch. 11 on cantilevered eaves, *dashibari* and *udegi*).

### 4.3 Kurazukuri (蔵造) shop-houses
- Merchant houses built with storehouse techniques — thick earth walls fully plastered, including the eaves and the window shutters — as fire-resistant urban houses. The most famous group is at **Kawagoe** (Saitama), largely rebuilt in this manner after the great fire of 1893. Features: massive ridge (箱棟 *hakomune*) with large ogre tiles (鬼瓦 *onigawara*), black plaster, heavy double-leaf doors (観音開き), plastered eaves.
- The construction rules are those of the dozō (§5).

## 5. Kura (蔵): storehouses

The earth-walled storehouse (**土蔵 *dozō***) is a timber frame completely enveloped in a thick, many-layered clay and lime-plaster skin, designed so that a neighbourhood fire burns around it while the contents survive.

### 5.1 Wall construction (summary; → Ch. 14 for earth-wall technique)
- **Frame:** posts, sills and beams, with nuki and 間渡し (*mawatashi*) as for ordinary earth walls; the timber is completely buried in the wall.
- **Keying:** posts and exposed timbers are wrapped with straw rope (縄巻き *nawamaki*) and hung with bundles of hemp fibre (下げ苧 *sageo*) nailed to the timber, so that the clay grips the wood; bamboo lath (小舞 *komai*) is tied on.
- **Coats:** rough coat (荒壁 *arakabe*) applied from both sides, then repeated leveling and body coats (斑直し *muranaoshi*, 中塗り *nakanuri*), and finally lime plaster (漆喰 *shikkui*) or polished finishes. Total thickness commonly about 20–30 cm (some well over 1 shaku), achieved over months with drying time between coats.
- **Wainscot:** the lower wall is protected by **腰巻 *koshimaki*** — a thickened band, or cladding with **海鼠壁 *namako-kabe*** (square tiles fixed with raised, rounded plaster joints) or removable timber boarding (下見板 *shitami-ita*).
- **Eaves band — 鉢巻 *hachimaki*:** a thickened, stepped plaster band at the top of the wall under the eaves, closing the junction of wall and roof against fire.

**R13-027** — **Must.** Every exposed timber surface to be plastered in a dozō shall be keyed (nawamaki, sageo, or lath) before the first clay coat. *Why:* clay does not adhere to smooth timber; thick coats slump and later fall off in large sheets, exposing the frame to fire.

**R13-028** — **Must.** Each clay coat shall be allowed to dry and crack (and the cracks filled by the next coat) before the next is applied; do not build thickness in few heavy coats. *Why:* thick wet layers shrink excessively and separate; the multiple thin coats with intervening drying are what give a sound, monolithic wall.

### 5.2 Roof: okiyane (置き屋根)
- The roof deck over the storehouse ceiling is itself covered with a thick layer of clay (土居葺き / 屋根土 — a fire-resistant "earth roof"), and over it a separate tiled roof — the **置き屋根 *okiyane*** ("placed roof") — is set on posts or blocks (置屋根束) with an air space between, and only lightly fixed.
- The air gap insulates the store from sun heat and ventilates; in a fire the outer roof may burn or be thrown off while the clay deck protects the interior.

**R13-029** — **Should.** The okiyane shall be supported on posts or blocks standing on the clay-covered deck and fixed only lightly (e.g. by pins into sleepers), leaving a continuous ventilated gap. *Why:* the design intent is that the outer roof is separable; rigid connection through the clay deck creates thermal and fire bridges and cracks the clay.

### 5.3 Doors and openings
- **観音開き扉 *kannon-biraki-tobira*** (double-leaf doors) of timber frame thickly plastered, with **stepped rebates** — the 掛子 (*kakego*) — on the door edges and the frame, typically several steps, which interlock when closed and form a labyrinth seal against smoke and heat. Behind them a sliding plastered or board door (裏白戸 *urajirodo*) and a wire-net door are common.
- Small windows with iron bars and plastered shutters with the same stepped seals.
- **用心土 *yōjin-tsuchi*** — a store of prepared clay kept by the door (often in a pot or recess) with which the closed doors' gaps were sealed (目塗り *menuri*) when fire approached.

**R13-030** — **Must.** The stepped rebates of kura doors and shutters shall be made so that the steps of leaf and frame engage fully with even clearances all round, checked with the doors closed after the final plaster coat. *Why:* the stepped seal is the fire barrier; a door that closes only on its outer step lets flame and hot gas through the rebate.

**R13-031** — **Should.** Kura doors of this weight shall hang on wrought-iron hinges fixed into the timber door frame (not the plaster) and the frame shall be tied into the wall frame at several points. *Why:* the plastered door leaves are very heavy; hinges anchored only in plaster pull out.
