# Chapter 04 — Tools and Workshop Process (大工道具と刻みの工程 / *daiku dōgu to kizami no kōtei*)

**Scope.** This chapter describes the Japanese carpenter's tool kit and the rules for its use and care, and the workshop sequence by which marked timber becomes a frame ready for erection. It covers:

- measuring and marking tools, story poles and templates;
- saws, planes, chisels, axes, adzes and the spear plane, hammers and awls;
- steel, forging and sharpening;
- powered equivalents and CNC pre-cutting, and what they change;
- the people of the workshop and their historical roles;
- the step-by-step *kizami* process from *itazu* to transport;
- work-holding, body technique and cutting order;
- surface finishing and traditional coatings.

Some subjects are covered elsewhere. Marking symbols, units and the *sashigane* scales are in → Ch. 02. Timber selection and orientation are in → Ch. 03. Joint geometry is in → Ch. 06–09. Erection is in → Ch. 15.

## 1. Principles of the Japanese tool kit

Five features distinguish the Japanese kit and govern how it is used.

1. **Pull stroke.** Saws and planes cut on the pull (引く *hiku*). A blade loaded in tension cannot buckle, so saw plates can be thin, kerfs narrow, and the carpenter's body pulls toward itself, low and stable. Chisels and adzes are struck or swung toward the user's own body line.
2. **Laminated steel.** Cutting edges are a thin layer of very hard steel (鋼 *hagane*) forge-welded to a soft iron or mild steel body (地金 *jigane*). The hard layer takes a keen edge. The soft body absorbs shock and makes sharpening quicker.
3. **The flat back (*ura*, 裏).** Planes and chisels have a hollow-ground back, so only a narrow margin has to be kept flat. Sharpening accuracy starts from this back.
4. **Tools are set up by the user.** A new plane is a blade and a blank body. The carpenter fits the blade to the body (*dai*, 台), trues the sole, and maintains it for its life. Handles are fitted and hoops seated by the user. A plane is not ready to use as bought.
5. **A large, specialised kit.** A survey reported by the Takenaka Carpentry Tools Museum (竹中大工道具館, Kobe) counted a standard carpenter's kit of the early 1940s at about 179 items. Treat the figure as reported; it illustrates scale, not a rule.

**R04-001** — **Must.** Every edge tool is kept sharp enough to cut end grain of sugi cleanly without crushing, before it is used on *keshō* (exposed) work.
*Why:* crushed fibres show as dull, fuzzy surfaces on white wood, which cannot be hidden with a finish.

**R04-002** — **Should.** Each carpenter sets up, sharpens and maintains his own tools. Tools are not lent for *keshō* work.
*Why:* a plane's dai and a chisel's edge are tuned to one user's hand and sharpening habit.

## 2. Measuring, marking and levelling tools; story poles and templates

### 2.1 Carpenter's square (曲尺 *sashigane*, also *kanejaku*)

- **Form:** a thin L-shaped steel square. The long arm (長手 *nagate*) is typically 1 *shaku* 5 *sun* (≈ 50 cm), and the short arm (妻手 *tsumade*) about half that. The face (表目 *omote-me*) carries ordinary graduations. The back (裏目 *ura-me*) carries the √2-scaled scale (角目 *kakume*) and the π-scale for circumference (丸目 *marume*).
- **Uses:** squaring; laying out slopes by rise per *shaku* (勾配 *kōbai*, → Ch. 05); finding the side of the largest square obtainable from a log (the *ura-me* read across the diameter at the small end); dividing widths; drawing curves by flexing the arm.
- **Detailed use:** → Ch. 02 and Ch. 05.

**R04-003** — **Must.** Check the *sashigane* for square against a trued reference, and replace it if bent. Never use it as a lever, scraper or straightedge for knife cuts.
*Why:* its thin arms deflect permanently, and every layout made with it inherits the error.

### 2.2 Ink line and ink pen (墨壺 *sumitsubo* and 墨差し *sumisashi*)

- **Sumitsubo:** a wooden ink pot with a silk or synthetic thread (糸 *ito*) wound on a reel (車 *kuruma*). The thread passes through an ink-soaked cotton wad (綿 *wata*) in the pot and ends in a pin (軽子 *karuko*). The carpenter pins the end, stretches the line, lifts it and snaps it (墨を打つ *sumi o utsu*) to leave a straight line. Traditional sumitsubo are often carved in keyaki in auspicious forms (crane, tortoise). Owning a first sumitsubo is a milestone in a carpenter's career.
- **Sumisashi:** a bamboo pen. One end is flattened into a chisel-edged spatula cut with fine slits to hold ink, for straight lines drawn against the *sashigane*. The other end is pointed and round for characters and symbols (合印 *aijirushi*, 番付 *bantsuke*, → Ch. 02). Carpenters make their own and re-cut them constantly.
- **Ink:** traditionally *sumi* (carbon ink). Red (朱 *shu*) is used for corrections or second-phase lines. Modern users sometimes use pencil or ballpoint on planed *keshō* faces where ink would stain. Practice varies.

**R04-004** — **Must.** Snap long lines (center lines 芯墨 *shinzumi*, level lines 陸墨 *rokuzumi*, 水墨 *mizuzumi*) with the line held taut and plucked **perpendicular to the surface**.
*Why:* a line plucked sideways leaves a curved mark.

**R04-005** — **Should.** On *keshō* faces, keep ink marks to the minimum needed and place them where the finish planing will remove them, or inside joints. Never write *bantsuke* on a visible face that will not be planed afterward.
*Why:* sumi soaks into white wood and cannot be planed out without losing dimension.

### 2.3 Marking gauges (罫引 *kebiki*)

| Type | Japanese | Form | Use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Line gauge | 筋罫引 *suji-kebiki* | One or two beams (棹 *sao*) through a fence (定規 *jōgi*), each with a small blade or pin | Scribing lines parallel to an edge: tenon cheeks, groove walls, chamfer limits |
| Double-beam / mortise gauge | 二本棹罫引 | Two beams set independently | Marking both walls of a mortise or tenon at one pass |
| Sickle gauge | 鎌罫引 *kama-kebiki* | A type of line gauge whose cutter projects on a hook-shaped (sickle) arm beyond the fence | Marking small tenons and mortises and lines in awkward places. Two-blade versions mark both mortise walls at once |
| Slitting gauge | 割罫引 *wari-kebiki* | A heavy, deep-cutting blade | Slitting thin boards (up to ≈ 6 *bu*, ≈ 18 mm) along the grain into strips: *shōji* bars (*kumiko*), laths, battens |
| Groove gauge | 溝罫引 | Two blades | Scoring the walls of grooves before ploughing |

Fences and beams are made of hard, stable wood (kashi, *isunoki* 柞). Beams are wedged or screwed.

**R04-006** — **Should.** Mark joints whose width must match (tenon and mortise, tongue and groove) with the **same gauge setting**, ideally gauged from the same reference face (基準面 *kijun-men*) of both members.
*Why:* two separate settings accumulate two errors. One setting guarantees a match.

**R04-007** — **Should.** Use a cutting gauge rather than a pencil or ink line where a crisp shoulder or wall is needed (tenon shoulders across grain, chamfer stops, *kumiko* laps).
*Why:* the scored line severs surface fibres, so the saw or chisel registers in it and the arris does not tear.

### 2.4 Plumb bob, water level and modern instruments

- **Plumb bob (下げ振り *sagefuri*):** a brass or iron bob on a thread. It is used to plumb posts during erection (→ Ch. 15). With a stand-off block (下げ振り定規 *sagefuri-jōgi*) the thread hangs at a fixed offset from the post face, so plumb can be read at the top and bottom of the post.
- **Water level (水盛り *mizumori*):** the traditional way to establish level on site. A long trough (水盛り台 *mizumori-dai*) filled with water gives a level surface. Floats with sighting pins mark the level onto stakes (→ Ch. 10, Ch. 15). The hose water level (水盛り管 *mizumori-kan*, a flexible tube filled with water) transfers level between distant points. From these marks the *mizunuki* (水貫, level boards on stakes, part of the *yarikata* 遣り方 setting-out frame) are fixed.
- **Modern:** automatic optical levels, rotating and line lasers, and total stations are now standard for setting out. Traditional water levelling remains valid and is used in restoration and as a check.

**R04-008** — **Must.** Take all heights on site from one bench mark (基準点 *kijun-ten*), transferred by water level or instrument to the *yarikata*. Never chain heights from one post top to the next.
*Why:* chained measurements accumulate error around the building.

### 2.5 Story poles and rods (尺杖 *shakuzue*, 間竿 *kenzao*, 馬鹿棒 *bakabō*)

Before drawings were printed, and still today for repetitive work, dimensions are transferred by **rods** rather than by reading numbers.

| Rod | Japanese | Content | Use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Height rod | 尺杖 *shakuzue* | Full-size heights of one building section: top of foundation stone, *dodai*, floor, *shikii*, *uchinori* (内法, head height of openings, e.g. 5 *shaku* 7–8 *sun*), *kamoi*, *nageshi*, ceiling, *keta*, eaves | Marking post heights and mortise positions, identical for all posts of one type |
| Grid rod | 間竿 *kenzao* | The *ken* modules of the plan (e.g. 6 *shaku* 3 *sun* in *kyō-ma*, 6 *shaku* in *inaka-ma*, → Ch. 02) | Setting out the grid on the *yarikata* and checking spans of *keta* and beams |
| Section rod / section drawing | 矩計 *kanabakari* | Height and section details of the wall, drawn on a board or rod | Reference for *shakuzue* and for coordinating trades |
| "Fool's stick" | 馬鹿棒 *bakabō* | Any rod cut or marked for one repeated dimension, so that "even a fool" gets it right | Spacing rafters, *kumiko*, floor joists; checking repeat heights |

**R04-009** — **Must.** All posts of the same type are marked from the **same shakuzue**, not by measuring each one separately with a tape.
*Why:* a rod reproduces the same dimension every time. Separate tape readings do not.

**R04-010** — **Should.** Keep the *shakuzue* and *kenzao* for the duration of the job (and archive them for important buildings). They are primary documents of the building's dimensions, often more reliable than drawings (→ Ch. 16).

### 2.6 Templates (型板 *kataita*) and full-size drawings (原寸図 *genzunzu*)

- **Templates** of thin board (sugi or plywood) record repeated profiles: *kibana* (木鼻, carved beam ends), *kaerumata*, *kōryō* (虹梁, rainbow beam) camber and *sode-kiri* (袖切, the rounded shoulders cut at the ends of a *kōryō*), eave curves (反り *sori*), *taruki* end profiles, bracket arms (*hijiki*), and *masu* (bearing block) profiles.
- **Full-size drawings** (*genzunzu*) are drawn on a large floor of boards (原寸場 *genzunba*) for roof corners, hip and valley members (*sumigi*, *tanigi*), curved eaves and *kumimono* bracket sets (→ Ch. 05, Ch. 11, Ch. 12). Members are laid on or offered up to the drawing, and the lines are transferred directly.

**R04-011** — **Should.** Make a template for any profile repeated more than a few times, and for every curved or compound member of a roof corner. Develop the template from a full-size drawing, not from scaled drawings.
*Why:* scaled drawings multiply errors, and curves are rarely dimensioned adequately.

## 3. Saws (鋸 *nokogiri*)

### 3.1 Tooth geometry: rip versus crosscut

| | Rip teeth 縦挽き *tatebiki* | Crosscut teeth 横挽き *yokobiki* |
|---|---|---|
| Cuts | Along the fibres | Across the fibres |
| Action | Each tooth works like a small chisel, paring and lifting fibres out of the kerf | Each tooth works like a knife, severing fibres at both kerf walls before the waste between them is removed |
| Filing | Filed essentially straight across. Front face (*shitame*, 下目) near upright or slightly raked. Little or no side bevel | Teeth filed with steep alternate bevels on the top (上目 *uwame*) and front (下目 *shitame*), giving sharp knife points. Tall, narrow teeth |
| Tooth size | Larger. On *ryōba* the rip teeth are graded, finer near the handle (元 *moto*) and coarser toward the tip (先 *saki*) | Uniform, finer |
| Set (あさり *asari*) | Alternate set, modest | Alternate set. Fine saws have very little |

**R04-012** — **Must.** Use rip teeth for cuts along the grain (tenon cheeks, ripping boards, long cheeks of splices) and crosscut teeth for cuts across the grain (shoulders, end cuts). On angled cuts use the tooth type whose direction is closer to the cut. For cuts at 45° either may be used; for *keshō* shoulders use crosscut.
*Why:* rip teeth used across the grain tear the fibres at the kerf edge. Crosscut teeth used with the grain cut slowly and clog.

### 3.2 Saw types

| Saw | Japanese | Form | Use and rules |
|---|---|---|---|
| Double-edged saw | 両刃鋸 *ryōba-noko* | Rip teeth on one edge, crosscut on the other. Blade ≈ 210–300 mm (7 *sun* – 1 *shaku*) for general work, larger for framing. Came into general use in the Meiji era | General cutting: lengths, tenon cheeks and shoulders in framing. Used for cheeks with the rip edge down, then turned for shoulders |
| Single-edged saw | 片刃鋸 *kataba-noko* | Rip or crosscut, one edge. Deeper blade | Deep cuts where a *ryōba*'s upper teeth would foul, and heavy framing cuts |
| Backed saw | 胴付鋸 *dōzuki-noko* | Thin plate stiffened by a steel or brass spine (背金 *segane*). Very fine crosscut (or rip) teeth. Named for the tenon shoulder (胴付 *dōzuki*) | Tenon shoulders, *keshō* crosscuts, dovetails, *tategu* and *kumiko*. Depth of cut limited by the spine |
| Grooving/plunge saw | 畔挽鋸 *azebiki-noko* | Short, convex-edged blade with teeth on both edges (rip and crosscut) on a long neck | Starting cuts in the middle of a surface: walls of housings and grooves, dovetail housings (*ari-mizo*), *shikii* groove ends. Plunges without an entry cut from the edge |
| Kamoi saw | 鴨居挽鋸 *kamoibiki-noko* | Narrow rip saw adapted to cutting in the face of a board | Cutting the walls of grooves in *kamoi* and similar members, and reportedly for cutting *sewari* kerfs in posts. Usage of the name varies |
| Nose saw / keyhole crosscut | 穴挽鋸 *anabiki-noko* (鼻丸鋸 *hanamaru-noko*) | Stout crosscut saw with a rounded nose | Cut-outs, holes, rough crosscutting of large sections and cutting-in from a bored hole. **Note:** the name *anabiki* is not the Japanese term for Western-style pit sawing |
| Turning / compass saw | 挽廻し鋸 *hikimawashi-noko* (also 廻し挽き *mawashibiki*) | Narrow tapering blade | Curves: *kibana* profiles, *kaerumata*, openings in *ranma*, cut-outs in boards |
| Pierce saw | 突廻し鋸 *tsukimawashi-noko* | Very narrow blade | Starting inside a bored hole, tight curves |
| Two-man frame rip saw | 大鋸 *ōga* | Large blade in a frame, worked by two sawyers | Historical conversion of logs into boards and squares (c. 14th–15th c. onward; dates approximate) |
| One-man broad rip saw | 前挽大鋸 *maebiki-ōga* | Very broad, heavy, curved-edge rip blade with a handle at right angles | The *kobiki*'s (sawyer's) principal tool for ripping logs into boards and squares, from about the 15th–16th c. until the spread of power mills in the late 19th–20th c. The log was propped at an angle on trestles and sawn by one man |
| Felling/bucking saw | 窓鋸 *madonoko*, 玉切り鋸 | Large crosscut with gullets ("windows") that clear sawdust | Felling and bucking (→ Ch. 03 §5) |

### 3.3 Saw maintenance

- Traditional saws are sharpened and set by a specialist saw-doctor (目立て屋 *metate-ya*), who also tensions the plate by hammering (腰入れ *koshi-ire*). Few carpenters sharpen their own framing saws, though many touch up fine saws.
- **Replaceable-blade saws** (替刃鋸 *kaeba-noko*), with impulse-hardened teeth (衝撃焼入れ), now dominate everyday use. Their teeth cannot be refiled; they are discarded when dull. For fine traditional work many carpenters still keep a set of forged, refileable saws.

**R04-013** — **Should.** Keep separate saws for *keshō* crosscuts (fine, sharp, little set) and for rough work. Never use the fine *keshō* saw on dirty or reclaimed timber.
*Why:* grit and nails ruin the fine teeth, and the next *keshō* cut then tears.

## 4. Planes (鉋 *kanna*)

### 4.1 Anatomy and set-up

```
       Side section of a two-blade plane (nimai-ganna), pulled toward the user ←

                      atama (blade head: tapped to advance)
                       \
                        \  kanna-mi (blade)       uragane (chip breaker)
           dai-gashira   \\_____________________ //
            (front)    ___\\___________________//______________  dai (oak body)
                      |    \\  osae-mizo       /               |
                      |_____\\____osaebō_____/________________| ← shitaba (sole)
                             ^ haguchi (mouth)            dai-jiri (tail: tap to retract)
```

- **Blade (鉋身 *kanna-mi*):** tapered in width and thickness, laminated, with a hollow back (*ura*).
- **Chip breaker (裏金 *uragane*):** a second blade set just behind the cutting edge. Its edge breaks and curls the shaving before a split can run ahead into the wood, which reduces tear-out. The two-blade plane (二枚鉋 *nimai-ganna*) spread in Japan from about the end of the Meiji period (c. 1890s–1910s; date approximate). Before that, single-blade planes (一枚鉋 *ichimai-ganna*) were used, and they still are for some finishing and specialist work.
- **Body (台 *dai*):** traditionally white oak (白樫 *shirakashi*), or red oak (赤樫 *akagashi*) for harder wear. The blade is held by friction in grooves (押さえ溝 *osae-mizo*) at the bedding angle. The chip breaker is held by a pin (押さえ棒 *osaebō*).
- **Bedding angle (仕込み勾配 *shikomi kōbai*):** commonly *8 sun kōbai* (8/10 rise, ≈ 38–39°) for general softwork. It is steeper, *8.5–9 sun kōbai* (≈ 40–42°) or up to 45° (*kane kōbai*), for hardwoods and figured grain, where a steeper cutting angle reduces tear-out.

**Adjustment:** tap the blade head (頭 *atama*) to advance the blade. Tap the rear top edge of the dai (台尻 *dai-jiri*), on its corner or centre, to retract. Tap the side of the blade head to adjust lateral skew. The chip breaker is tapped to set it.

**Chip-breaker setting (indicative):**
- Finishing: ≈ 0.05–0.1 mm behind the edge.
- Medium: ≈ 0.2–0.3 mm.
- Roughing: ≈ 0.5–1 mm, or backed off entirely.

### 4.2 Truing the sole (台直し *dainaoshi*)

The oak body moves with humidity and wears in use. A Japanese plane sole is **not** fully flat. It is relieved so that it bears only in defined zones, and the relief is a very small amount, on the order of a paper's thickness (≈ 0.05–0.1 mm).

- **Roughing and intermediate planes (荒 *ara*, 中 *chū* / 中しこ *nakashiko*):** three bearing zones: the front edge (台頭 *dai-gashira*), just ahead of the mouth (刃口 *haguchi*), and the tail (台尻 *dai-jiri*).
- **Finishing plane (仕上げ *shiage*):** two bearing zones. Schools differ. The commonest teaching keeps the zone just ahead of the mouth and one end zone, relieving the rest.
- The sole is checked with a straightedge (下端定規 *shitaba-jōgi*) against the light, and scraped with a scraper-plane whose blade stands at about 90° (台直し鉋 *dainaoshi-ganna*, also 立鉋 *tachi-ganna*).
- The sole is always trued **with the blade in place, retracted slightly** from cutting position. Inserting the blade wedges the dai and changes its shape.

**R04-014** — **Must.** Check the plane sole before each finishing session and after any significant change in humidity (season, moving from shop to site). Re-true it with the blade installed.

**R04-015** — **Should.** Keep a graded set of planes for each stage: rough (荒), intermediate (中), finishing (仕上げ), and for top work an extra-fine finishing plane (上仕上げ *jō-shiage*). Each is tuned for its stage (mouth opening, chip-breaker setting, sole relief). Do not adjust one plane back and forth between stages.

### 4.3 Plane types

| Plane | Japanese | Form | Use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Flat (smoothing) plane | 平鉋 *hira-ganna* | Standard. Named by nominal blade size, e.g. 寸八 *sun-hachi* (the usual general size, blade ≈ 70 mm), 寸六 *sun-roku* (≈ 65 mm); nominal names do not equal actual widths | Surfacing, sizing, finishing |
| Long jointer | 長台鉋 *naga-dai-ganna* | Long body (≈ 1.5–2 *shaku* and more) | Straightening edges and long faces; jointing boards; truing *shikii*, *kamoi* and *kumiko* stock |
| Sole-truing plane | 台直し鉋 *dainaoshi-ganna* | Blade near vertical | Scraping plane soles (§4.2) |
| Edge / corner plane | 際鉋 *kiwa-ganna* | Blade skewed and exposed at one side (left and right versions) | Cleaning into inside corners, rebates and the edges of housings |
| Side-rebate / groove-wall plane | 脇取鉋 *wakitori-ganna* | Narrow, cutting on the side (left/right) | Widening or trimming the walls of grooves (e.g. *shikii* grooves) |
| Compass (convex-sole) plane | 反り台鉋 *soridai-ganna* | Sole curved lengthwise | Concave surfaces: cambered undersides, curved eaves members, *kōryō* soffits |
| Round-sole / hollow planes | 丸鉋 *maru-ganna* (内丸 *uchimaru*, 外丸 *sotomaru*) | Sole and blade curved across | Concave and convex mouldings; round posts |
| Spokeshave | 南京鉋 *nankin-ganna* | Two handles, short sole | Curves, round rods, *kibana* edges, handles |
| Chamfer plane | 面取り鉋 *mentori-ganna* | Fixed fence and depth stop; many profiles (→ §13.3) | Chamfers and small mouldings on arrises |
| Grooving plane | 溝鉋 *mizo-ganna* (also 決り鉋 *shakuri-ganna*) | Narrow blade with fence | Grooves in *shikii* and *kamoi*, panel grooves |
| Groove-bottom plane (router plane) | 底取り鉋 *sokotori-ganna* | Blade projecting below a broad sole | Leveling the bottom of housings and grooves to a set depth |
| Rebate plane | 相决り鉋 *aijakuri-ganna* (rebate-plane family; names vary regionally) | Blade the full width of the sole | Rebates (*shakuri*) for lapped boards, *hame-ita* |

**R04-016** — **Should.** Plane with the grain (順目 *naraime*). A first guess from orientation: on the *kiomote* face plane from *sue* toward *moto*, and on the *kiura* face from *moto* toward *sue*. Always confirm by reading the grain on the edge, since local grain overrides the rule.
*Why:* planing against the grain (逆目 *sakame*) tears fibres below the surface.

**R04-017** — **Should.** Where the grain reverses (around knots, figured grain), close the chip breaker, reduce the depth of cut, use a plane with a steeper bedding angle, or skew the plane.

## 5. Chisels (鑿 *nomi*)

### 5.1 Anatomy

- **Blade:** laminated, with hollow *ura*. The **neck** (首 *kubi*) runs to the **tang** (込み *komi*), which enters the handle (柄 *e*) through a ferrule (口金 *kuchigane*).
- **Struck chisels** (叩き鑿 *tataki-nomi*) have an iron hoop (冠 *kan*, 桂 *katsura*) at the head of the handle. The hoop is fitted so that the end grain of the handle stands about 1–2 mm proud. The protruding wood is then peened over the hoop by a few blows (the handle's own *kigoroshi*), so that the hoop cannot fly off and the handle end does not split.
- **Handles:** red or white oak, or *gumi* (茱萸, silverberry), traditionally.

**R04-018** — **Must.** Seat the hoop on a new or re-fitted *tataki-nomi* by compressing the handle end over it before use. Re-seat it when the hoop loosens.
*Why:* a loose hoop splits the handle and can fly off under a heavy blow.

### 5.2 Chisel types

| Chisel | Japanese | Form | Use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bench chisel (general, struck) | 追入鑿 *oire-nomi* | Short blade. Sets typically of ten widths from 1 *bu* (≈ 3 mm) to 1 *sun* 4 *bu* (≈ 42 mm) | General joinery, furniture, *tategu*, small housings |
| Framing chisel, long | 大入鑿 *ōire-nomi* | As *oire* but longer blade and handle | Timber-frame housings and tenons |
| Heavy framing chisel | 厚鑿 *atsu-nomi*, 叩き鑿 in the narrow sense | Thick, heavy, wide | Framing: large housings, splice seats, heavy waste removal with a heavy *gennō* |
| Mortise chisel | 向待鑿 *mukōmachi-nomi* | Narrow, very deep (thick) blade, parallel sides, strong neck | Deep, narrow mortises: *nuki* holes, *komisen* holes, tenon mortises. Levers out waste without bending |
| Paring chisel | 突鑿 *tsuki-nomi* | Long blade and long handle, no hoop. Pushed by hand and shoulder, never struck. Wide (≈ 1–2 *sun*) | Paring the faces of tenons, housings and splices to the line; finishing mortise walls; flattening seats |
| Dovetail / bevel-edge chisel | 鎬鑿 *shinogi-nomi* | Triangular section with sharp ridge-bevels | Acute corners of dovetail housings (*ari*), *kama* heads |
| Cranked-neck chisel | 鏝鑿 *kote-nomi* | Neck cranked like a trowel, so the blade lies flat below the handle | Flattening the bottoms of wide housings and grooves where a straight chisel's handle would foul |
| Knock-through chisel | 打抜鑿 *uchinuki-nomi* | Blunt, stout | Driving waste out of a through-mortise after both faces are cut |
| Bottom-clearing chisel | 底浚い鑿 *soko-sarai-nomi* | Bent, spoon-like end | Clearing and flattening the bottoms of blind mortises |
| Gouges | 丸鑿 *maru-nomi* (内丸 *uchimaru* / 外丸 *sotomaru*) | Curved cross-section | Carving, round housings, *kibana* profiles |

**R04-019** — **Must.** Never strike a *tsuki-nomi* (paring chisel) or any chisel without a hoop.
*Why:* the handle splits and the long blade's tang loosens.

**R04-020** — **Should.** Size the mortise chisel to the mortise: use a *mukōmachi* of exactly the mortise width wherever the mortise width is a standard size (e.g. *nuki* thickness), so the walls are defined by the chisel itself.
*Why:* a mortise cut with an undersized chisel and widened by paring is rarely parallel.

## 6. Axes, adze and spear plane

### 6.1 Axes (斧 *ono*, 鉞 *masakari*, 与岐 *yoki*)

| Tool | Japanese | Form | Use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Felling axe | 与岐 / 斧 *yoki* | Narrow, heavy bit | Felling, notching, splitting (→ Ch. 03) |
| General axe | 斧 *ono* | Medium bit | Splitting, rough shaping |
| Broad axe / hewing axe | 鉞 *masakari* | Broad bit, one-sided bevel in hewing forms | Hewing logs to flat faces (はつり *hatsuri*) before adze or plane finishing |
| Splitting wedges | 楔 *kusabi*, 矢 *ya* | Iron or oak | Riving logs (打ち割り *uchiwari*, → Ch. 03 §5.5) |

### 6.2 Adze (釿 / 手斧 *chōna*)

- **Form:** a curved blade on a long, bent handle, the blade across the line of swing. The carpenter stands on or astride the timber and swings toward his own feet, taking controlled scallops.
- **Uses:**
  - **Leveling and dressing** hewn faces of beams and posts (primary historical use), after axe hewing and before planing.
  - **Decorative texture** (名栗 *naguri*): regular faceted scallops left as the finished surface on posts, boards, beams and floorboards, especially in *sukiya* and tea architecture (kuri is the classic species, → Ch. 03).
  - **Ceremony:** the *chōna-hajime* (釿始め), the first ceremonial adze strokes at the start of construction of a shrine or temple (→ Ch. 15).
- **Chōna-hatsuri texture:** the surface shows shallow, overlapping, lens-shaped facets in regular rows. Size and rhythm vary with the carpenter; they are a signature.

**R04-021** — **Must.** When adzing, keep the leading foot behind the line of the stroke and wear protective footwear. Work toward yourself only with the blade path clear of the feet.
*Why:* the adze is the most dangerous tool in the kit.

**R04-022** — **Should.** For *naguri* finishes, lay out rows and spacing so the scallops are regular across the member, and adze **before** cutting the joints on that face, leaving the joint zones flat.
*Why:* joints need flat reference faces. Adzing after cutting damages arrises.

### 6.3 Spear plane (槍鉋 *yariganna*)

- **Form:** a spear-shaped, slightly curved double-edged blade on a long handle. It is pulled toward the user along the grain, shaving the surface with a slight rolling action.
- **History:** the principal surface-finishing tool in ancient Japan. It fell out of use after the bench plane (台鉋 *dai-ganna*) spread, around the 15th–16th centuries (dates approximate), though it survived in some specialist uses. **Nishioka Tsunekazu** revived it for the Yakushiji reconstruction in the 1970s–80s, studying tool marks on ancient members and having blades forged by smiths, notably Shirataka Yukinori (白鷹幸伯), who also forged wrought-iron nails for the Yakushiji work. It is now used in restoration of ancient buildings.
- **Texture:** a subtly rippled, silky, lustrous surface with shallow long facets. Nishioka and others held that it sheds water better than a flat planed surface, since the fibres are sliced along their length and not crossed. Treat this as a practitioner's claim.

**R04-023** — **Should (restoration of ancient buildings).** Where original members show *yariganna* or *chōna* marks, finish new replacement members with the same tool, not with a bench plane.
*Why:* surface texture is part of the historic fabric and the evidence of period working (→ Ch. 16).

## 7. Hammers, awls and small tools

### 7.1 Hammer (玄能 / 玄翁 *gennō*)

- **Head forms:** double-faced (両口 *ryōguchi*), single-faced with a pointed or wedge end (片口 *kataguchi*), octagonal (八角 *hakkaku*), round (丸 *maru*), barrel-shaped (太鼓 *taiko*). Weights run from about 150 g for fine work to over 1.1 kg for framing. Traditional weights are in *monme* (匁, ≈ 3.75 g), e.g. 100 *monme* ≈ 375 g.
- **The two faces:** one face is **flat** (平面), the other slightly **domed / convex** (木殺し面 *kigoroshi-men*).
  - **Flat face:** striking chisels. Flat-on-flat gives the most direct, square blow.
  - **Domed face:** the final blows on a nail head, which drive it flush without the hammer's edge bruising the wood around it. The deliberate compression of wood at joints (*kigoroshi*, 木殺し, → Ch. 06). Seating members without leaving crescent marks. Settling a joint at the end of assembly.
- **Handle:** usually *kashi*, fitted by the user. Length is often chosen to suit the user's forearm. The handle is shaped slightly thinner near the head to give spring.

**R04-024** — **Must.** Strike chisels with the flat face. Use the domed face for final nail blows, *kigoroshi*, and any blow that lands on a finished (*keshō*) surface.
*Why:* the flat face transmits the blow squarely to the chisel handle. The domed face cannot leave an edge mark on the wood.

**R04-025** — **Should.** For *kigoroshi*, compress the tenon or tongue with the domed face evenly over its surface, working toward the shoulder. Do not crush the arrises that must stay crisp.

**Wooden mallets** (木槌 *kizuchi*, 掛矢 *kakeya*: the large mallet used in *tatemae*) are used for driving members together and for seating splices (→ Ch. 15).

### 7.2 Awls and boring tools (錐 *kiri*)

| Awl | Japanese | Point | Use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Three-edged awl | 三つ目錐 *mitsume-giri* | Triangular | Pilot holes for nails and screws, especially in hardwood and near ends |
| Four-edged awl | 四つ目錐 *yotsume-giri* | Square | Pilot holes for nails, holes for small pins |
| Cup awl | 壺錐 *tsubo-giri* | Semicircular cutting edges | Flat-bottomed round holes of fixed size: dowels (*dabo*), small pins, and the pin holes of *tategu* |
| Mouse-tooth awl | ねずみ歯錐 *nezumiba-giri* | Forked spur point | Clean holes in thin boards and bamboo |

Awls are spun between the palms (揉み錐 *momi-giri*): the hands run down the shaft and return quickly to the top. For larger holes, historic braces (繰子錐 *kuriko-giri*) and augers were used; today, drills.

**R04-026** — **Should.** Pre-bore nail and pin holes in hardwoods and near member ends (kuri, keyaki, kashi, and in sugi and hinoki within about 5 nail diameters of an end).
*Why:* to prevent splitting (→ Ch. 09).

### 7.3 Other small tools

Knives (切出し *kiridashi*); scrapers; nail sets (釘締め *kugishime*); pincers (やっとこ, 釘抜き *kuginuki*); files and rasps; straightedges (定規 *jōgi*, 直定規); bevel gauges (自由金 *jiyūgane*, 自由矩); inside calipers; clamps (a Western addition, rarely used in framing); carpenter's pencils; *kasugai* (鎹, iron dogs) used temporarily to hold members during *kizami* and erection.

## 8. Steel, forging and sharpening

### 8.1 Laminated construction

```
        Cross-section of a laminated chisel / plane blade (edge toward viewer)

         ______________________________
        |          jigane (地金)        |   soft iron / low-carbon steel (thick)
        |______________________________|
        |__________hagane (鋼)_________|   hard high-carbon steel (thin)
              \___ ura (裏, hollowed) __/   the back: flat margin (糸裏 ito-ura) around a hollow (裏すき ura-suki)
```

- **Hagane (hard steel):** traditionally *tamahagane* (玉鋼), bloomery steel from the *tatara* furnace. In modern tools the usual steels are the Yasugi steels (安来鋼), *shirogami* (白紙, "white paper", plain high-carbon) and *aogami* (青紙, "blue paper", with chromium and tungsten), and various carbon tool steels. Quenched hardness is typically around HRC 60–65 (approximate).
- **Jigane (soft iron):** traditionally wrought iron (錬鉄), including prized old stock recycled from ships' anchors, chains and bridges. Today, low-carbon steel.
- **Forging:** the steel is forge-welded (鍛接 *tansetsu*) to the iron with a flux, drawn out, shaped, hollowed on the back, coated with clay, water-quenched (水焼入れ) and tempered (焼戻し).
- **Why laminate:** the hard edge takes and keeps a very keen edge. The soft body absorbs shock and resists snapping. On the stone, most of the bevel is soft iron, so sharpening is fast, and the flat is registered by the narrow *ura* margin.

### 8.2 The back (*ura*): flattening and restoring

- **Ura:** the back is hollowed (裏すき *ura-suki*), leaving a flat rim (糸裏 *ito-ura*, "thread back") along the edge and sides. Only this rim is flattened.
- **Flattening the back (裏押し *ura-oshi*):** the back is rubbed on a flat steel plate (金盤 *kanaban*) with carborundum powder (金剛砂 *kongōsha*) and water, pressed flat with a stick near the edge. It is then refined on medium and finishing stones.
- **Pushing out the back (裏出し *ura-dashi*):** plane blades only. As a plane blade is sharpened back over years, the flat band at the edge wears away (裏切れ *ura-gire*, "back run-out"). To restore it, the blade is laid with its back on the rounded edge of an anvil. The **soft iron (*jigane*) on the bevel side**, just behind the edge, is tapped lightly with a small hammer. This bends the edge region very slightly toward the back, so that a new flat band can be pressed.
- Chisels are generally **not** *ura-dashi*'d. Their backs are re-flattened by *ura-oshi*, and the hollow is re-established as necessary.

**R04-027** — **Must.** In *ura-dashi*, strike only the soft iron (*jigane*) layer, never the hard steel (*hagane*).
*Why:* the hardened steel is brittle and cracks under a direct blow.

**R04-028** — **Must.** Never create a back bevel on the *ura* (by lifting the blade while honing the back). Keep the *ura* dead flat to the edge.
*Why:* the chip breaker and the geometry of the cut depend on a flat back. A rounded back cannot be fixed without heavy regrinding.

### 8.3 Stones (砥石 *toishi*)

| Stage | Japanese | Grit (synthetic, JIS approx.) | Natural examples | Purpose |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Coarse | 荒砥 *ara-to* | ≈ #80–#400 | 大村砥 *Ōmura-do*, 天草砥 *Amakusa-do* (coarse to medium) | Removing chips, reshaping bevels |
| Medium | 中砥 *naka-to* | ≈ #800–#2000 | 青砥 *Ao-to*, 伊予砥 *Iyo-to* | Establishing the edge and burr after coarse work, routine sharpening |
| Finishing | 仕上砥 *shiage-to* | ≈ #3000–#8000 and finer | Kyoto *awase-do* (京都合砥, finishing stones from the mountains north-west of Kyoto; named quarries and strata such as Nakayama 中山, Ōhirayama 大平, Shōbudani 菖蒲谷, Okudo 奥殿; attributions of individual stones are notoriously uncertain) | Final polish of edge and back |
| Slurry stone | 名倉砥 *nagura-do* | — | Mikawa nagura (三河名倉) | Raising slurry on finishing stones |

Stones must be kept **flat**. They are flattened frequently on a flattening plate, a coarse diamond plate, or against each other (面直し *men-naoshi*).

### 8.4 Sharpening sequence and angles

**Sequence (routine):**
1. If the edge is chipped or the bevel is out of shape: coarse stone.
2. Medium stone: bevel held at a constant angle, flat on the stone, until a burr (返り *kaeri*) forms along the whole edge.
3. Finishing stone: bevel, then the *ura* laid dead flat, alternately, until the burr is removed and the edge is polished.
4. For plane blades, polish the chip breaker's edge and check that it seats without a gap.
5. Test on end grain or by shaving.

**Bevel angles (切刃角 *kiriha-kaku*; representative ranges, varying with steel, wood and school):**

| Tool | Typical bevel angle | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Finishing plane (softwood: hinoki, sugi) | ≈ 25–28° | Lower for the keenest cut in softwood |
| General / hardwood plane | ≈ 28–32° | Steeper for keyaki, kashi, knots |
| Paring chisel (*tsuki-nomi*) | ≈ 25–28° | Hand pressure only |
| Bench / framing chisel (*oire*, *ōire*, *atsu*) | ≈ 28–32° | Struck |
| Mortise chisel (*mukōmachi*) | ≈ 30–35° | Heavy levering |
| Chisels for hardwood | +3–5° over the above; some add a small secondary bevel (小刃 *koba*) | Prevents chipping |
| Adze, axes | ≈ 25–35°, axes steeper for splitting | — |

**R04-029** — **Must.** Sharpen the bevel **flat** at a constant angle. Do not rock the blade into a rounded (convex) bevel, except where a small, deliberate secondary bevel (*koba*) is chosen for hardwood chisels.
*Why:* a rounded bevel raises the effective cutting angle, and it cannot be registered for the next sharpening.

**R04-030** — **Should.** Sharpen little and often. Return to the finishing stone as soon as the shaving or the surface deteriorates, before the edge is visibly dull.

**R04-031** — **Must.** Flatten stones regularly, at the latest when a straightedge shows any hollow.
*Why:* a hollow stone makes a convex bevel and a rounded back.

**R04-032** — **Should.** Protect edge tools from rust with camellia oil (椿油 *tsubaki-abura*) after use and in storage. Store planes with the blade retracted (to prevent damage to the edge and uneven pressure on the dai), and chisels in rolls or boxes, never loose.

### 9.2 CNC pre-cutting (プレカット *purekatto*)

Automated pre-cutting lines began in Japan around the 1970s–80s. Since the 2000s they have cut the great majority of wooden post-and-beam house frames; figures of about 90 % or more of *zairai* house frames are commonly cited (approximate). The machine reads CAD data, cuts standard joints in dried, dimensioned stock (often glulam), and marks each member with its grid address.

**What changes with pre-cutting:**
- **Joint repertoire:** mostly *koshikake-ari-tsugi*, *koshikake-kama-tsugi*, *ari-kake*, short tenons (短ほぞ), plus metal connectors (→ Ch. 17). Complex hand joints (*okkake-daisen*, *kanawa*, *shachi-sen* connections, through tenons with wedges) require special machines or hand work.
- **Geometry:** router-cut housings have **rounded inside corners** (tool radius). Mating parts are rounded to match. A hand-cut part does not fit a machine-cut part without adjustment.
- **Material:** pre-cutting assumes dry, straight, dimensionally uniform stock. It cannot read the individual tree (*kusegumi*, → Ch. 03 §11), cannot use crooked logs, and does not decide *moto/sue*, *se*, or *kiomote* unless an operator does.
- **Tolerances:** very consistent, typically fractions of a millimetre. They are designed for tight fit in dry material, with less allowance for *kigoroshi*.

**R04-033** — **Must.** Do not mix hand-cut and machine-cut halves of the same joint without checking the geometry (inside-corner radius, fit allowance). Adjust one half deliberately.

**R04-034** — **Should.** In pre-cut work, an experienced carpenter still checks and assigns *moto/sue*, crown and show faces (→ Ch. 03) before the stock is fed. Hand-mark *keshō* members and crooked or natural logs.
*Why:* the machine places every member as data, not as a tree.

**R04-035** — **May.** Combine machine and hand work: machine roughing of mortises and tenons (*kakunomi*, *hozotori*), with hand finishing and fitting of all *keshō* joints and all joints whose fit depends on *kigoroshi*. This is common practice in present-day traditional workshops.

- **Workshop shed** (下小屋 *shitagoya*, also 作業場 *sagyōba*, 刻み場 *kizami-ba*): a roofed shed with an earth or board floor, where timber is stored, marked and cut. It often has a board floor for full-size drawings (*genzunba*).
- **Timber yard** (材木置場, 土場 *doba*): stickered stacks under cover (→ Ch. 03 §7).

*Why:* each marker's hand differs slightly. A frame marked by several independent people does not close.

## 11. The workshop process (刻み *kizami*), step by step

The sequence below is the typical workflow for a hand-cut frame. It is shown for a house or minka. Shrine and temple work adds full-size drawings, templates and more extensive trial assembly.

### Step 1 — Plans and *itazu*

The *tōryō* receives the client's requirements or the architect's drawings and draws the **itazu** (板図), a plan of the frame on a board (traditionally thin sugi or hinoki, today plywood) at a small scale such as 1:20 (→ Ch. 02). It shows:

- the grid with its addresses (番付 *bantsuke*: i-ro-ha one way, numbers the other);
- posts (through-posts / *tōshibashira* and storey posts / *kudabashira* distinguished);
- beams, *keta* and *dodai*, with directions and splice positions;
- the heights of floors and beams;
- *moto* directions where decided (→ Ch. 03 R03-041).

Separate *itazu* are drawn for each floor and for the roof. The *itazu* stays in the workshop and goes to site for erection.

### Step 2 — Take-off and *kidori* (木拾い *kihiroi*, 木取り *kidori*)

- **Take-off (*kihiroi*):** a list of every member with species, section, length, grade, *keshō/nomono* (→ Ch. 03 §12).
- **Kidori:** allocating actual pieces of stock to specific members. The *tōryō* reads each stick:
  - moisture;
  - *moto/sue*;
  - crown (*se*, *tōge*);
  - bark side;
  - knots and defects;
  - twist;
  - the best faces;
  - growth aspect where known.

  He then assigns it: the best posts to the principal room and *toko*, crooked logs to beams that suit their curve, sticks with knots to positions where the knots fall between joints. The member's address is written on its end or on a hidden face.

**R04-037** — **Must.** Do the *kidori* before any *sumitsuke*, and make it for the whole frame at once, so that the best stock is not consumed by early, unimportant members.

**R04-038** — **Should.** Assign stock in order of importance: first the *keshō* posts and beams of the principal rooms, then other *keshō* members, then primary structure, then *nomono*.

### Step 3 — Rough dressing (荒木取り *ara-kidori*, 荒削り *ara-kezuri*)

- Cut members to rough length with allowance at both ends.
- Dress to section: traditionally by adze and plane, today with a jointer and thicknesser. For squared stock, first plane two adjacent faces **square to each other** as reference faces (基準面 *kijun-men*), then bring the other faces to dimension.
- Leave a **finishing allowance** (仕上げ代 *shiage-shiro*) on *keshō* faces, typically about 1–3 mm total, for the final hand planing after joints are cut.
- Let freshly dressed stock rest (stickered) for some days if possible. Newly exposed surfaces can move, and it is better that they do so before marking.

**R04-039** — **Should.** Establish reference faces before marking, and take all marking from center lines (*shinzumi*) and those reference faces, never from an undressed or finished face.
*Why:* marking from center lines means the finishing allowance, taken equally from both sides, does not shift the joints.

### Step 4 — Marking (墨付け *sumitsuke*)

- Snap **center lines** (*shinzumi*) on all relevant faces of every member, and **level lines** (*rokuzumi*, *mizuzumi*) on posts (e.g. a line at a set height above the floor, from the *shakuzue*).
- Mark all joints from the *itazu*, rods and templates, using the standard marking symbols for:
  - cut / do not cut (the waste side);
  - center lines;
  - mortise through or blind;
  - the *moto* end;
  - the reference face.
- Write the address (*bantsuke*) on each member in a position that will be hidden or planed off. Also write *aijirushi* (matching marks) at splices and at complex joints, so that the matching halves are recognised.
- **Check** each marked member against the *itazu* before it leaves the marker. Many workshops have a second person check critical members.

**R04-040** — **Must.** No member is cut until its marking is complete and checked.
*Why:* a cut cannot be undone, and correction costs a member.

**R04-041** — **Should.** Mark members in the order in which they will be cut, and cut them in erection order (from the *dodai* and posts upward), so that the first members needed on site are finished first and errors found in lower members can be corrected in upper members still to be cut.

### Step 5 — Cutting (刻み *kizami*)

**General order within a member:**
1. **Crosscuts that establish length**, with any end allowance that the joint needs. Never cut a member to final length before the joint at that end is marked.
2. **Saw cuts that define the joint:** shoulders (crosscut), cheeks (rip), the walls of housings (*azebiki* or *ryōba*). Saw on the waste side of the line (§12.3).
3. **Chisel work:** remove waste, chop mortises, cut housings.
4. **Paring to the line:** *tsuki-nomi*, and *kote-nomi* for bottoms, *sokotori-ganna*.
5. **Boring** pin holes (*komisen*, *shachisen*), offset for draw-boring where required (→ Ch. 09).

**Order between the parts of a joint:**
- **Female (女木 *megi*: mortises, housings, seats) is usually cut first; male (男木 *ogi*: tenons, tongues) second.**
  *Why:* chopping mortises involves heavy pounding and levering that can damage finished tenons nearby. The male part is easier to trim for fit, so it is adjusted to the female, not the reverse. Where both halves are cut from one set of lines (the norm in Japanese marking), each half is cut to its own lines. The rule then concerns what to adjust if a fit is tight: adjust the male.
- **Mortises before tenons within a member** that has both (e.g. a post with a mortise and a tenon at its end): cut the mortises while the member is still at full length and full strength, and cut the end tenon last.

**R04-042** — **Should.** Within a member, saw before chiseling: define every shoulder and wall with a saw cut or a knife/chisel cut on the line before removing waste with the chisel.
*Why:* a chisel levering waste out without a defined boundary splits past the line.

**R04-043** — **Should.** Cut the female half of a joint first and fit the male to it. When a joint is too tight, adjust the male.

**R04-044** — **Must.** Cut end tenons, splice tongues and other projecting parts **last** in a member, after all mortises and housings in that member are complete.
*Why:* projecting parts are fragile. A long member is turned and hammered many times during mortising.

**R04-045** — **Must.** Leave the center lines (*shinzumi*) and level lines visible on the finished joint wherever possible (e.g. on the tenon end and on the mortise-face surroundings).
*Why:* they are needed for checking at *karigumi* and at *tatemae*.

### Step 6 — Finishing planing (仕上げ削り *shiage-kezuri*)

- *Keshō* faces are finish-planed **after** the joints are cut and **shortly before erection**. The final pass with a finishing plane takes a thin continuous shaving (thin enough to be translucent), removing the finishing allowance equally from opposing faces.
- *Why after cutting:* the surface is then fresh at erection, free of handling marks, ink, dirt and oxidation. Arrises are chamfered (§13.3) only after the joints at that arris are cut, so the chamfer stops (*men-dome*) can be placed correctly at joint lines.
- *Why not immediately before erection in all cases:* members that will be very hard to reach after erection (e.g. high *keta*, the undersides of eaves) are finish-planed and protected before erection.
- Joints are generally not finish-planed. Cheeks and shoulders are left at their pared or sawn fit.

**R04-046** — **Should.** Finish-plane *keshō* surfaces after all joint cutting on that member, and as late as practical before erection. Wrap the member immediately afterward (Step 8).

**R04-047** — **Must.** Take the finishing allowance equally from opposite faces, so the center line stays the center of the member.
*Why:* joints were marked from center lines.

### Step 7 — Trial assembly (仮組 *karigumi*)

- **Purpose:** to check fit, angles and cumulative dimensions before the site. It is essential where members meet in three dimensions or at angles.
- **When:**
  - **Always:** *kumimono* bracket sets, hip and valley corners (*sumigi*, *tanigi*), curved eaves assemblies (*nokizori*), roof-corner rafters (*sumi-taruki*), complex *shiguchi* at multi-member nodes, *ranma* and *tategu* sets, and any member cut from a template for the first time.
  - **Often:** *keshō* frames of principal rooms (posts, *nageshi*, *kamoi*), small pavilions and gates (assembled entirely in the shop).
  - **Usually not:** simple, repetitive house frames cut by an experienced team from a good *itazu*. Fits are checked member by member instead.
- Trial assembly is done on a level floor or on trestles set level, with the grid marked out. Joints are **not driven home**: no *kigoroshi*, and pins not driven. Joints are assembled only far enough to check fit, then dismantled.

**R04-048** — **Must.** Trial-assemble every hip/valley corner, bracket set, and any three-dimensional multi-member node before sending it to site.

**R04-049** — **Must.** In trial assembly, never fully drive joints that depend on *kigoroshi* or on draw-bore pins.
*Why:* the compressed wood recovers only partly. A joint driven home once in the shop is looser at the real erection.

### Step 8 — Protection and storage (養生 *yōjō*)

- *Keshō* members are wrapped in paper (養生紙 *yōjō-gami*, traditionally Japanese paper or kraft paper, today also cardboard and film). Corners and arrises are protected with board or cardboard angles.
- Members are stored stickered, flat, under cover, off the ground, and out of direct sun. Direct sun darkens white hinoki and sugi within days.
- Joints are kept clean. Tenons are protected from knocks, e.g. by boards, or by nailing a temporary block over a long tenon.
- Hands and gloves must be clean. Oily hands leave marks on *shiraki* that are hard to remove.

**R04-050** — **Must.** Wrap all *keshō* members after final planing, and keep them wrapped until the wall and roof finishes near them are complete (→ Ch. 15).
*Why:* *shiraki* surfaces mark permanently from sun, dirty hands, rain spotting and plaster splashes.

### Step 9 — Transport and site marking

- Each member carries its address (*bantsuke*) and, where relevant, orientation marks: "up" (上), the *moto* end, the front face. Marks go where they will be hidden after assembly (tops of beams under floor boards, inner faces, tenons, splice surfaces) or on removable tape on *keshō* faces.
- Members are bundled and loaded **in reverse order of erection**, so that the first members needed (*dodai*, posts) come off the truck first.
- On site, members are laid out near their positions according to the *itazu* before *tatemae* (→ Ch. 15).

**R04-051** — **Should.** Load for transport in reverse erection order, and lay out on site by grid address before erection begins.

## 12. Work-holding, posture and cutting technique

### 12.1 Work-holding

The Japanese workshop traditionally has **no bench vise**. Work is held by trestles, stops and the carpenter's own weight.

- **Trestles (馬 *uma*, "horses"):** pairs of low, heavy trestles (typically knee height or lower). Timbers are laid across them for marking and cutting. The carpenter often sits or kneels on the timber to hold it while chiseling, and stands on it while adzing. Trestles are leveled and spaced so the member does not sag.
- **Planing beam (削り台 *kezuri-dai*):** a long, stiff plank or beam, usually set on the floor or on low trestles, often sloping slightly down toward the user. A stop (当て止め *atedome*, 止め *tome*) is fixed at the far end. The work butts against the stop, and the plane is pulled toward the user, so the pulling force holds the work against the stop. Boards and small members are planed on the *kezuri-dai*. Large members are planed in place on the *uma*.
- **Floor work:** fine joinery, *tategu* and sharpening are traditionally done sitting on the floor, with a low planing board and the stone on a stand (砥石台 *toishi-dai*).
- **Temporary holding:** wedges, cleats nailed to the trestle, iron dogs (*kasugai*). No clamping marks on *keshō* faces.

**R04-052** — **Should.** Keep trestles level and at the same height, and support long members at enough points that they do not bend during marking and cutting.
*Why:* a member marked while sagging, or twisted on uneven trestles, is marked with a false geometry.

### 12.2 Posture

- **Sawing:** the carpenter stands or crouches with the eye directly above the line and the saw, the forearm in line with the saw. Long, relaxed strokes use the whole length of the blade, and the saw's own weight does the work. Pushing hard buckles nothing (the saw cuts on the pull), but it wanders.
- **Rip sawing** long cheeks: saw at a low angle to the surface (≈ 15–30°) for guidance. **Crosscutting**: steeper (≈ 30–45°).
- **Check squareness by the reflection:** the image of the wood reflected in the polished saw plate should continue the line of the kerf. If the reflection bends, the saw is not square to the surface.
- **Chiseling:** sitting astride or kneeling on the member, with the body behind the chisel, the chisel vertical as seen from two directions, and the *gennō* swung from the elbow and shoulder with the face square to the handle end.
- **Planing:** feet apart, the plane pulled with the whole body stepping back, not with the arms alone. Pressure goes on the front of the plane at the start of the stroke and on the rear at the end.

### 12.3 Sawing to the line

Japanese marking lines have a width: a *sumisashi* line is about 0.3–0.5 mm. There are three ways to treat it:

| Convention | Japanese | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Leave the line | 墨を残す *sumi o nokosu* | Cut in the waste beside the line, leaving the whole line on the piece |
| Split the line | 墨の芯 / 墨半分 *sumi no shin / sumi hanbun* | Cut down the middle of the line |
| Remove the line | 墨を払う *sumi o harau* | Cut so the line is removed with the waste |

The fit of a joint is controlled by choosing the convention consistently for the male and female parts. A common convention for tight joints is to **leave the line** on the male part and on the walls of the female part, so that both parts are slightly generous. The final fit is then made by paring and *kigoroshi*. Conventions differ between workshops and between joint types (e.g. *keshō* shoulders versus hidden seats), so each workshop must state its own.

**R04-053** — **Must.** A workshop uses one stated line convention for each class of joint (e.g. "tenons and mortise walls: leave the line; seats: split the line"), and every carpenter follows it.
*Why:* fits depend on it. A tenon cut by one convention and its mortise by another is off by a full line width (≈ 0.3–0.5 mm), which is decisive in a *kigoroshi* joint.

**R04-054** — **Should.** Start every cut with a few light strokes near the handle end of the blade (on a *ryōba* rip edge the teeth are finest there), guided by the thumbnail or a block. Establish the kerf on both the top and the near face before sawing through.

### 12.4 Cutting a mortise with a *mukōmachi* (sequence)

For a through-mortise (e.g. a *nuki* hole in a post):

1. **Mark** the mortise on both opposite faces from the center line, with the gauge set to the chisel width (R04-006, R04-020).
2. **Optionally bore** a series of holes inside the lines to remove bulk (auger, drill or *kakunomi* for rough work).
3. **First chop:** set the *mukōmachi* a few mm inside one end line, bevel facing the centre of the mortise (toward the waste), and drive it in. Repeat at the other end.
4. **Work from the centre toward the ends** in successive chops about 3–5 mm apart, bevel toward the centre, forming a V. Lever out chips toward the centre after each pair of chops. The strong neck of the *mukōmachi* is made for this.
5. **Go down in stages** to about half the depth (for through mortises).
6. **Turn the member** and repeat from the opposite face, meeting in the middle. **Never drive a through-mortise from one side only.**
   *Why:* the exit face splits out (裏割れ *ura-ware*).
7. **Final cut at the end walls:** place the chisel exactly on the end line with the flat back (*ura*) toward the finished wall, vertical, and chop to the middle from each face.
8. **Pare the side walls** flat and parallel with an *oire* or *tsuki-nomi*, checking with a square and a straightedge in several directions.
9. **Knock out** any remaining waste with the *uchinuki-nomi*.
10. **Check:** width with a gauge block or offcut of the mating member, straightness, and freedom from wind (twist between the two faces).

For a **blind mortise**, steps 1–5 and 7–10 apply to one face, with the bottom cleared by the *soko-sarai-nomi*.

**R04-055** — **Must.** Cut through-mortises from both faces, meeting in the middle.

**R04-056** — **Should.** Make the final cut at each end wall with the chisel's flat back toward the finished wall and the bevel toward the waste.
*Why:* the bevel side of the chisel wedges the blade away from itself. With the flat back on the line, the blade stays on the line.

**R04-057** — **Should.** Never lever against the finished end wall of a mortise. Lever only against waste still to be removed.
*Why:* levering crushes the end wall, and an end wall that carries tenon bearing or wedge pressure must be sound.

### 12.5 Paring

- Pare with the *tsuki-nomi*, back flat on the reference surface (for flat faces) or bevel down (to follow a concave curve).
- Across end grain, pare from both edges toward the centre to avoid breaking out the far arris.
- On cheek faces of tenons, pare along the grain in the direction that does not dive (read the grain as for planing).
- Take the last fine shaving into the gauge line itself, so that the gauge line's scored edge becomes the arris.

**R04-058** — **Should.** Pare across end grain from both sides toward the middle. On *keshō* arrises, pare toward the solid wood, never off the edge.

## 13. Surface finishing

### 13.1 Planed surfaces and the *shiraki* ideal

The highest-grade Japanese interior surface is **white wood** (白木 *shiraki*): hinoki, sugi, tsuga and similar species left **unfinished** after the finishing plane.

- A well-sharpened plane slices the cells cleanly. The surface is smooth and lustrous (艶 *tsuya*), sheds dust and moisture better than a sanded surface, and ages to a warm tone.
- **Sandpaper is not used** on *keshō* surfaces in traditional work. Sanding tears and abrades the cell walls, dulls the lustre, embeds grit (which then ruins plane edges if the surface is ever re-planed), and raises the grain when the surface next becomes damp.
- **Plane finish quality:** a continuous, translucent shaving (a few hundredths of a millimetre on a good finishing plane) along the full length of the member, without tear-out or chatter. Planing competitions (削ろう会 *Kezurōkai*, from the 1990s) have reported shavings of only a few micrometres. That is a demonstration of plane set-up, not a construction requirement.

**R04-059** — **Must.** Do not sand *keshō* surfaces in traditional work. Finish with the plane (or the *chōna*/*yariganna* where specified).

**R04-060** — **Should.** Finish-plane *keshō* members in the direction of the grain along their **full length** in single continuous strokes where possible, overlapping by about a third of the blade width.
*Why:* stop marks and short strokes show as bands in raking light.

**R04-061** — **Should.** Clean soiled or weathered *shiraki* in old buildings by lye washing (灰汁洗い *aku-arai*: traditional alkaline washing, today with specialist cleaning agents), not by re-planing, unless the historic surface is not significant (→ Ch. 16).

### 13.2 Adze and spear-plane textures

- **Chōna-hatsuri / naguri** (§6.2): faceted texture. Used as a finish on *sukiya* posts, boards, the edges of shelves, entrance steps and floor boards (for foot grip and aesthetic), beams in rustic interiors, and fence boards. Kuri is the classic species.
- **Yariganna** (§6.3): long, gently rippled lustrous facets. Restoration of ancient buildings; also used for high-grade contemporary work.

**R04-062** — **Should.** Keep textured finishes (*naguri*, *yariganna*) clear of joint faces, bearing surfaces and the zones marked for chamfer stops. Texture only the exposed field.

### 13.3 Chamfers and small mouldings (面取り *mentori*)

Arrises on *keshō* members are almost always chamfered or moulded. The chamfer protects the arris from splintering, reads as a shadow line, and in historic work is an indicator of date and rank.

| Profile | Japanese | Form | Typical use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hairline chamfer | 糸面 *itomen* | Very small 45° chamfer, ≈ 0.5–2 mm | Standard for modern *shoin* and house posts, *kamoi*, *nageshi*, *tategu*; the default "chamfer" |
| Flat chamfer | 切面 *kirimen* | 45° flat chamfer of visible size | Posts, beams, in larger proportion in older and heavier work |
| Large chamfer | 大面 *ōmen* | Large 45° chamfer | Ancient and medieval temple posts, gates, minka posts |
| Kichō chamfer | 几帳面 *kichōmen* | Chamfer (flat or slightly curved) bordered on each side by a small step or notch | Refined posts and frames, historic temple and palace work (Heian–Kamakura onward), *tokogamachi* |
| Monkey-cheek | 猿頬面 *saruboho-men* | Deep bevel on both arrises of the bottom face, giving a trapezoidal section | *Saobuchi* (棹縁, ceiling battens), *sarubō* battens |
| Round | 丸面 *marumen*, 坊主面 *bōzu-men* | Rounded arris | Handrails, *tategu* |
| Gingko | 銀杏面 *ichō-men* | Curved chamfer | Fittings, furniture |
| Bark-edged | 面皮 *menkawa* | The corners of a post left with the natural round of the log (and sometimes bark) | *Sukiya* and tea-room posts (面皮柱 *menkawa-bashira*) |

**Proportion rules.** Chamfer size is proportioned to the member, and historically it **decreased over time**. A widely cited generalisation for square temple posts runs roughly as follows (approximate; with many exceptions):
- ancient (Nara–Heian): chamfer about 1/7–1/10 of post width;
- medieval: about 1/10–1/14;
- early modern (Edo): about 1/14 and smaller, down to hairline chamfers.

Kiwari texts give specific chamfer ratios per school (→ Ch. 01, Ch. 12). In *shoin* and house work the chamfer also governs the **reveals** of fittings: *kamoi* and *nageshi* are set flush with the post face (面一 *tsuraichi*), within the chamfer (面内 *mennai*), or at the middle of the chamfer (面中 *mennaka*) (→ Ch. 14).

**R04-063** — **Should.** Size chamfers in proportion to member width, consistently for all members of the same rank in a room or building. For restoration, match the historic chamfer ratio of the building's period (→ Ch. 16).

**R04-064** — **Must.** Stop chamfers (面止め *men-dome*) where the member meets another member at a joint, so that the joint line is tight at its visible edge. Cut the chamfer after the joint is cut, and end it with a small stop (e.g. a 45° or curved stop) a little short of the joint line, or mitre it into the adjoining member's chamfer, depending on the joint.
*Why:* a chamfer run through a joint leaves a visible notch or gap where the members meet.

### 13.4 Traditional coatings

Traditional Japanese buildings are largely unpainted inside, with specific coatings where durability, colour or status demand them.

| Finish | Japanese | Composition | Use and notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Charred cedar | 焼杉 *yakisugi* | Surface of sugi boards charred to a carbon layer. Traditionally three boards bound into a triangular flue and burned from inside. Used as charred, or brushed (磨き *migaki*) and sometimes oiled | Exterior cladding in western Japan (Setouchi, Kansai). The char layer resists weathering, insects and surface ignition. It weathers and flakes over decades |
| Wiped lacquer | 拭き漆 *fuki-urushi* | Raw lacquer (生漆 *ki-urushi*) rubbed in and wiped off, repeated many times, with curing in humid conditions between coats | Alcove front rails (*tokogamachi*), entrance front beams (*agari-gamachi*), handrails, floors of high-status rooms, fittings. Deep, hard, water-resistant finish that shows the grain |
| Persimmon tannin | 柿渋 *kakishibu* | Fermented juice of unripe astringent persimmons | Waterproofing and preserving wood, paper and fabric. Darkens to brown with light exposure. Also used as a binder |
| Red iron oxide | 弁柄・紅殻 *bengara* | Iron-oxide pigment (Fe₂O₃), traditionally bound with *kakishibu*, water or oil (荏油 *e-abura*, perilla oil) | The red-brown lattices and facades of Kyoto and other *machiya*; the exterior woodwork of rural houses. Protective and decorative |
| Pine-soot black | 松煙 *shōen* (with *bengara* or *kakishibu*) | Pine soot (the traditional black of *sumi* ink) mixed into *bengara* or *kakishibu* | Dark brown to black coloration of exterior boards and lattices, a regional machiya colour. Also used to tone new wood to match old in repairs |
| Perilla / drying oils | 荏油 *e-abura*, 亜麻仁油 (linseed, modern) | Drying oil, often with pigment | Floors, exterior woodwork, verandahs |
| Vermilion / red lead | 丹 *ni*, 朱 *shu* (and white 胡粉 *gofun*) | Mineral pigments in glue or lacquer | Shrine and temple painted woodwork (丹塗り *ninuri*), → Ch. 12 |
| Wax | 蝋 *rō* | Plant or insect wax | Rubbed into *shikii* grooves and runners of sliding fittings |

**R04-065** — **Should.** In interior *shoin* and *sukiya* work, leave *keshō* softwoods as *shiraki* unless a specific finish is part of the design tradition (e.g. *fuki-urushi* on the *tokogamachi*).
*Why:* the planed surface is the finish, and coatings change colour and age differently.

**R04-066** — **Should.** Apply coatings to exterior woodwork as a maintained system: *bengara* with *kakishibu* or oil, or *yakisugi*. Plan periodic renewal (→ Ch. 16). Do not seal end grain of exterior members so that moisture is trapped behind the coating.

**R04-067** — **Must.** Never apply a coating before all joints on that member are cut and fitted. Coat joint faces only where the tradition calls for it (e.g. preservative on *dodai* seats), and never coat the contact faces of *kigoroshi* joints.
*Why:* coatings change friction and swelling behaviour at joints, and a coating in a joint prevents the compressed wood from recovering and gripping.

## 14. Summary checklist

**Tools**
- Edges sharp, backs flat, stones flat (R04-001, R04-027 – R04-031).
- Plane soles trued for season and stage (R04-014, R04-015).
- *Gennō* faces used correctly (R04-024, R04-025).
- Separate *keshō* saws; right tooth type (R04-012, R04-013).

**Marking**
- One *itazu*, one set of rods, one marker in charge (R04-009, R04-036).
- Reference faces and center lines; same gauge settings for mating parts (R04-006, R04-039).
- Marking complete and checked before cutting (R04-040).
- Line convention stated and followed (R04-053).

**Cutting**
- *Kidori* for the whole frame first (R04-037, R04-038).
- Saw before chisel; female first; projecting parts last; center lines preserved (R04-042 – R04-045).
- Through-mortises from both faces (R04-055 – R04-057).

**Finishing and dispatch**
- Finish planing after cutting, equal from both faces (R04-046, R04-047).
- No sanding (R04-059).
- Chamfers proportioned and stopped at joints (R04-063, R04-064).
- Trial assembly where required, without driving joints home (R04-048, R04-049).
- Wrapped, stored, loaded in reverse erection order (R04-050, R04-051).

Specialised names (e.g. *kamoibiki*, *anabiki*, the rebate-plane family) vary regionally, and some are used differently by modern tool vendors than in historical writing. Chronologies (introduction of the *ōga*, *maebiki*, *dai-ganna*, two-blade plane, *ryōba*) are given as approximate periods, and specialists debate them.
