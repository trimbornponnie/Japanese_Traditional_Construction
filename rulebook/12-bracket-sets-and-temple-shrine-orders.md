# Chapter 12 — Bracket Sets and Temple/Shrine Orders (組物・社寺建築 / *kumimono, shaji kenchiku*)

**Scope.** This chapter covers the "orders" of Japanese religious architecture: the bracket sets (組物, *kumimono*, also 斗栱 *tokyō*) that carry the eaves of temples and shrines, the four Buddhist architectural styles (様式, *yōshiki*) and their construction rules, the proportional system (木割, *kiwari*) that ties column, bracket and rafter together, pagoda construction, the principal shrine styles (神社本殿形式) and torii, and the attachment of carved ornament. It assumes the unit system, *shiwari* (枝割) rafter module and marking conventions of → Ch. 02, the frame members of → Ch. 10 and the roof, rafter and eave framing of → Ch. 11 (including *hanegi* cantilevers, *nokizori* and *kayaoi*). Interior fittings, ceilings and tokonoma belong to → Ch. 14; dismantling repair and the Ise renewal cycle as a repair method to → Ch. 16.

A caution that runs through the chapter: bracket-set proportions and style features are **period- and school-dependent**. Every dimension table below is labelled *representative*; a practitioner restoring or reproducing a specific building must work from a measured survey of that building or from the kiwari book of the school that built it (→ Ch. 01).

## 1. The order: column, bracket, purlin, rafter

A Japanese temple or shrine building is read vertically in three zones:

| Zone | Japanese | Members |
|---|---|---|
| Body / shaft | 軸部 *jikubu* | foundation stones (礎石 *soseki*), plinths (礎盤 *soban* in Zen style), columns (柱), head ties (頭貫 *kashiranuki*), plates (台輪 *daiwa*), penetrating ties (貫 *nuki*), non-penetrating clasping beams (長押 *nageshi*), tie beams (虹梁 *kōryō*) |
| Bracketing | 組物 *kumimono* | bearing blocks (斗 *to/masu*), bracket arms (肘木 *hijiki*), tail rafters (尾垂木 *odaruki*), infill between brackets (中備 *nakazonae*) |
| Eaves / roof | 軒・小屋 *noki, koya* | eave purlin (丸桁 *gangyō*), rafters (垂木 *taruki*), fascia (茅負 *kayaoi*), eave ceilings (軒天井), roof frame (→ Ch. 11) |

The kumimono does four jobs at once, and every rule in §2–§3 serves one of them:

1. **Spread point load.** The column top is a small bearing area; the bracket collects the load of a long purlin (桁 *keta*) at several points (the three *to* of a *mitsudo*) and funnels it into one column.
2. **Carry the eave purlin outward.** Each step outward (手先, *tesaki*) moves the *gangyō* further from the column line, so the eave projects further without enormous rafters.
3. **Reduce the clear span of the purlin.** The arm length of the hijiki shortens the effective span between supports (a structural bonus of the bracket that is often overlooked).
4. **Give a deformable, friction-damped joint layer.** Stacked blocks with dowels allow small rotations and slip under earthquake (→ §6.5 and → Ch. 17).

The general principle is that **the kumimono stack is a gravity-held assembly**: the members are dowelled (太枘 *dabo*) and notched, but they are held together essentially by the weight of the roof above. Nothing in a classical bracket set is designed to take tension (→ Ch. 06 on compression-dominant joinery; → Ch. 09 on dabo).

**R12-001** — **Must.** Every member of a bracket set shall be designed to act in compression and bending under gravity load; do not rely on any bracket joint (dabo, notch, lap) to resist uplift or tension. *Why:* the dabo are locating pins, not fasteners; uplift resistance comes from roof dead load and, in the inner zone, from the tail of the odaruki or hanegi being held down (→ §2.6, → Ch. 11).

**R12-002** — **Must.** The column centre line, the daito centre, and the centre of the bracket set (the intersection of its arms) shall coincide in plan. *Why:* an eccentric daito puts the column top in bending and rotates the stack under load; all layout lines (芯墨 *shinzumi*) of the set are struck from the column centre (→ Ch. 02 marking).

### 2.1 Bearing blocks — general (斗, *to*; colloquially *masu*)
- **Class:** bearing block, dowel-located (dabo) and notch-seated.
- **Typical use:** at every bearing point of a bracket set; the name derives from the rice-measure box (枡) it resembles.
- **Parts:** the upper vertical band, the 耳 (*mimi*, "ears"), rises on either side of the slot into which the arm sits; below it the 敷 / 敷面 (*shiki*, *shikimen*) is the plain vertical band under the slot bottom; the lowest part, the 斗尻 (*tojiri*), is tapered inwards with a concave profile called 斗繰り (*tokuri*). The slot itself — the part that "holds" the arm — is called 含み (*fukumi*) in some texts. Some period forms add a thin plate at the base, the 皿 (*sara*) → 皿斗 *sarato* (§2.2).
- **Load behaviour:** pure bearing; the arm above presses into the slot bottom and the block presses on the arm or daito below. With the usual grain orientation (R12-004) this is compression perpendicular to grain, the weakest direction of wood in bearing — hence the emphasis on dense, knot-free stock (R12-006). The tojiri taper does no structural work; it expresses the load path visually.
- **Proportions (representative):** height commonly divided mimi : shiki : tojiri ≈ 2 : 1 : 2 (i.e. 4 : 2 : 4 in tenths). Medieval and later work tends to deepen the tojiri and reduce the mimi; Asuka/Nara work has a more pronounced, deeply curved tokuri. Treat this as an approximate characterisation and measure the precedent.
- **Orientation rules:** grain direction — see R12-004. Makito are oriented with the long side along the arm they sit on.
- **Marking:** centre lines on all four faces and on top and bottom; slot width equals the thickness of the arm it receives, marked from the arm, not from a nominal size.
- **Assembly:** the block is placed over a dabo projecting from the member below and receives a dabo into its top or into the arm above.

**R12-003** — **Must.** Each to is located on the member below it by at least one dabo (太枘) — typically a square hardwood pin roughly ⅙–¼ of the block width, set half into each member. *Why:* without the pin, earthquake shaking walks the blocks off the arms; the pin is also the reference that keeps the stack true during tatemae (→ Ch. 09 §dabo).

**R12-004** — **Should.** Cut to so that the wood grain runs horizontally, parallel to the arm the block receives (i.e. the fibres lie along the long direction of a makito). *Why:* this is the dominant historical practice; it lets the mimi resist splitting when the arm bears in the slot, and it keeps shrinkage (largest across the grain) in the vertical direction where it merely lowers the stack uniformly. Variant: some restorers report vertical-grain blocks in particular buildings — follow the precedent in restoration work.

**R12-005** — **Must.** The slot (fukumi) of a to shall be a snug fit on the arm thickness — hand-tight, with no play in the direction across the arm. *Why:* the mimi are what prevent the arm rolling sideways; play here converts into wobble of the whole stack.

**R12-006** — **Should.** Blocks are made of the hardest, straightest-grained stock available for the job (in hinoki buildings, knot-free hinoki; in some regions keyaki for exposed small blocks). *Why:* the to concentrates the whole roof load into small areas across the grain; crushing of blocks is a classic cause of sagging eaves found in dismantling repairs (→ Ch. 16).

### 2.2 The kinds of to

| Term | Reading | Position and form |
|---|---|---|
| 大斗 | *daito* | the large block directly on the column head (or on the daiwa in Zen style), slotted in one direction (hira-mitsudo) or in a cross (de-mitsudo and above) |
| 巻斗 | *makito* | the rectangular block on the arm, slotted in **one** direction only; the commonest small block |
| 方斗 | *hōto* | a square block slotted in **two** directions (cross slot), used where two arms or an arm and a sanehijiki cross at the outer end of a projecting arm |
| 鬼斗 | *onito* | the irregularly shaped block at a corner bracket where arms meet at 45°; its plan is cut to receive the diagonal (隅行 *sumiyuki*) arm and the two orthogonal arms |
| 皿斗 | *sarato* | a to with a thin projecting plate (皿板 *sara-ita*) at its base; characteristic of Asuka-style work (Hōryū-ji) and of daibutsuyō, absent in mature wayō |
| 斗 (on a strut) | — | the block on top of a *kento-zuka* or *taiheizuka* (§2.8) |

(The Chinese term 散斗 *sandou* corresponds broadly to the Japanese makito; it is sometimes seen in comparative literature but is not the normal Japanese workshop word.)

**R12-007** — **Must.** Use a hōto (two-way slot) wherever two arms cross above an arm end, and an onito at the diagonal of corner sets; never substitute a makito and "notch it more". *Why:* a makito's two remaining mimi cannot restrain a second, orthogonal arm; the cross slot of the hōto is what makes the upper arm crossing stable.

### 2.3 Bracket arms (肘木, *hijiki*)
- **Class:** short beam / corbel, seated in to slots, crossing others by half-lap (相欠き *aikaki*).
- **Typical use:** every bracket set; the underside of each end is cut back in a curved or faceted profile (the end treatment, 肘木の繰形, is a strong dating clue).
- **Load behaviour:** short-span bending and shear between to; in projecting sets, cantilever bending about the daito.
- **Proportions (representative):** thickness (width) ≈ the width of the to's slot = roughly the tojiri base width of the makito it carries; depth (成 *sei*) ≈ 1.0–1.3 × makito height. Length for a three-block arm is governed by the rafter module in rokushigake (§5.3): outer-face to outer-face of the end makito = 5 shi + 1 rafter width.
- **Orientation rules:** where two arms cross in the daito or a hōto, the arm parallel to the wall and the projecting arm are half-lapped and **both run continuously through the block**. Which of the two is the lower (notched-from-above) member differs between styles, schools and even tiers of one set — record and follow the precedent; in new work, make the member with the larger cantilever the one whose lap is shallower if the design allows unequal laps.
- **Marking:** centre lines of each to seat; the underside end profile from a template (型板 *katagi*) of the building's style.

Named kinds of arm:

| Term | Reading | Description |
|---|---|---|
| 舟肘木 | *funa-hijiki* | a single boat-shaped arm set directly on the column head without any daito; the simplest set |
| 枠肘木 | *waku-hijiki* | the pair of arms crossing in the daito (parallel + projecting) forming the "frame" of a de-mitsudo or multi-step set |
| 秤肘木 | *hakari-hijiki* | an arm balanced on a single to like a steelyard (秤) — e.g. the arm on a hōto at the outer end of a step carrying two makito |
| 通肘木 | *tōri-hijiki* | a long, continuous arm running along the wall plane through several bracket sets and bays, tying them together; in multi-step sets there may be one at each tier |
| 実肘木 | *sanehijiki* | a short arm set directly under the purlin (gangyō) on top of the uppermost to, in effect a bolster; common in wayō shrine and later work, and in zenshūyō |
| 挿肘木 | *sashi-hijiki* | an arm **tenoned into the column body** rather than sitting on a daito; the defining member of daibutsuyō (§4.3) |
| 花肘木 | *hana-hijiki* | a carved, often pierced ornamental arm, typically at the *kōhai* (向拝, entrance canopy) of a shrine or temple |
| 手先肘木 | *tesaki-hijiki* | generic for the arms that project outward, one per step |
| 雲肘木 | *kumo-hijiki* | the cloud-profiled arm of Asuka-style buildings (Hōryū-ji kondō and pagoda), paired with 雲斗 *kumoto* |

**R12-008** — **Must.** Where a projecting arm and a wall-parallel arm cross, the half-lap shall remove no more than half the depth of each, and the reduced section shall lie **entirely within the slot of the daito (or hōto)** that receives the crossing. The lower arm is notched from above and bears with its full underside on the slot bottom; the upper arm is notched from below. *Why:* inside the block the lapped section is confined by the mimi on all sides and bears continuously; a lap that extends beyond the block face leaves a half-depth section exposed to the cantilever moment at exactly the point where it is greatest.

**R12-009** — **Should.** Tōri-hijiki shall be spliced only above a to or over a column centre, never at mid-bay (→ Ch. 07 on splice placement). *Why:* between bearing points the tōri-hijiki acts as a beam tying the bays; a splice there is a hinge.

### 2.4 Eave purlin (丸桁, *gangyō*) and wall purlins
- The **gangyō** is the purlin at the outermost bracket point that receives the *jidaruki* (base rafters). The name ("round purlin") recalls its original round section; in most extant work it is rectangular with rounded or plain faces, set on sanehijiki or directly on the topmost to.
- Inside it, the **側桁** (*gawageta*) or **通肘木** line at the wall plane carries the inner part of the rafters or the eave ceiling.
- The gangyō is spliced over a bracket (R12-009 applies) and its joints are usually *kanawa-tsugi* or *okkake-daisen* (→ Ch. 07).

**R12-010** — **Must.** The top of the gangyō shall be set exactly to the rafter seat line derived from the eave slope and the rafter module (→ Ch. 05 kōbai, → Ch. 11 §noki); cumulative shrinkage of to and arms under it shall be allowed for by setting it slightly high (typical allowance a few bu per tier in green-ish timber). *Why:* a stack of five to seven cross-grain layers loses measurable height as it dries; if the purlin is set to the final line with no allowance, the eave sags below the kayaoi line.

### 2.5 Eave ceilings and infill (支輪 *shirin*, 軒天井 *nokitenjō*, 小天井 *kotenjō*)
- In multi-step sets the spaces between the projecting tiers are closed by boards: the **小天井** (*kotenjō*) is a small flat ceiling between tiers; the **軒天井** (*nokitenjō*) closes the soffit between the wall and the gangyō; the **支輪** (*shirin*) is a curved (cove) panel of ribs and boards rising from one tier to the next — in wayō often a set of curved ribs (支輪子 *shirinko*) with boards (支輪板) between.
- These members are **non-structural**; they must be fitted after the brackets are loaded, and they must not be wedged tight between bracket tiers.

**R12-011** — **Must.** Shirin and eave ceilings shall be fitted with slotted or floating joints that allow the bracket tiers to move and settle; never nail a shirin rigidly across two tiers. *Why:* differential settlement of the tiers would split the thin boards or, worse, let the infill take load it cannot carry.

### 2.6 Tail rafters (尾垂木, *odaruki*)
- **Class:** inclined lever / cantilever.
- **Typical use:** in futatesaki and mitesaki sets; the odaruki runs from the outer tier, over a fulcrum on the bracket set near the wall line, back into the building, where its tail (尾垂木尻 *odaruki-jiri*) is held down.
- **Load behaviour:** a first-class lever. The outer end carries the outer tiers and gangyō (eave load); the fulcrum is the to or arm at the wall plane; the tail is held down by the dead load of the inner purlin (入側桁), a strut, or the roof structure over it. The lever lets a deep eave be carried with modest arm sizes.
- **Proportions (representative):** section similar to or slightly larger than a jidaruki in width, deeper in depth; slope near but not equal to the rafter slope (in mature wayō, the visible odaruki slope is set independently for appearance).
- **Orientation rules:** the maximum bending moment in a lever is at the fulcrum, so the soundest, knot-free length of the log must be placed there; the moto/sue direction is then chosen so that the stiffer butt portion lies over the fulcrum-to-outer-end length. Practice on moto/sue direction is not uniform between workshops (→ Ch. 03 on moto/sue) — record what you find in restoration.
- **Evolution:** in Nara and early Heian halls the odaruki is a genuine structural lever. From the later Heian period onward the hidden cantilever (桔木 *hanegi*, → Ch. 11) inside the roof space takes over the real work, and the visible odaruki is increasingly a **decorative** member (化粧尾垂木 *keshō-odaruki*) — a short piece whose tail stops soon after the wall line.

**R12-012** — **Must.** A structural odaruki shall bear on a defined fulcrum (a to or arm seat cut to its underside slope) and its tail shall be positively held down by dead load; never rely on the pinning of the tail. *Why:* the lever is only as good as its hold-down; tails held by a pin alone pull out as the eave creeps.

**R12-013** — **Must.** In a restoration, identify whether the odaruki is structural or decorative before dismantling; if the eave is carried by hanegi, the odaruki must not be "strengthened" to take load it was not detailed for. *Why:* reloading a decorative odaruki shifts load to its shallow fulcrum seat, which crushes (→ Ch. 16).

### 2.7 Crossing joints and seats in the set — summary of joinery

| Joint | Where | Rule |
|---|---|---|
| Dabo (太枘) block-to-arm and daito-to-column | every to | R12-003; square pin, half in each member |
| Half-lap (相欠き) arm crossings | daito, hōto, onito | R12-008; equal halves unless precedent differs |
| Slot seat (含み) | arm in to | R12-005; hand-tight |
| Odaruki seat (尾垂木の掛け) | fulcrum to/arm | R12-012; underside cut to slope |
| Gangyō seat | on sanehijiki / top to | dabo or shallow housing; splice over a set |
| Daito on column | column head | dabo(s) into column top; in Zen style the daito sits on the daiwa and is dowelled through |
| Kashiranuki at column | column head | through-housing/lap with the column head; ends project as kibana in daibutsuyō/zenshūyō (§10.2) |

**R12-014** — **Should.** Cut all bracket components in the shop to templates and trial-assemble each typical set dry (仮組 *karigumi*) before tatemae; number every block with the set's grid reference (→ Ch. 02 i-ro-ha grid, → Ch. 15). *Why:* a bracket set consists of 10–60 small parts per column; errors of a bu in each accumulate into visible waviness of the eave line.

### 2.8 Infill between columns (中備, *nakazonae*)
Between column-top bracket sets in *sparse* arrangements (§3.8), the wall-plane purlin or the tōri-hijiki is supported mid-bay by an infill member:

| Member | Reading | Form |
|---|---|---|
| 間斗束 | *kento-zuka* | a short strut on the kashiranuki (or a tie below) with a to on top — the standard wayō infill |
| 蟇股 | *kaerumata* | "frog-legs": a splayed, often pierced and carved block on the kashiranuki, carrying a to; → §10.1 |
| 蓑束 | *minozuka* | a strut with a flared, "raincoat"-shaped base |
| 撥束 | *bachizuka* | a strut splayed like a *biwa* plectrum |
| 双斗 | *sōto* | two to on a short arm on a strut (Nara/Heian examples) |
| 人字形割束 | *hitoji-gata warizuka* | inverted-V strut of Asuka/Nara work (Hōryū-ji) |
| 花肘木 | *hana-hijiki* | ornamental arm, especially at kōhai |
| 詰組 | *tsumegumi* | full bracket sets between columns (Zen style) instead of a strut |

**R12-015** — **Must.** A nakazonae shall bear on a member that can take a point load (kashiranuki with adequate depth, or a tie beam); it shall not stand on a nageshi or on wall infill. *Why:* the nakazonae is structural — it props the purlin at mid-bay. Kaerumata that are purely ornamental (e.g. on a thin *nuki* in minor buildings) should be recognised as such and not loaded.

## 3. Bracket-set types in increasing complexity

The progression below is the standard classification used in Japanese architectural history and restoration practice. "Step" (手先, *tesaki*) means one tier of projection outward from the wall plane. The count is of how many times the load is carried outward before reaching the gangyō.

| Type | Japanese | Tesaki | Gangyō position | Typical use / period |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Boat arm | 舟肘木 *funa-hijiki* | 0 | on the arm at the wall plane | residences, subsidiary halls, many shrine honden |
| Block and arm | 大斗肘木 *daito-hijiki* | 0 | on the arm | halls of modest rank; Nara-period precedents |
| Flat three-block | 平三斗 *hira-mitsudo* | 0 | on the three makito (with or without sanehijiki) | wayō walls, gates, corridors |
| Projecting three-block | 出三斗 *de-mitsudo* | 0 (a crossing arm projects but carries nothing outward except the beam end) | on the three makito | where a beam (梁) or tie meets the wall at right angles |
| One-step (projecting set) | 出組 *degumi* = 一手先 *hitotesaki* | 1 | on the outer end of the projecting arm | medium-rank halls; widespread from the late Heian period |
| Two-step | 二手先 *futatesaki* | 2 | on the second tier | high-rank halls, gates, pagodas |
| Three-step | 三手先 *mitesaki* | 3 | on the third tier; odaruki normal | the highest rank: kondō, pagodas (e.g. Yakushi-ji East Pagoda, 730; Tōshōdai-ji Kondō, late 8th c.; Daigo-ji Five-storey Pagoda, 951) |
| Four-step and more | 四手先 etc. | 4+ | — | rare; daibutsuyō uses multiple tiers of *sashi-hijiki* instead (six tiers at the Tōdai-ji Nandaimon) |

(Hōryū-ji's Asuka-style kondō and pagoda use the cloud-bracket system 雲斗雲肘木 *kumoto-kumohijiki*, which projects in two stages with an odaruki but is conventionally treated separately from the wayō series.)

### 3.1 Funa-hijiki (舟肘木)
- A single arm laid directly on the column head, housed over it with a shallow notch or dabo, carrying the keta. Both ends of the underside are cut in a gentle curve like a boat hull.
- **Load flow:** keta → arm → column. The arm reduces the keta's span by about its length and prevents the keta splitting at the column head.

**R12-016** — **Should.** A funa-hijiki's length is usually 2.5–3.5 × the column width, its depth about 0.5–0.7 × column width (representative). Its centre is dowelled to the column head. *Why:* shorter arms give no span reduction; longer ones tip under unequal bay loads.

### 3.2 Daito-hijiki (大斗肘木)
- A daito on the column carrying an arm; the keta sits directly on the arm. The daito raises the arm and accommodates the kashiranuki beneath.

### 3.3 Hira-mitsudo (平三斗)
- Daito + one arm parallel to the wall + three makito (two at the ends, one at centre over the daito). The keta (or sanehijiki + keta) rests on the three makito.
- **Load flow:** keta → three makito → arm (in bending) → daito → column.
- This is the key unit for **rokushigake** (§5.3): the three makito carry six rafters.

### 3.4 De-mitsudo (出三斗)
- A hira-mitsudo plus a second arm crossing it at right angles in the daito, projecting out of (and into) the wall plane. The outer end of the crossing arm carries a single makito (sometimes a *kibana*-like end). It does **not** move the gangyō outward; it receives a beam (繋ぎ梁, *tsunagi-bari*, or kōryō) meeting the wall at right angles, or simply gives visual depth.

**R12-017** — **Must.** In de-mitsudo the crossing arm is lapped with the wall arm in the daito's cross slot (R12-008); where a beam end rests on the inner end of the crossing arm, that arm must be continuous through the daito. *Why:* the arm acts as a corbel under the beam end; a cut or spliced arm would drop the beam.

### 3.5 Degumi (出組) — one step
- The projecting arm (枠肘木, crossing the wall arm in the daito) carries at its outer end a hōto; on that hōto sits a second arm parallel to the wall carrying makito and the gangyō (often via sanehijiki). The wall plane continues upward with a tōri-hijiki.
- The **tesaki dimension** (horizontal distance from wall centre line to the outer bearing line) is set so that the gangyō falls on the rafter grid (§5).
- Between the wall plane and the gangyō the soffit is closed by a *nokitenjō*.


### 3.6 Futatesaki (二手先) — two steps
- The degumi process repeated: a second projecting arm from the first tier's hōto carries a second hōto and the outer arm. An **odaruki** is usually introduced, lying on the first-tier makito and carrying the outermost to on its end (or passing between tiers), the fulcrum being at the wall-plane tier.
- The eave between tiers is closed with kotenjō and a shirin rises from the outer tier to the gangyō.

### 3.7 Mitesaki (三手先) — three steps
- Three tiers of projection, with one (sometimes two) odaruki. The classic form of the highest-rank halls and pagodas of the Nara period onward: Yakushi-ji East Pagoda (730), Tōshōdai-ji Kondō (late 8th century), Daigo-ji Five-storey Pagoda (951), and in the Kamakura period many wayō main halls and pagodas.
- The mitesaki corner set is the most complex assembly in Japanese carpentry: three orthogonal arms from each face and a diagonal (隅行) series meet at each tier on onito, with a diagonal odaruki (隅尾垂木) under the hip rafter (→ Ch. 11 corner rafters; → Ch. 05 for the 45° geometry).

**R12-018** — **Must.** In multi-step sets the tesaki dimension of each tier shall be the same for all sets on a face, and the corner set's diagonal tiers shall be laid out at √2 × tesaki along the 45° line. *Why:* the gangyō and the hip rafter must intersect at a point determined by the orthogonal tiers; any mismatch appears as a kink in the gangyō at the corner.

**R12-019** — **Must.** Assemble multi-step sets tier by tier around the whole building (all first tiers, then all second tiers, …), checking the height of each tier with a level line before the next. *Why:* the tōri-hijiki and the eave ceiling framing tie each tier across bays; completing one set to full height before its neighbours makes the tōri-hijiki impossible to insert and hides cumulative height errors.

### 3.8 Sparse and dense arrangements (疎組 *magumi/sokumi* vs 詰組 *tsumegumi*)
- **疎組** (sparse arrangement): bracket sets only on column heads; between them a nakazonae (kento-zuka, kaerumata) or nothing. Wayō and daibutsuyō.
- **詰組** (dense arrangement): full bracket sets also between columns, standing on the **daiwa** (台輪, a wide flat plate laid over the column heads and head tie). Zenshūyō.

**R12-020** — **Must.** Tsumegumi requires a daiwa (or an equally stiff plate) on the column heads to carry the intermediate sets; never stand an intermediate full bracket set on a kashiranuki alone. *Why:* each intermediate set brings a concentrated eave load mid-bay; the daiwa, laid flat and wide over the head tie, spreads that load and resists the torsion from the outward-projecting arms.

**R12-021** — **Should.** In tsumegumi the intermediate sets are spaced evenly within each bay (commonly one or two per bay, depending on span), and the bay widths are adjusted so that intermediate and column sets align with the rafter layout. *Why:* fan rafters in Zen buildings do not follow a parallel shiwari (§4.4), but the gangyō support spacing must still be regular to keep the eave line straight.

### 3.9 Corner sets (隅組物 *sumi-kumimono*)
- At the corner, arms project on both faces and along the diagonal. Blocks where three directions meet are onito; the diagonal members carry the hip rafter (隅木 *sumigi*) (→ Ch. 11).
- In wayō, corner sets often receive **added** makito or extended arms (隅の延び) so the eave does not look thin at the corner.

**R12-022** — **Must.** The hip rafter (sumigi) at the corner shall be carried on the corner set's diagonal tiers and never on the orthogonal arms alone. *Why:* the sumigi carries the largest single eave load in the building (the two half-bays of both eaves meeting at the corner); the diagonal tiers are what is aligned under it.

## 4. The Buddhist architectural styles

Japanese architectural historians recognise, after the Asuka/Nara period's continental styles, three medieval styles and their combination. The styles are primarily **constructional systems**, not just decoration: each implies a particular way of tying the frame and supporting the eaves.

### 4.1 Precursors: Asuka and Nara work (brief)
- **Hōryū-ji** (kondō and five-storey pagoda, late 7th c. rebuilding; the pagoda's shinbashira timber has a dendrochronological felling date of 594): cloud brackets (雲斗・雲肘木), 皿斗 blocks, inverted-V struts (人字形割束), swastika-fret balustrades (卍崩しの高欄), entasis in columns (胴張り *dōbari*).
- **Nara period** (Yakushi-ji East Pagoda 730; Tōshōdai-ji Kondō): the three-step set with odaruki is established; this system matured into wayō.

### 4.2 Wayō (和様) — Japanese style
**Characteristic members and joints**
- **Nageshi** (長押) as the principal lateral tie: heavy horizontal members applied to the faces of the columns — 地長押 (*jinageshi*) at floor level, 腰長押 (*koshinageshi*) at sill level, 内法長押 (*uchinori-nageshi*) at lintel level, and 頭長押 in some halls — nailed (wa-kugi) to the columns. Wayō relies on the nageshi, the kashiranuki and the mass of the roof for lateral stability; penetrating nuki through the columns are **not** part of classic wayō (they are introduced from daibutsuyō, → "shin-wayō" in §4.5).
- **Kashiranuki** (頭貫) housed into the column tops, its ends **not** projecting (no kibana) in pure wayō.
- **Kumimono** only on the columns (sparse), with kento-zuka or kaerumata as infill.
- **Rafters**: parallel, closely spaced 繁垂木 (*shigedaruki*) in two layers — base rafters 地垂木 (*jidaruki*) and flying rafters 飛檐垂木 (*hiendaruki*) — the 二軒 *futanoki* eave (→ Ch. 11).
- **Openings**: plank doors (板扉) hung on pivots, lattice shutters (蔀戸 *shitomido*), vertical-slat windows (連子窓 *renjimado*).
- **Interior**: raised wooden floor; coffered ceilings (格天井, 小組格天井), or an exposed "decorative roof underside" (化粧屋根裏).

**Proportion character**: wayō aims at low, broad, calm proportions; column heights are modest relative to bay width, rafters fine and dense.

**R12-023** — **Must.** In a wayō reconstruction do not add penetrating nuki or projecting kashiranuki ends unless the precedent shows them. *Why:* their presence is the diagnostic of *shin-wayō*/setchūyō; adding them falsifies the style (and, in designated buildings, is not permissible under restoration principles, → Ch. 16).

**R12-024** — **Should.** Nageshi in wayō are fitted to the column curvature by scribing (光付け *hikaritsuke*, → Ch. 10) and fixed with wa-kugi driven at an angle; at corners they are joined with a mitred or lapped return. *Why:* the nageshi works as a clasp; a gap between nageshi and column loses the frictional clamping on which the frame's lateral stability depends.

### 4.3 Daibutsuyō (大仏様), also called Tenjikuyō (天竺様)
Introduced by the monk **Chōgen** (重源, 1121–1206) for the rebuilding of Tōdai-ji after the 1180 fire. The older name *tenjikuyō* ("Indian style") is misleading and has largely been replaced by *daibutsuyō* in modern scholarship. Only a few buildings survive: the Tōdai-ji Nandaimon (1199), the Tōdai-ji Kaisandō (inner part), and Jōdo-ji Jōdodō at Ono, Hyōgo (1194). Its strictly structural logic made it influential far beyond its few examples — nuki and kibana entered all later work.

**Characteristic members and joints**
- **挿肘木 sashi-hijiki:** bracket arms are **tenoned into the columns**, tier above tier. At the Nandaimon, six tiers of sashi-hijiki project from the columns to carry the eave. The column itself is the spine of the bracket system; there is no separate daito on top for each arm.
- **貫 nuki:** multiple penetrating ties pass *through* the columns at several levels (頭貫, 飛貫, 内法貫 etc.), wedged, making the frame rigid in both directions. At the Nandaimon the nuki pass at many levels through single columns roughly 20 m long (figures vary by source; the gate is c. 25 m high overall).
- **遊離尾垂木 yūri-odaruki** ("detached tail rafter"): a lever-like odaruki placed between the columns (as nakazonae), pivoting on a tie/arm tier, its outer end supporting the eave purlin mid-bay and its inner end restrained by the structure — balancing eave and roof loads by the lever principle.
- **皿斗 sarato:** blocks with a plate at their base.
- **木鼻 kibana:** the projecting ends of the nuki and sashi-hijiki beyond the column face are shaped with a simple moulded profile — the origin of the kibana tradition (§10.2).
- **鼻隠板 hanakakushi-ita:** a fascia board nailed over the ends of the rafters, concealing the rafter ends (the rafters themselves are single-layer, not futanoki).
- **Rafters:** parallel in the body of each eave, but **fan-shaped only at the corners** (隅扇垂木 *sumi-ōgi-daruki*), radiating from the corner.
- **虹梁 kōryō with round section**, and **大瓶束 taiheizuka** (large bottle-shaped strut) on the kōryō, notably inside Jōdodō.
- **No ceiling:** the entire roof structure is exposed (化粧屋根裏), which is why every member is shaped and finished.
- **Doors:** framed panel doors (桟唐戸) pivoted in 藁座 (*waraza*) blocks.
- **Standardisation:** members are few in cross-section type and repeated, a rationalisation suited to the enormous scale and to the scarcity of large timber in the 1180s–1190s.

**R12-025** — **Must.** Sashi-hijiki tenons shall pass through the column (or deep into it) and be locked with wedges or pins, and the column shall be sized so that the mortises of all tiers at one level together remove no more than about one third of its section in any horizontal plane. *Why:* multiple tiers of through-mortises weaken the column exactly where the eave moment enters it; the daibutsuyō columns are very large for that reason. The one-third figure is a prudent guideline, not a historical rule — check each case.

**R12-026** — **Must.** Mortises for sashi-hijiki and nuki at different levels shall be staggered so that no two mortises in the same direction lie at the same height, and mortises in the two orthogonal directions are offset vertically by at least the member depth where the geometry allows. *Why:* coincident mortises leave a column "necked" at one level; this is the classic failure mode of heavily nuki-ed columns (→ Ch. 10 on nuki holes).

**R12-027** — **Should.** Where a yūri-odaruki is used, its tail restraint shall be a positive bearing under a member carrying dead load (e.g. under a nuki or beam), not a pin. *Why:* same principle as R12-012.

### 4.4 Zenshūyō (禅宗様), also called Karayō (唐様)
Introduced with Zen Buddhism from Song China in the 13th century (Kenchō-ji, Engaku-ji and others in Kamakura; Kyoto Gozan). The earliest precisely dated survivor is the Butsuden of Kōzan-ji, Shimonoseki (1320); others include Shōfuku-ji Jizōdō, Tokyo (1407), and the Shariden of Engaku-ji, Kamakura (generally dated to the 15th century, moved from another temple). The octagonal three-storey pagoda of Anraku-ji (Nagano) is a Zen-style pagoda of the late Kamakura period.

**Characteristic members and joints**
- **詰組 tsumegumi:** bracket sets both on and between columns, carried on the **台輪 daiwa**, a wide flat plate over the kashiranuki (R12-020).
- **Brackets** with odaruki whose projecting ends are cut in a characteristic profile, and blocks set with a pronounced inward curve; arm ends with a distinctive curved profile; 拳鼻 (*kobushibana*, "fist-nose") ends to the projecting arms and nuki.
- **扇垂木 ōgi-daruki:** rafters **fan out across the whole eave** from a point on the building's axis at each corner quadrant, not only at the corners. The layout is a kikujutsu problem in its own right (→ Ch. 05; → Ch. 11 fan rafters).
- **粽 chimaki:** columns taper (rounded "rice-dumpling" profile) at top and bottom ends.
- **礎盤 soban:** a plinth, stone or wood, between the foundation stone and the column foot, often with a moulded profile.
- **海老虹梁 ebi-kōryō:** a "shrimp-shaped" curved tie beam connecting a lower outer column (e.g. of the mokoshi, 裳階) to a higher inner column, curving upward as it goes in.
- **大瓶束 taiheizuka** on kōryō; **拳鼻** and **象鼻** ornaments.
- **花頭窓 katōmado:** windows with a bell- or flame-shaped head.
- **桟唐戸 sankarado:** frame-and-panel doors, pivoted in **藁座 waraza** (projecting pivot blocks on the lintel and sill).
- **弓欄間 yumiranma:** bow-shaped (curved-slat) transom panels above the doors; also 波欄間 (wave pattern).
- **鏡天井 kagami-tenjō:** a flat, smooth board ceiling ("mirror ceiling") over the central space, often painted with a dragon.
- **Mokoshi (裳階):** an enclosing pent roof at the lowest level, making a one-storey building look two-storeyed.
- **Floor:** stone or tile paving at ground level (土間, 瓦敷), not raised wooden floors.

**R12-028** — **Must.** Fan rafters (ōgi-daruki) shall each be individually laid out from the fan centre, and each rafter's seat on the gangyō and the wall purlin shall be cut to its own angle; do not "average" the angles. *Why:* each fan rafter meets the purlins at a different angle; averaged seats produce twisted rafters and an uneven kayaoi.

**R12-029** — **Should.** In Zen-style work the daiwa shall be a continuous member around the building with its splices over columns, lapped at corners with ends projecting (and shaped) beyond the corner columns. *Why:* the daiwa is the ring beam for the tsumegumi; a discontinuity is a weak point under the mid-bay sets.

**R12-030** — **Should.** Ebi-kōryō shall be cut from naturally curved stock whose grain follows the curve as far as possible; where sawn from straight timber, the section at the steepest point of curvature must retain continuous fibres over at least half its depth. *Why:* short grain in the curve leads to splitting along the grain at the column tenon.

### 4.5 Setchūyō (折衷様) and shin-wayō (新和様)
- **Shin-wayō** ("new Japanese style"): wayō buildings that adopt daibutsuyō's **penetrating nuki** and **kibana** while otherwise remaining wayō (from the late Kamakura period, especially in the Kinai). Example commonly cited: Taisan-ji Hondō, Kobe (early 14th c.).
- **Setchūyō** ("eclectic style"): a wayō frame with daibutsuyō and zenshūyō details together — nuki, kibana, sankarado, katōmado, sometimes ebi-kōryō and taiheizuka inside. Representative examples: Kakurin-ji Hondō (1397), Kanshin-ji Kondō (Nanbokuchō period, 14th c.), Myōō-in Hondō, Fukuyama (1321), Jōdo-ji Hondō, Onomichi (1327).
- From the Muromachi period on, most temple building is effectively setchūyō; the pure categories are an analytic tool.

**R12-031** — **Should.** When documenting or reproducing a building, record the style of each element (frame ties, brackets, rafters, openings, ornament) separately rather than labelling the whole building by one style. *Why:* setchūyō buildings combine systems member by member; a single label hides which structural system is actually in use.

**R12-032** — **Must.** Once penetrating nuki are adopted in a wayō frame (shin-wayō, setchūyō), they shall be wedged (楔 *kusabi*) at the column and their holes laid out per R12-026. *Why:* an unwedged nuki gives little lateral stiffness; the whole point of adopting nuki is the wedged frictional moment connection (→ Ch. 10, → Ch. 17).

## 5. Kiwari for temples and shrines; rokushigake and shiwari

### 5.1 The two base modules
Japanese proportional systems for religious buildings use one of two bases (→ Ch. 01 for the texts, → Ch. 02 for the general kiwari method):

1. **Column diameter** (柱径 / 柱の太さ) derived from the bay span. The Tokugawa master carpenter **Heinouchi (Heinōchi) Masanobu** (平内政信) compiled **『匠明』 *Shōmei*** (dated 1608), in five books — 門記集 (gates), 社記集 (shrines), 堂記集 (halls), 塔記集 (pagodas), 殿屋集 (residences). Shōmei is notable for taking the **column** as its unit: the central bay span (中の間 *naka-no-ma*) is chosen, the column diameter is set as a fraction of it (commonly quoted as one tenth; some entries and schools use about one twelfth or other fractions), and most other members are given as multiples or fractions of the column.
2. **Rafter module** (枝 *shi*, one rafter width + one gap = rafter centre-to-centre spacing). From the Kamakura period on, bay spans were increasingly fixed as whole numbers of *shi* (枝割 *shiwari*, → Ch. 02), and later kiwari books of several schools derive the bracket sizes from the rafter module. In mature practice the two systems interlock: the bays are integer *shi*, and the column and brackets are sized so that the bracket blocks fall correctly under the rafters (rokushigake, §5.3).

**R12-033** — **Must.** Choose and declare one governing module (column- or shi-based) at the start of design, and derive all subordinate dimensions from it; never mix unrelated nominal sizes into a kiwari scheme. *Why:* the aesthetic and the fit of bracket to rafter both depend on every member being a rational function of the same unit.

### 5.3 Rokushigake (六枝掛) — the six-rafter rule

**Definition.** *Rokushigake* is the proportional convention, established in the Kamakura period (becoming standard by the later 13th–14th century), in which **exactly six rafters (six *shi*) sit over the three makito of a mitsudo**, two over each makito, and the bracket dimensions are fixed accordingly:

- makito width (top, along the arm) = **2 rafter widths + 1 gap = w + S**;
- the two rafters over each makito have their outer faces flush with the makito faces;
- makito centres are **2 S** apart; hence
- the outer-to-outer length over the end makito = **5 S + w**;
- the column centre line (= centre of the middle makito) falls at the **centre of a gap between two rafters** (柱真は垂木の間真), never under a rafter.

```
   Rokushigake: plan of rafters over a mitsudo (looking down), schematic

   rafters:   ▮   ▮   ▮   ▮   ▮   ▮
             |<S>|
   makito:  [▮ _ ▮] [▮ _ ▮] [▮ _ ▮]
             end     centre   end
                       ↑
              column centre = centre of the gap between the two middle rafters
   makito width = w + S ; makito c/c = 2S ; overall = 5S + w
```

**Consequences:**
- Because the column centre is at a gap centre, **every bay span is a whole number of S**, and the layout of rafters from bay to bay is continuous and symmetrical about every column.
- The bracket arm, the makito and the daito are all dimensioned from S and w, so **choosing S fixes the bracket set**, and choosing the number of S in each bay fixes the plan.
- The same logic extends to multi-step sets: the outer tiers' makito are placed so that the rafters over the gangyō are also in the 2-per-makito relation.

**R12-034** — **Must.** In a rokushigake building, all bay spans shall be integer multiples of S, and the column centre lines shall fall at rafter-gap centres. *Why:* any fractional bay throws the six-rafter alignment out in that bay and in every bay beyond it; the error is immediately visible from below.

**R12-035** — **Must.** Makito width in a rokushigake building = w + S exactly (to the bu), measured at the top of the block. *Why:* this is the identity on which the rule rests. Note the tojiri is narrower than the top; measure at the top.

**R12-036** — **Should.** Corner bays and the corner set are adjusted (e.g. by extended arms or additional makito, or by a slightly different end bay count) so that the rafters over the corner resolve symmetrically on both faces. *Why:* at the corner the two faces' rafter grids meet the hip; historical buildings solve this with various local adjustments that should be recorded in any survey.

**Historical note.** Not every medieval building follows rokushigake; research (e.g. studies of "non-rokushigake" buildings in the *Journal of Architecture and Planning*, AIJ) documents halls where bay spans and three-block arms are related to the rafters by other ratios. Rokushigake is therefore a norm of the Kamakura–Edo mainstream, not a universal law.

### 5.4 How shiwari aligns brackets and rafters (→ Ch. 02 §shiwari)
Shiwari is the method of expressing every horizontal dimension of the elevation in *shi*: bays, bracket spacing, eave projections, even the projection of the corner. With rokushigake it becomes a closed system: the designer picks (1) the rafter size and spacing, hence S, and (2) the number of S for each bay; everything else follows.

**R12-037** — **Must.** Lay out the rafter grid on the story pole (間竿 *kenzao*) or full-size drawing (原寸図 *genzunzu*) **before** cutting any bracket member. *Why:* the brackets are dependent on the rafter grid, not the other way round; this is the reverse of the order a Western framer would assume.

### 5.5 Worked example — deriving a three-bay hall from S

*Illustrative only; numbers are chosen to show the method and are within representative ranges, not taken from a specific building.*

**Brief:** a three-bay-square (三間堂, 方三間) wayō hall with de-mitsudo/degumi sets on columns, kento-zuka as infill, futanoki parallel rafters, rokushigake.

**Step 1 — rafter module.**
Choose rafter width w = 3.0 sun (91 mm), depth 3.6 sun (109 mm), hon-shige gap = depth = 3.6 sun.
→ S = w + gap = **6.6 sun (200 mm)**.

**Step 2 — bays in S.**
Central bay = 18 S = 118.8 sun = **11.88 shaku (3.60 m)**; side bays = 15 S = **9.90 shaku (3.00 m)**. Overall front (column centres) = 48 S = 31.68 shaku (9.60 m). Same on the flank.

**Step 3 — column.**
D ≈ central bay / 10 = 1.19 shaku → take **D = 1.2 shaku (364 mm)** at the head (slightly larger at the foot if the column is tapered).

**Step 4 — bracket set (rokushigake).**
- Makito width = w + S = 3.0 + 6.6 = **9.6 sun**; height ≈ 0.6 × 9.6 ≈ **5.8 sun**.
- Makito c/c = 2 S = **13.2 sun**.
- Three-block arm, outer faces of end makito = 5 S + w = 33 + 3 = **36 sun (3.6 shaku, 1.09 m)**, i.e. ≈ 3 D.
- Arm depth ≈ 1.1 × makito height ≈ **6.4 sun**; arm width ≈ makito tojiri width ≈ **6.5 sun** (fit to slot).
- Daito width ≈ 1.2 D ≈ **14.4 sun**; height ≈ 0.65 × 14.4 ≈ **9.4 sun**.
- One-step tesaki (degumi, if used on the front): 2 S = **13.2 sun** from wall centre to gangyō centre, keeping the gangyō on the rafter grid.

**Step 5 — frame ties.**
Kashiranuki ≈ 0.55 D × 0.4 D ≈ **6.6 × 4.8 sun**; uchinori-nageshi depth ≈ 0.6 D ≈ **7.2 sun**.

**Step 6 — heights (check against precedent).**
Column height ≈ 1.1 × central bay ≈ **13 shaku (3.94 m)** floor to head; bracket stack (daito + arm + makito + sanehijiki + gangyō) adds ≈ 3–3.5 shaku; eave line and roof pitch then follow from → Ch. 11 and Ch. 05.

**Step 7 — rafters over the bay.**
Central bay of 18 S: the column set carries 6 rafters (3 S either side of the column centre); between two column sets, 18 − 6 = 12 S are left, i.e. 12 rafters; a kento-zuka at mid-bay with a makito carries the middle two of those. Each side bay (15 S) gives 15 − 6 = 9 S between sets; with an odd count, the mid-bay strut cannot sit symmetrically under two rafters — **the designer would adjust side bays to 14 or 16 S**. (This is exactly the kind of check that shiwari makes routine.) Revise: side bays = **16 S = 10.56 shaku (3.20 m)**, overall front 50 S = 33.0 shaku (10.0 m).

**Step 8 — record.**
Write S, w, all bay counts and every derived size on the kenzao and in the drawing title block; all shop templates are made from these.

**R12-038** — **Should.** When bays carry a mid-bay nakazonae with a makito, choose bay counts (in S) so that the number of S between adjacent column sets is **even**, allowing the infill makito to sit symmetrically under two rafters. *Why:* shown in Step 7; an odd count leaves the infill block off-centre relative to the rafters.

**R12-039** — **Should.** Check the column height to bay ratio and the overall eave depth against at least two surveyed buildings of the same style, rank and period before fixing them. *Why:* heights are the least standardised part of kiwari and the part most responsible for "wrong-looking" reproductions.

## 6. Pagodas (塔, *tō*)

### 6.1 Types and principal examples
The Japanese Buddhist pagoda is a timber tower of three (三重塔 *sanjū-no-tō*) or five (五重塔 *gojū-no-tō*) storeys, square in plan (octagonal and two-storey 多宝塔 *tahōtō* forms also exist). It is a reliquary monument, not a usable multi-storey building: the upper storeys have no floors to use.

| Pagoda | Date (as commonly given) | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Hōryū-ji Five-storey Pagoda | late 7th c. (rebuilding after 670 fire); shinbashira felled 594 (dendrochronology) | Asuka-style cloud brackets; shinso originally below ground |
| Hokki-ji Three-storey Pagoda | c. 706 | oldest three-storey pagoda |
| Yakushi-ji East Pagoda | 730 | three storeys each with a mokoshi (appears six-roofed); mitesaki; dismantling repair 2009–2020 |
| Murō-ji Five-storey Pagoda | c. 800 | smallest outdoor five-storey pagoda (c. 16 m) |
| Daigo-ji Five-storey Pagoda | 951 | oldest building in Kyoto city |
| Kōfuku-ji Five-storey Pagoda | 1426 (rebuilding) | c. 50 m |
| Rurikō-ji Five-storey Pagoda, Yamaguchi | 1442 | Muromachi |
| Tō-ji Five-storey Pagoda | 1644 | tallest timber pagoda in Japan, c. 55 m |
| Nikkō Tōshōgū Five-storey Pagoda | 1818 (rebuilding of 1650 original) | suspended shinbashira |

### 6.2 Structural anatomy
- **側柱** (*gawabashira*): the outer columns of each storey (12 for a three-by-three-bay plan).
- **四天柱** (*shitenbashira*): the four inner columns around the centre, framing the innermost bay where the shinbashira stands and around which the first storey's Buddhist images are placed.
- **心柱** (*shinbashira*): the central pillar, rising from the foundation (or from a higher level, §6.3) through all storeys to carry the **相輪** (*sōrin*) finial above the top roof. It is **not** a column of the storey frames; the storeys are built around it with clearance.
- **Storeys as independent layered frames.** Each storey is a complete frame of columns, head ties, brackets and eaves. The upper storey's columns do **not** continue the lower columns; they stand on a ring of sills/beams (柱盤 *hashiraban*) laid on the lower storey's roof framing and bracket tiers, set inward by the reduction (§6.4). The whole tower is therefore a stack of frames resting on each other by gravity, with the shinbashira threaded through the centre.
- **Sōrin** (相輪): from bottom to top — 露盤 (*roban*, the base box on the roof), 伏鉢 (*fukubachi*, inverted bowl), 請花 (*ukebana*, lotus), 九輪 (*kurin*, nine rings), 水煙 (*suien*, flame-shaped "water smoke"), 竜車 (*ryūsha*), 宝珠 (*hōju*, jewel). The metal sōrin is fitted over the top of the shinbashira and is heavy; it is carried by the shinbashira, not by the top roof.

**R12-040** — **Must.** The shinbashira shall pass through each storey with a clearance and shall not be rigidly framed into the storey frames; any connecting members (where present) must allow vertical sliding. *Why:* the storey frames shrink and settle far more than the vertical-grained shinbashira (they are made of many horizontal, cross-grain layers — brackets, beams, hashiraban). A shinbashira framed rigidly into them would either hang the storeys or lift the sōrin off the roof.

**R12-041** — **Must.** Provide for differential settlement at the junction of the top roof and the sōrin (roban): the roof covering around the shinbashira must be able to drop relative to the pillar without opening a leak. *Why:* over decades the frame settles by centimetres; historically, gaps at the roban and the need to trim the base of the shinbashira during repairs are well documented, and the suspended shinbashira of later pagodas is one answer (§6.3).

**R12-042** — **Must.** Upper-storey columns shall stand on a continuous hashiraban ring carried on the lower storey's structural tiers (brackets, beams or cantilevers), never directly on rafters or roof boarding. *Why:* the upper storey is a point load on the lower storey's eave structure; only the bracket and beam tiers are designed to take it.

### 6.3 Shinbashira foundations — the sequence of types
| Type | Japanese | Description | Representative |
|---|---|---|---|
| Underground shinso | 地下式心礎 | the shinbashira stands on a foundation stone (心礎 *shinso*) buried well below ground, the relic chamber in the stone | Asuka period; Hōryū-ji (the buried portion of the pillar rotted, leaving a cavity found during the 1940s–50s repair) |
| Ground-level shinso | 地上式心礎 | the shinso sits at or near the podium surface | Nara period onward |
| Raised shinbashira | 心柱を梁上に立てる | the shinbashira starts on beams at the first storey (or higher), not on a stone — reported for some later (Edo-period) pagodas; the relic function moves upward | later period; flag as less common |
| Suspended shinbashira | 懸垂式 / 吊り心柱 | the shinbashira hangs from the upper storeys (at Nikkō, from the fourth storey by chains), its foot clear of the shinso by a small gap (popular accounts give c. 10 cm) | Nikkō Tōshōgū (1818) and some other Edo-period pagodas |

**R12-043** — **Should.** Where a suspended shinbashira is used, the gap at the foot shall be sized to the expected long-term settlement of the storeys plus a margin, and must be inspected at every maintenance cycle. *Why:* the gap closes as the frames shrink; once the foot bears, the hanging support is overloaded or the sōrin–roof junction opens.

**R12-044** — **Must.** The shinbashira is usually made of several lengths spliced vertically; such splices shall be designed for axial compression and for bending under lateral sway, e.g. long scarf or *kanawa-tsugi*-type splices with iron bands (→ Ch. 07, → Ch. 09), placed at levels where the pillar is laterally supported by clearance collars. *Why:* the shinbashira sways and may strike the storey framing in an earthquake; a splice that can hinge concentrates that impact.

### 6.4 Reduction of storeys (逓減率, *teigenritsu*)
- *Teigen* is the progressive reduction in plan width of each successive storey. The *teigenritsu* is usually expressed as the ratio of the top storey's width (at column centres) to the first storey's.
- Early pagodas have strong reduction — Hōryū-ji's fifth storey is roughly half the width of its first (ratio ≈ 0.5), giving a steep, tapering silhouette. Later pagodas (especially Edo-period) show much less reduction (ratios commonly quoted in the 0.6–0.7+ range), giving a more cylindrical, vertical look. Treat these as approximate characterisations.
- Reduction is achieved by reducing bay width (often keeping three bays per face but shrinking each in whole *shi*), and in some cases reducing the number of bays at upper storeys (Hōryū-ji: three bays below, two at the top).
- Eave projection is also reduced storey by storey, but usually less than proportionally, so upper roofs appear to overhang generously.

**R12-045** — **Must.** In a shiwari-designed pagoda each storey's bay widths shall be whole numbers of *shi*, with the reduction taken in whole *shi* per storey per bay (or per face). *Why:* each storey has its own complete rafter layout and bracket alignment (§5.3); fractional reductions break rokushigake storey by storey.

**R12-046** — **Should.** Set out all storeys' column lines together on one full-size plan (原寸図) and on the story pole for the shinbashira before cutting. *Why:* the vertical alignment of storey centres, the shinbashira clearances and the eave overlaps are only verifiable as a whole; errors discovered at the fourth storey cannot be corrected.

### 6.5 Earthquake behaviour — hypotheses, stated carefully
It is commonly stated that no timber pagoda in Japan is known to have collapsed from earthquake shaking alone, while pagodas have been lost to fire, lightning and wind (e.g. Shitennō-ji's pagoda in the Muroto typhoon of 1934). The claim about earthquakes is widely repeated and broadly accepted but rests on incomplete historical records; treat it as "no well-documented case" rather than proof of invulnerability.

Explanations proposed include:
1. **Stacked-frame flexibility and friction damping.** Each storey rests on the one below with dowelled, not rigidly fixed, joints; bracket sets with many bearing surfaces dissipate energy by friction and small slips (→ Ch. 17).
2. **Long natural period.** A tall, flexible timber tower has a natural period longer than the dominant period of many earthquakes, reducing resonant response.
3. **Heavy, deep eaves as balancing masses**, keeping each storey's centre of mass close to its support.
4. **Shinbashira as a limiter/damper.** When storeys sway in opposite directions (higher modes), they strike the shinbashira, which acts like a pole restraining excessive relative displacement (sometimes explained as a *kannuki* "bolt" effect).
5. **"Snake dance" (蛇踊り) hypothesis:** the storeys sway alternately left and right like the segments of a dancing snake, so the tower as a whole does not develop a large overall overturning motion. This was popularised in Japanese books on pagoda structure in the 1990s (e.g. 上田篤 ed., 『五重塔はなぜ倒れないか』, Shinchōsha, 1996); it is a qualitative model, not an established engineering result.

Scale-model shaking-table tests and analytical studies have been carried out in Japan; they generally support the importance of joint friction and storey-by-storey flexibility, while the specific contribution of the shinbashira remains debated.

**Tokyo Skytree (2012).** Its designers adopted a "shinbashira vibration control" (心柱制振) system: a central reinforced-concrete cylinder, structurally separate from the outer steel tower in its upper part and coupled to it by oil dampers, acting as a counter-phase mass. The designers and operators explicitly cite the pagoda shinbashira as inspiration. **Flag:** this is an inspiration by analogy; the Skytree's mechanism (engineered added-mass damping) is not a reproduction of any demonstrated pagoda mechanism, and should not be cited as proof of how pagodas work.

**R12-047** — **Must.** In repair or new construction, do not add rigid bracing, steel frames or fixed connections between shinbashira and storeys to "improve" earthquake performance without a full structural analysis that accounts for the stacked-frame behaviour. *Why:* stiffening changes the dynamic behaviour that has, empirically, served these buildings well; it can shift forces into joints never designed for them (→ Ch. 16, Ch. 17).

## 7. Shrine styles (神社建築)

### 7.1 Overview
Shrine main halls (本殿 *honden*) are classified by plan, entrance side and roof form. Two basic distinctions:
- **平入 *hirairi*:** entrance on the eave (long, non-gable) side.
- **妻入 *tsumairi*:** entrance on the gable end.

Other key terms: 千木 (*chigi*, crossed finials at the gable ends of the ridge), 鰹木 (*katsuogi*, short cylindrical logs laid across the ridge), 棟持柱 (*munamochi-bashira*, free-standing ridge-support posts at the gables), 掘立柱 (*hottate-bashira*, posts set directly in the ground), 高床 (*takayuka*, raised floor), 向拝 (*kōhai*, entrance canopy), 庇 (*hisashi*, aisle/pent), 檜皮葺 (*hiwadabuki*, cypress-bark roofing), 萱葺 (*kayabuki*, thatch), 高欄 (*kōran*, balustrade).

### 7.2 Shinmei-zukuri (神明造)
- **Plan:** rectangular, hirairi, raised floor surrounded by a veranda with balustrade; central stair on the long side. At Ise the main sanctuary (正殿 *shōden*) is three bays by two.
- **Frame:** round posts set directly in the ground (**hottate-bashira**) without foundation stones; walls of thick horizontal planks (板壁) let into grooves in the posts; a **munamochi-bashira** stands free outside each gable wall, set in the ground and supporting the ridge directly.
- **Roof:** straight (no *sori*) gable of thick thatch (at Ise, *kaya*); the barge boards (破風 *hafu*) extend through the roof to become the **chigi**; **katsuogi** weigh down the ridge. At Ise the Naikū shōden has 10 katsuogi and chigi cut horizontally at the tips (内削ぎ *uchisogi*), the Gekū shōden 9 katsuogi and vertically cut tips (外削ぎ *sotosogi*) — commonly cited, and a frequently used (though not universal) convention associating uchisogi with female and sotosogi with male deities elsewhere.
- **Ise-specific:** the exact Ise form is called **唯一神明造** (*yuiitsu shinmei-zukuri*) and by custom is not copied exactly by other shrines. The **心御柱** (*shin-no-mihashira*) is a sacred post under the centre of the floor, not structural and not visible; its site on the alternate plot (古殿地 *kodenchi*) is protected by a small shelter between rebuildings.

**R12-048** — **Must.** Hottate-bashira shall be charred or otherwise protected only as the shrine's tradition prescribes and shall be backfilled with rammed earth in layers, the post plumb in both directions before tamping. *Why:* an earth-fast post gets all its lateral fixity from the compacted backfill; it is also why earth-fast shrines are inherently limited-life structures (hence the Ise cycle, §9).

**R12-049** — **Must.** The munamochi-bashira shall carry the ridge independently of the gable wall and shall not be framed into the wall planks. *Why:* the wall planks shrink and settle; the free-standing ridge post keeps the ridge line while the walls move.

**R12-050** — **Should.** Plank walls let into post grooves shall be fitted with room to shrink and settle (no nailing across the grain to the posts). *Why:* horizontal plank walls lose height as they dry; a nailed plank hangs and splits.

### 7.3 Taisha-zukuri (大社造)
- **Plan:** square, two bays by two, nine posts: four corners, four mid-side posts and the central **心御柱** (*shin-no-mihashira*), which in Izumo is a real, very large structural post. The mid-posts of the gable walls are the **宇豆柱** (*uzubashira*), carrying the ridge and set slightly outside the wall line.
- **Entrance:** on the gable (tsumairi) but in the **right-hand bay**, not the centre, reached by a long covered stair.
- **Interior:** a partition runs from the central post towards one side wall, so the path turns and the enshrined deity's seat faces sideways (at Izumo to the west) — the plan is spatially asymmetric.
- **Roof:** gable with curved (反り) surfaces, cypress-bark roofing, chigi and (at Izumo) three katsuogi.
- **Scale:** the present Izumo honden (1744) is about 24 m (8 *jō*) high. Excavations in 2000 revealed post bases of three bundled cedar logs forming columns of c. 3 m overall diameter, dated to the 13th century, lending support to traditions of an even taller earlier hall; the reconstruction of heights beyond this remains hypothetical.

**R12-051** — **Must.** In a taisha plan the central post and the uzubashira are primary structure; their diameter shall exceed that of the corner posts (in Izumo substantially so), and floor and ridge beams shall be framed to them. *Why:* the central post takes the floor and roof loads of a very tall, heavy hall; treating it as a symbolic post would under-design the structure.

### 7.4 Sumiyoshi-zukuri (住吉造)
- Long, narrow, tsumairi hall (four bays deep, two wide), divided into outer (外陣) and inner (内陣) chambers; straight gable roof with no curve, cypress bark; chigi and katsuogi; no veranda.
- Frame posts on stones; board walls; posts painted vermilion, walls white — the colour scheme is part of the type.
- At Sumiyoshi Taisha the four honden face west; three stand in line one behind another and the fourth beside the third.

### 7.5 Kasuga-zukuri (春日造)
- A one-bay-square tsumairi hall with a front canopy (kōhai) over the stair. Curved gable roof of cypress bark with a pent roof at the front.
- The body sits on a sill frame (**井桁土台** *igeta-dodai*) rather than on individual foundation stones — a detail associated with the periodic rebuilding/relocation (式年造替) of Kasuga Taisha, whose honden are four identical small halls side by side.
- Common in the Nara region for small shrines.

**R12-052** — **Should.** A Kasuga-type igeta sill shall be joined at its corners with interlocking laps (相欠き/渡り腮, → Ch. 08) and the posts tenoned into it; the sill shall bear on stones or a stone plinth continuously enough to prevent racking of the frame. *Why:* the sill is the only base; it must work as a rigid ring.

### 7.6 Nagare-zukuri (流造)
- The most common honden type in Japan. Hirairi; a gable roof whose **front slope is extended** (流れる, "flows") to cover the front stair as the kōhai, supported on kōhai posts.
- Sizes from one-bay (一間社流造) to three-bay (三間社流造) and larger; small ones often built on a sill frame (土台) like Kasuga.
- The ridge usually has neither chigi nor katsuogi in the older examples, though many have them.
- **Structure of the extended slope:** the front rafters run continuously from the ridge over the main purlin to the kōhai purlin with a gentle change of curvature; the kōhai posts are joined to the body by **海老虹梁** or straight tie beams (繋ぎ虹梁) and the gap between the bracket and the sloping rafters is filled by a **手挟** (*tebasami*, §10.6).
- **Variant:** 両流造 (*ryōnagare*) — both slopes extended (Itsukushima).

**R12-053** — **Must.** Where the front slope is extended over a kōhai, the kōhai posts and their tie beams to the main frame shall be designed to carry the full extended roof load; the tie beam shall be tenoned and pinned (or wedged) into the main post, not simply seated. *Why:* the kōhai is an outrigger; a seated tie lets the kōhai posts rotate outward under the eave load.

### 7.7 Hachiman-zukuri (八幡造)
- Two parallel gabled halls, hirairi — the front 外殿 (*gaiden*) and the rear 内殿 (*naiden*) — joined under a common valley with a horizontal gutter between their roofs; the linking space (相の間 *ai-no-ma*) forms a single interior.
- Representative: Usa Jingū (Kyushu; honden rebuilt 1855–61), Iwashimizu Hachimangū (1634).

**R12-054** — **Must.** The valley gutter between the two roofs of a hachiman honden shall be a continuous, generously sized trough with falls to both ends and robust overflow provision, and the eaves of both halls shall be framed to carry it. *Why:* a valley between two long roofs concentrates all rain from two slopes into one line; blockage or undersizing floods the ai-no-ma — historically a chronic maintenance point.

### 7.8 Hie-zukuri (日吉造)
- Hirairi core of three bays by two with hisashi on the front and both sides but **not** at the rear; the rear roof slope is cut back sharply, giving a unique profile (sometimes called 聖帝造 *shōtei-zukuri*).
- Raised on a high floor with an under-floor room (下殿 *gedono*) at Hiyoshi Taisha (Ōtsu).

### 7.9 Gongen-zukuri (権現造)
- The honden (rear) and haiden (front, worship hall) are linked by the **石の間** (*ishi-no-ma*), a connecting passage whose floor is **lower** than both halls (originally stone-paved, hence the name; in many examples later floored in timber or lacquer at the lower level). The whole forms an H- or 工-shaped plan under a complex roof with many gables — hence also 八棟造 (*yatsumune-zukuri*).
- Kitano Tenmangū (1607) is often cited as the prototype; the name derives from Tōshō Daigongen, the deified Tokugawa Ieyasu, whose Nikkō Tōshōgū (rebuilt 1636 under Iemitsu) is the most elaborate example.
- Construction specifics: multiple irimoya and chidori-hafu roofs intersecting over the ishi-no-ma require complex valleys (→ Ch. 11 valley framing); dense polychrome carving (§10).

**R12-055** — **Must.** In gongen-zukuri the three roof masses (honden, ishi-no-ma, haiden) shall be framed so that each hall is independently stable, with the ishi-no-ma roof framed between them without transferring thrust. *Why:* the halls differ in height and weight and settle differently; a rigidly shared frame would crack at the junctions and open the valleys.

### 7.10 Gion-zukuri (祇園造)
- Honden and worship hall enclosed together under one large hipped-gable (irimoya) roof with hisashi and *magobisashi* around — Yasaka Jinja, Kyoto (1654). It reflects the historic Buddhist–Shinto amalgamation of the site, with a temple-like enclosed hall.

### 8.3 Joinery
- **Pillar head:** a tenon (枘) at the pillar top passes through the shimaki (or daiwa + shimaki) and into the underside of the kasagi (or the kasagi is dowelled to the shimaki). The kasagi and shimaki together act as a built-up lintel.
- **Nuki:** passes through rectangular mortises in both pillars. The mortise is cut slightly larger than the nuki in height; the nuki is driven, and a **wedge (楔)** is driven into the remaining space (usually above the nuki at each pillar face on the outside, visible), locking the nuki and pulling the frame tight. In myōjin-type torii the nuki ends project beyond the pillars.
- **Gakuzuka:** tenoned into the underside of the shimaki and into the top of the nuki at mid-span; it transfers part of the lintel load to the nuki and keeps nuki and lintels parallel.
- **Kasagi splices:** long kasagi are spliced (e.g. *kanawa-tsugi* or *okkake-daisen-tsugi*, → Ch. 07) directly above a pillar or at mid-span over the gakuzuka, never at an unsupported point; the top is often protected by a copper cap (銅板包み).
- **Base:** pillars are either earth-fast (掘立) or seated on stone (礎石, sometimes with a buried extension), with kamebara or nemaki protecting the vulnerable foot from splash and rot. At Itsukushima the great torii stands by its own weight in the tidal flat on a pile foundation; the kasagi is a box filled with stones (weights of several tonnes are commonly quoted) to increase its stability.
- **Lean (転び):** in myōjin and related types the pillars lean inward toward each other at the top by a small amount; the pillar head and foot are cut square to the true vertical/horizontal of the lintels and base, not to the pillar axis.

**R12-056** — **Must.** The nuki of a torii shall be locked at each pillar by a wedge (kusabi) driven in the mortise, and the wedges shall be re-driven after the first seasons of drying. *Why:* the nuki–pillar wedge is the torii's only moment connection in the plane of the gate; as the timber shrinks the wedge loosens and the gate racks.

**R12-057** — **Must.** Pillars with inward lean (korobi) shall have their head tenon and foot cut to the *true* vertical and horizontal lines struck from a centre line, and the nuki mortises shall be laid out horizontally (not perpendicular to the leaning pillar axis). *Why:* this is the same "tilted post" layout used in kikujutsu (→ Ch. 05); cutting to the pillar axis tilts the lintel seats and the nuki.

**R12-058** — **Should.** The pillar foot of an earth-fast or stone-set torii shall be protected by kamebara, nemaki or a copper sleeve and inspected at regular intervals. *Why:* nearly all torii failures are foot rot at ground level; the top members last far longer.

### 8.4 Proportions (qualitative, representative)
Torii proportions vary greatly: modern surveyed examples have pillar spacing from about 4 to 8 pillar diameters and heights from about 6 to 10 pillar diameters. Kiwari books and modern manufacturers' tables relate members to the pillar diameter D roughly as follows (**representative only; school- and shrine-specific**):

| Member | Relation to pillar diameter D |
|---|---|
| Kasagi depth | ≈ 0.6–1.0 D, larger at the ends where *sorimashi* is used |
| Shimaki depth | ≈ 0.5–0.7 D |
| Kasagi overhang beyond pillar | ≈ 1.0–2.0 D |
| Nuki depth × width | ≈ 0.5–0.7 D × 0.3–0.5 D |
| Nuki projection beyond pillar (myōjin) | ≈ 0.5–1.0 D |
| Gakuzuka width | ≈ 0.5–0.8 D |
| Korobi (inward lean) | small — commonly of the order of a few percent of height; take from the school's kiwari or precedent |

**R12-059** — **Should.** Reproduce a specific shrine's torii only from its measured drawings or the shrine's own records; use generic ratios only for new, unprecedented torii. *Why:* shrines treat their torii form as part of their identity; generic proportions produce a recognisably "wrong" gate.

## 9. Ise Jingū and the shikinen sengū as knowledge transmission

The **式年遷宮** (*shikinen sengū*) of Ise Jingū rebuilds the main sanctuaries of the Inner (Naikū) and Outer (Gekū) Shrines, together with many subsidiary buildings, bridges and sacred treasures, on alternate adjacent plots **every 20 years**. Tradition dates the first sengū to 690 (Naikū) under Empress Jitō. The cycle was interrupted for over a century in the Sengoku period and restored in the late 16th century. The 62nd sengū took place in 2013; the 63rd is scheduled for 2033.

As a construction system it is notable for (details of repair philosophy in → Ch. 16):
- **Generational overlap:** a 20-year interval means that a carpenter typically takes part in two or three cycles — as apprentice, as experienced hand and as master — so that the full sequence of tasks is transmitted in person.
- **Fixed forms with materials renewed:** the earth-fast posts and thatch have a service life of the order of the cycle; rebuilding before decay replaces the need for long-term durability.
- **Timber supply planning:** around ten thousand hinoki logs are commonly quoted per sengū; since the Edo period much of it has come from the Kiso region (the 御杣山 *misomayama*), and the shrine has managed its own forests since the 1920s to supply future cycles.
- **Ritual sequence** tied to construction stages: timber felling (御杣始祭), hauling of timber by the public (御木曳 *okihiki*), post-raising (立柱祭), ridge-raising (上棟祭) and the transfer (遷御 *sengyo*).
- **Reuse of timber:** dismantled members are reused elsewhere; commonly cited is the reuse of the old shōden munamochi-bashira as the torii at each end of the Uji Bridge, which in turn pass to other shrines after a further cycle.

**R12-060** — **Should.** Where a periodic-rebuilding tradition exists, the rebuilding shall reproduce the form and joinery from the building being replaced and from the tradition's records, not from generalised style rules. *Why:* the value of the system is exact transmission; "improvement" breaks the chain of evidence.

## 10. Decorative carving and ornament: how it attaches

Ornament in Japanese religious building is almost always **either the shaped end of a structural member or a separately made piece mechanically keyed to one**. Glue was not used structurally; nails are used for light attachments only (and are hidden). The recurring design problem is to fix a carved piece so that it cannot fall, does not split the member it attaches to, and can be removed for repair.

### 10.1 Kaerumata (蟇股, "frog-legs")
- **Class:** infill strut / ornament (nakazonae).
- **Typical use:** between columns on the kashiranuki or a tie beam, carrying a to under the purlin; also in gables and on kōhai.
- **Forms:** 板蟇股 (*ita-kaerumata*, a solid plank with splayed outline; early, structural) and 本蟇股 (*hon-kaerumata* or *sukashi-kaerumata*, open with carved infill between the "legs"; from the late Heian period, e.g. Chūson-ji Konjikidō, 1124). In the Momoyama and Edo periods the infill carving becomes elaborate (animals, plants) and polychrome.
- **Load behaviour:** in early and medieval work it genuinely props the purlin; in later work often mainly ornamental.
- **Joinery:** the feet are housed (shallow short tenons or dabo) into the member below; the top carries a to on a dabo. In elaborate pieces the carved centre may be a separate insert fitted within the frame of the legs.
- **Dating:** the outline of the legs and the style of the carving are standard dating evidence.

**R12-061** — **Must.** A kaerumata that is expected to carry load shall be made from one piece for its load-bearing outline (top and both legs), set out on the board so that the grain runs horizontally along the top and as continuously as possible into both legs; check the leg junctions for short grain before carving. *Why:* the load passes from the top block down both legs; short grain at the leg junctions is the typical failure. Where the outline makes short grain unavoidable, the piece should be treated as ornamental and the purlin supported otherwise.

**R12-062** — **Should.** Separately carved inserts in kaerumata and panels shall be fixed by concealed housings, dovetail keys or hidden pins that allow removal, not by nails through the carving. *Why:* carvings are the parts most often removed for conservation; nails split fine carving and rust-stain it.

### 10.2 Kibana (木鼻)
- **Definition:** the carved end of a horizontal member (head tie, nuki, arm) projecting beyond a column; originating in daibutsuyō's moulded nuki ends.
- **Forms:** 繰形 moulded end (daibutsuyō); 拳鼻 (*kobushibana*, fist, Zen style); 象鼻 (*zōbana*, elephant head); 獅子鼻 (*shishibana*, lion head); 貘鼻 (*bakubana*, tapir) and others — the animal forms are Momoyama–Edo.
- **Joinery:** (a) **integral** — carved on the end of the nuki or kashiranuki itself, the best practice for structural ends; (b) **separately made** (sometimes called 付け木鼻 *tsuke-kibana*) — a carved block fixed to the member end by a housed tenon (雇い枘 *yatoi-hozo*) or dovetail into the end grain, pinned (込栓) through the column/member, bearing against the column face.

**R12-063** — **Must.** Where a kibana is carved integrally on a nuki or head tie, the carving shall not reduce the section of the member inside the column or within one member-depth of the column face. *Why:* the nuki's moment resistance is developed at the column face; decorative undercutting there weakens exactly the working section.

**R12-064** — **Must.** A separately made kibana shall be keyed into the member end (yatoi tenon or dovetail) and pinned, and shall bear against the column face; never fix it with nails alone. *Why:* animal-head kibana are heavy and cantilever from the column; nailed ones fall — a documented hazard in old buildings.

### 10.3 Gegyo (懸魚) and hafu ornament
- **Definition:** a board ornament hung under the apex (拝み *ogami*) of the barge boards (hafu) at a gable, originally concealing the ridge-purlin end. **降懸魚** (*kudari-gegyo*) or **桁隠** (*ketakakushi*) are similar pieces covering the purlin ends along the hafu.
- **Forms:** 猪目懸魚 (*inome*, heart-shaped cut-outs), 蕪懸魚 (*kabura*, turnip), 梅鉢懸魚 (*umebachi*), 三花懸魚 (*mitsubana*); wing-like side pieces 鰭 (*hire*); a rosette 六葉 (*rokuyō*) at the centre often covers the fixing pin.
- **Joinery:** hung from the hafu apex by a tenon or pin through the hafu, the head of which is concealed by the rokuyō; hire fitted to the gegyo edge by tongues/splines (雇い実) and to the hafu underside.

**R12-065** — **Should.** The gegyo shall be hung so that it can move independently of the hafu (pinned at the top, with slotted or tongued connections to the hire). *Why:* the hafu and gegyo are thin boards with grain in different directions; rigid fixing splits one or the other.

### 10.4 Kōryō (虹梁) and eyō (絵様)
- **Definition:** "rainbow beam" — a tie beam whose underside and top are gently arched (the *mukuri* of the beam), with ends reduced (袖切 *sodekiri*) to fit into the column or bracket and with a carved **眉** (*mayu*, "eyebrow" line) along the side near the ends.
- **Eyō (絵様)** are the low-relief scroll carvings (渦 *uzu* spirals and 若葉 *wakaba* leaf tendrils) near the beam ends; their drawing changes systematically over time and is one of the principal tools for dating buildings (the Japanese literature on 絵様 dating is extensive).
- **Kurikata (繰形):** the moulded profiles of arm ends, kibana and beam ends; also period-diagnostic.
- **Types:** 虹梁 in daibutsuyō has a round section; wayō and Zen kōryō are rectangular with rounded arrises; 海老虹梁 (§4.4).
- **Joinery:** tenoned into the columns (often through-tenon or long stub tenon with pins), or seated on a bracket (de-mitsudo crossing arm) with its end dressed as the arm's continuation.

**R12-066** — **Must.** The arch (mukuri) of a kōryō shall be cut from the top and bottom of a beam of sufficient depth that the **structural depth at mid-span is not less than the design depth**; the camber is ornament, not an excuse to reduce the section. *Why:* a kōryō spans between columns and often carries a taiheizuka or struts at mid-span.

**R12-067** — **Should.** In restoration, eyō and kurikata shall be recorded by rubbing or full-size tracing before dismantling and reproduced from the traced template, not redrawn freehand. *Why:* the drawing is dating evidence and part of the designated fabric; stylistic drift in re-carving destroys both.

### 10.5 Ranma and carved panels (欄間, 彫刻)
- Religious buildings use carved transom panels (彫刻欄間), Zen-style bow transoms (弓欄間), wave transoms (波欄間), and the lavish carved panels of Momoyama–Edo shrines and mausolea (Nikkō). Domestic ranma are covered in → Ch. 14.
- Panels are held in grooves (溝 *mizo*) in surrounding frames and are **floating**.

**R12-068** — **Must.** Carved panels and ranma shall float in grooves with seasonal clearance, and shall never be glued or nailed across their width to the frame. *Why:* wide carved boards move across the grain; restrained, they crack through the carving.

### 10.6 Tebasami (手挟) and kōhai ornament
- The **tebasami** is a triangular, usually carved board or block that fills the gap between the top of a kōhai bracket set (horizontal) and the sloping rafters above it.
- It is non-structural: it is fitted after the rafters are set, housed lightly into the bracket top and the rafter underside.
- 花肘木 (*hana-hijiki*) at the kōhai may carry some purlin load; it must then be treated as a bracket arm (R12-008 applies).

**R12-069** — **Must.** Tebasami and other infill carvings shall be installed after the rafters and roof have been loaded, and shall not be wedged tight. *Why:* they would otherwise pick up roof load through settlement and split.

### 10.7 General rules for ornament

**R12-070** — **Must.** Every ornament shall have a stated method of removal for repair (which pins to draw, in what order); record this on the drawings. *Why:* dismantling repair (→ Ch. 16) repeatedly destroys ornament that was fixed with no thought for removal.

**R12-071** — **Should.** Hardware on ornament (飾金具 *kazari-kanagu*, nail covers 釘隠 *kugikakushi*, rokuyō) shall be fixed with non-ferrous or protected fasteners and shall not trap water against end grain. *Why:* rust staining and end-grain rot under metal fittings are among the most common defects found in shrine repairs.

**R12-072** — **Should.** Surface finishes (丹塗 *ni-nuri* red lead/cinnabar paint, 極彩色 polychromy, lacquer) are applied only after carpentry and kigoroshi are complete and the timber has reached near-equilibrium moisture content. *Why:* paint on moving, drying timber cracks along joints and at end grain; repainting cycles are part of the maintenance plan (→ Ch. 16).
