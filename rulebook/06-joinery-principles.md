# Chapter 06 — General Principles of Joinery (継手・仕口の原則 / *tsugite・shiguchi no gensoku*)

**Scope.** This chapter sets out the principles that govern every joint in a Japanese timber frame, independent of the particular joint chosen. It defines the vocabulary (tsugite, shiguchi, kumite, hagi, tome, sashimono), classifies joints by geometry and by function, sketches the historical development of Japanese joinery, explains the physical behaviour of wood that joints exploit or must respect (anisotropy, embedment, shear, shrinkage, friction, wedge action), and then states the rules for assigning male and female roles, placing joints in the frame, sequencing assembly, achieving the correct fit, limiting loss of cross-section, and treating visible versus hidden faces. It ends with a decision table for choosing a joint. The individual joints are catalogued in → Ch. 07 (splices) and → Ch. 08 (connections); pins, wedges and keys in → Ch. 09; marking conventions in → Ch. 02; timber orientation in → Ch. 03; frame-level load paths in → Ch. 10; assembly order on site in → Ch. 15; and code-based structural verification in → Ch. 17.

## 1. Vocabulary and definitions

### 1.1 The two great families

- **Splice joint** (継手, *tsugite*) — a joint that unites two members **end to end along a common axis**, so that together they act as one longer member. Typical: a sill (土台, *dodai*) made of two lengths, a wall plate (桁, *keta*) spanning several bays, a ridge (棟木, *munagi*), a purlin (母屋, *moya*), a post base replaced by a new piece (根継ぎ, *netsugi*). Splices are needed because timber lengths are limited (standard logs in Japan are commonly 3 m, 4 m or 6 m; historically 2 ken, 3 ken lengths), because long straight timber is costly, and because repair requires replacing only the decayed part.
- **Connection joint** (仕口, *shiguchi*) — a joint that unites members meeting **at an angle** (usually 90°, but also the oblique angles of hips, braces and bracket arms). Typical: beam-to-post, girder-to-beam, sill-to-sill at a corner, rafter-to-plate. Shiguchi include tenons (枘, *hozo*), housings (大入れ, *ōire*), laps and notches (渡り腮, *watari-ago*; 相欠き, *aikaki*), dovetail housings (蟻掛け, *arikake*), and multi-member corner assemblies. They are catalogued in → Ch. 08.

The distinction is by **axis**, not by shape: a dovetail used to extend a sill is a tsugite (蟻継ぎ, *ari-tsugi*), while the same dovetail used to hang a joist on a beam is a shiguchi (蟻掛け, *arikake*). Many joint "shapes" therefore appear in both chapters under different names.

### 1.2 Related families

- **Crossing joint** (組手, *kumite*) — two members crossing each other and interlocking, most often each halved (相欠き組手) so that their faces are flush, e.g. crossing sills, crossing tie-beams, lattice (組子, *kumiko*) in shōji. Kumite is sometimes treated as a subset of shiguchi (members at an angle) but differs in that both members *continue through* the intersection. → Ch. 08, → Ch. 14 (kumiko).
- **Edge joint** (矧ぎ, *hagi*) — joining boards **edge to edge** to make a wider panel: plain butted (芋矧ぎ, *imo-hagi*), tongue-and-groove (本実矧ぎ, *hon-zane-hagi*), loose tongue (雇い実矧ぎ, *yatoi-zane-hagi*), rebated (相決り, *aijakuri*), splined, or keyed with butterfly keys (千切り, *chigiri*) and dowels (太枘, *dabo*). → Ch. 14 for floors, ceilings and doors; → Ch. 07 §6 for chigiri.
- **Mitre** (留め, *tome*) — a joint in which the meeting line bisects the angle so that neither end grain is seen; used for finish members (長押 *nageshi*, 回り縁 *mawaribuchi*, 框 *kamachi*, 額縁 frames). A mitre is almost always combined with a hidden structural element (tenon, spline, dovetail, 車知 *shachi* key) because the mitre surface itself is end grain meeting end grain at an angle and has little strength. → Ch. 08, → Ch. 14.
- **Sashimono** — the word has two meanings that must not be confused:
  - 差物 (*sashimono*) in framing: a deep horizontal member **inserted** (差す, *sasu*) between posts with long tenons and pins, as in the large 差鴨居 (*sashigamoi*) and 差物 of minka and machiya, which provide moment resistance to the frame (→ Ch. 10, → Ch. 13).
  - 指物 (*sashimono*) in joinery/cabinetmaking: furniture and fittings made by fine joinery without nails (→ Ch. 14 touches on its techniques in tategu).

**R06-001** — **Must.** Every joint drawing, marking and discussion uses the same terms for the same parts: the member with the projecting part is the male (*ogi*), the one with the recess is the female (*megi*). *Why:* in the workshop the words "ogi/megi" and "uwaki/shitaki" are used to allocate cutting to different carpenters; ambiguity leads to both ends of a splice being cut male.

## 2. Classification of joints

### 2.1 By geometry

The same small number of geometric ideas recurs in hundreds of named joints. Knowing the family tells you what a joint can and cannot do.

| Geometric family | Principle | Splice examples (→ Ch. 07) | Connection examples (→ Ch. 08) | Resists pull-out by |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Butt** (突付け, *tsukitsuke*) | square ends meet | 突付け継ぎ (rafters on a plate, boards) | 突付け of studs; butt with nails/straps | nothing inherent — needs nails, straps, splines |
| **Lap / halving** (相欠き, *aikaki*) | each member cut to half depth and overlapped | 相欠き継ぎ, 布継ぎ | 相欠き組手, 渡り腮 | pins or nails only (plain); hook if stepped |
| **Scarf** (殺ぎ, *sogi*) | long sloping cut, end grain avoided | 殺ぎ継ぎ, 追掛け大栓継ぎ (stepped scarf), 金輪継ぎ | — | pins, steps, keys |
| **Seat / support** (台持ち, 腰掛け) | one member bears on a ledge of the other or on a support below | 台持ち継ぎ, 腰掛け蟻/鎌 | 腰掛け of beams, 大入れ | combined with a hook shape |
| **Tenon** (枘, *hozo*) | projecting tongue into a mortise | 竿継ぎ, 雇い竿 | 短枘, 長枘, 重枘, 小根枘, 扇枘 … | pins, wedges, shachi |
| **Dovetail** (蟻, *ari*) | head wider than neck; flared sides | 蟻継ぎ, 腰掛け蟻継ぎ, 隠し蟻 | 蟻掛け, 蟻落とし, 寄せ蟻 | geometry (limited) |
| **Gooseneck / kama** (鎌, *kama*) | long neck with a head that has barbed shoulders | 鎌継ぎ, 腰掛け鎌継ぎ, 目違い鎌 | 鎌掛け, 鎌枘 | geometry (better than ari) |
| **Key / pin** (栓・車知・千切り) | a separate hardwood piece locks two members | 竿車知, 金輪 (sen), 追掛け (daisen), 千切り | 込み栓, 鼻栓, 車知栓 | the key in shear/bearing |
| **Rebate / tongue-and-groove** (決り, 実) | edge or end with a tongue or step | 目違い継ぎ, 箱目違い, 雇い実 | 相決り | alignment only |
| **Housing** (大入れ, *ōire*) | whole section let into a shallow recess | — | 大入れ, 大入れ蟻掛け | none inherent; bearing & alignment |
| **Mitre** (留め, *tome*) | angle bisected, end grain hidden | 宮島継ぎ (partly), 留め継ぎ in finish | 留め, 襟輪留め, 三方留め | hidden tenon/key |

### 2.2 By function (what force the joint must carry)

Every joint must be chosen for the forces it will actually see. Think in terms of the five basic actions.

| Action | What it means in a splice | What it means in a connection | Joints that are good at it | Joints that are poor at it |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Compression** (along axis) | members pushed end to end (posts, struts, netsugi) | a member bearing on another (post on sill) | butt, mechigai, hako-mechigai, kanawa, daimochi | — (almost any joint carries end compression if faces bear fully) |
| **Tension** (along axis) | members pulled apart (sills and plates in racking, tie-beams, nuki) | a beam pulling out of a post | okkake-daisen, kanawa, sao-shachi, kama (moderate), ari (low) | butt, plain lap, sogi without pins |
| **Bending** (moment) | splice in a span | beam-to-post moment (rigid-ish connections) | okkake-daisen, kanawa, shiribasami, daimochi on a support; sashimono with long tenons & pins | koshikake-ari/kama (act as hinges), butt |
| **Shear** (transverse) | vertical load passing across the splice | beam end reaction into post | seats (koshikake), daimochi, watari-ago, housings | plain tenons without seat, butt |
| **Torsion / out-of-plane** | members twisting or being pushed sideways | twisting of beams under eccentric loads | hako-mechigai, kanawa (with mechigai), kumite, housings | plain ari, plain lap |

**R06-002** — **Must.** Identify the dominant action (compression, tension, bending, shear, torsion) at a joint before choosing its type, and choose from a family that is good at that action. *Why:* joints fail not because they are "weak joints" in general but because they are asked to do what their geometry cannot (e.g. a koshikake-kama asked to carry mid-span bending).

**R06-003** — **Must.** A joint that has no inherent resistance to a force (e.g. a butt joint in tension, a housing in pull-out) must receive that resistance from a pin, key, wedge, strap or from the arrangement of other members — or the force must be designed out. *Why:* friction alone is unreliable once the wood has dried and shrunk.

### 2.3 By direction of assembly

A third, very practical classification is **how the two pieces come together**. It determines when in the sequence the joint can be made (§7).

| Assembly movement | Japanese term | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Dropped from above (vertical insertion) | 落とし込み *otoshikomi* | koshikake-ari, koshikake-kama, ari-kake, watari-ago |
| Slid in from the side (perpendicular to axis) | 横入れ / 横から差す | kanawa, shiribasami, hako-mechigai-kama (some forms) |
| Slid along the axis | 追掛け *okkake*, 差し込み | okkake-daisen (final slide), sao-tsugi, tenons |
| Inserted diagonally | 斜め入れ | shihō-ari, shihō-kama netsugi (→ Ch. 07 §6) |
| Assembled then locked by a separate key | 栓・車知・楔締め | sao-shachi, kanawa (sen), nuki wedging |

## 3. Historical development

The history of Japanese joinery is a history of tools and of the demands placed on frames. Dates below are approximate and some attributions remain matters of scholarly discussion; → Ch. 01 for sources.

### 3.1 Prehistory: lashing, notching, early mortises (Jōmon, Yayoi)

- The earliest buildings (pit dwellings, 竪穴住居 *tateana-jūkyo*) relied mainly on **earth-fast posts** (掘立柱, *hottate-bashira*) with forked tops and **lashed** members; the embedment of posts in the ground supplied lateral stability.
- The **Sakuramachi site** (桜町遺跡, Oyabe, Toyama Prefecture) yielded building timbers dated to roughly 4,000 years ago (Middle–Late Jōmon), with worked **mortise-like holes** and **holes for horizontal pieces through posts** (貫穴 *nuki-ana* in the excavators' terminology) and notched laps; these are widely cited as evidence that raised-floor structures and deliberate joinery existed already in the Jōmon period. The exact interpretation of individual pieces is still discussed.
- Yayoi-period sites such as **Toro** (登呂遺跡, Shizuoka) and **Yamaki** (山木遺跡, Izunokuni, Shizuoka) preserved many wooden building elements (posts, boards, ladders, parts of raised storehouses, 高床倉庫 *takayuka-sōko*) with mortises, through-holes and notches. They show that tenon-and-mortise and through-member principles were known before the arrival of continental temple building. The rat-guards (鼠返し, *nezumi-gaeshi*) of raised storehouses are a characteristic element.
- With stone and later iron tools (axes, adzes, chisels), and with timber obtained by **splitting** (打ち割り製材, *uchiwari seizai*) rather than sawing, joints were necessarily simple: notches, laps, through-holes, pegs.

### 3.2 Asuka–Nara: continental temple carpentry

- With Buddhism (6th century) carpenters from the Korean peninsula introduced the Chinese-derived system of stone foundations (礎石, *soseki*), posts standing on stones, bracket sets and heavy tiled roofs. **Hōryū-ji** (rebuilt in the late 7th–early 8th century) is the classic reference.
- Joinery of this period is comparatively **limited in repertoire but massive in scale**: tenons, housings, laps, scarf-type and seat-type splices (台持ち *daimochi*, 殺ぎ *sogi*), and — reported in several Nara-period buildings — kama-type hooked splices. Lateral stability depended heavily on the **weight of the roof**, the **bracket assemblies** and the **nailed tie-rails** (長押, *nageshi*) wrapped around the posts, rather than on penetrating tie-beams.
- Iron nails (和釘, *wakugi*) and cramps (鎹, *kasugai*) were used extensively for secondary members.
- Timber was still split and dressed with the **adze** (釿, *chōna*) and **spear plane** (槍鉋, *yariganna*). There was no rip saw; crosscut saws were small. This favours joints that can be chopped with chisels and dressed with adze and yariganna.

### 3.3 Heian: domestication

- The Japanese "native" style (和様, *wayō*) developed; the hidden roof (野屋根, *noyane*) with cantilever beams (桔木, *hanegi*) appears in the later Heian period (→ Ch. 11). Joinery grew more refined but its basic toolkit did not change radically.

### 3.4 Kamakura: the nuki revolution

- In the reconstruction of **Tōdai-ji** after 1180, the priest **Chōgen** (重源) introduced the **Great Buddha style** (大仏様, *daibutsuyō*) from Song China. Its defining structural feature is the **penetrating tie-beam** (貫, *nuki*): horizontal members passing *through* mortises in the posts and locked with wedges, creating a frame whose stability comes from the embedment of nuki in the posts rather than from nailed nageshi. Surviving examples: the **Nandaimon of Tōdai-ji** (c. 1199–1203) and the **Jōdo-dō of Jōdo-ji**, Ono (c. 1192–1194).
- The **Zen style** (禅宗様, *zenshūyō*) arriving slightly later also used nuki extensively; the eclectic style (折衷様, *setchūyō*) absorbed nuki into wayō. From the medieval period on, **nuki became the normal way** of tying posts together in Japan (→ Ch. 10, → Ch. 12).
- Nuki demanded **splices inside post mortises** and **tension-capable hooked splices**. One widely repeated account traces the **略鎌 (*ryaku-kama*, "abbreviated kama")** — a half-lap with a small hook — to around 1200, developing into the **okkake-daisen splice** (追掛け大栓継ぎ) by roughly the 14th–15th centuries. Treat these dates as approximate.

### 3.5 Muromachi: the saw changes everything

- The two-man frame rip saw (大鋸, *ōga*) is generally said to have arrived from China in the **14th–15th century**. Before it, Japan had no true rip saw and boards were split with wedges. The ōga (and its successor, the one-man pit-saw 前挽大鋸 *maebiki-ōga*, which remained the main sawing tool until machine sawmills of the mid-Meiji period) made **sawn square timber and thin boards** economical, allowed **less straight-grained species** to be used, and — crucially for joinery — produced **flat, true reference surfaces**.
- The **bench plane** (台鉋, *daiganna*) came into general use from roughly the late Muromachi/Momoyama period, allowing precise finishing.
- These tools allowed **fine, tight-fitting, complex joints** — seated splices (腰掛け *koshikake* forms are reported to become common from the later Muromachi period), refined kama and ari, stepped scarfs — and enabled the thin members and precise surfaces of **shoin** and later **sukiya** architecture.

### 3.6 Momoyama and Edo: codification and elaboration

- Carpentry proportion manuals (木割書, *kiwarisho*) such as the **Shōmei** (匠明, dated 1608, Heinouchi family) codified member proportions (→ Ch. 01, Ch. 02, Ch. 12). Kikujutsu (規矩術) texts codified geometry (→ Ch. 05).
- The urban townhouse (町家, *machiya*) and large farmhouses (民家, *minka*) developed long-span beams (梁, *hari*), sashimono and complex multi-member corners. Joint names multiplied; many elaborate forms (hidden kama, four-way dovetails, decorative mitred splices) belong to this period or later, and some show-pieces are more demonstrations of skill (腕自慢) than common structural practice.
- Knowledge was transmitted within families and guilds; the same joint may carry different names regionally, and the same name may denote different shapes (see note R06-004).

### 3.7 Meiji to the present: hardware, codes, precut

- Western trusses, bolts and straps entered with Meiji-period (from 1868) construction; after the **Nōbi earthquake (1891)** official recommendations increasingly favoured metal reinforcement and diagonal bracing.
- The **Building Standard Law** (建築基準法, 1950) and its later revisions (notably 1981, and the 2000 notifications specifying joint hardware) moved mainstream housing toward braced frames with metal fittings (→ Ch. 17).
- From the late 1970s–1980s **precut** (プレカット) CAD/CAM machining spread; by the 2010s the great majority (commonly quoted as over 90 %) of new post-and-beam houses were machine-cut. Precut standardized a small set of joints (koshikake-ari, koshikake-kama, ōire-arikake, short tenons) plus hardware; modern machines can also cut okkake-daisen and kanawa.
- In parallel, research since the 1990s on **embedment (めり込み)** and on the seismic behaviour of traditional frames has given engineering support to nuki, wedged and pinned joints and to large-deformation design (→ Ch. 17).

**R06-004** — **Should.** When a joint is named in drawings or specification, add a sketch or reference to this rulebook's entry. *Why:* joint names vary by region, school and period (e.g. what one workshop calls *shiribasami* another may call a kind of *kanawa*); a sketch removes ambiguity.

## 4. Physical principles

### 4.1 Wood is anisotropic

Wood is a bundle of long, hollow cells oriented along the stem. Its properties differ in the three principal directions:

- **L** — longitudinal (along the grain)
- **R** — radial (from pith toward bark)
- **T** — tangential (along the growth rings)

Representative ratios for clear softwood of the kinds used in Japanese framing (hinoki, sugi, matsu, hiba). Values vary by species, density and moisture; use them to understand behaviour, not for design.

| Property | Along grain (L) | Across grain (R/T) | Ratio L : ⊥ (approx.) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Compression strength | high | low (crushes, then densifies) | ≈ 5–10 : 1 |
| Tension strength | very high | very low (splits) | ≈ 10–30 : 1 |
| Stiffness (E) | high | low | ≈ 15–30 : 1 |
| Shear strength *parallel to grain* | — | ≈ 1/8–1/15 of compression parallel | — |
| Shrinkage, green → oven-dry | ≈ 0.1–0.3 % | R ≈ 2–5 %, T ≈ 5–10 % | L is negligible; T ≈ 1.5–2.5 × R |

Consequences for joinery:

1. **Load end grain in compression, long grain in tension.** A post standing on a sill or a netsugi carries huge loads end-to-end. A tenon pulls well along its grain until the wood beyond its pin shears out.
2. **Avoid tension across the grain.** Anything that pries fibres apart (a wedge driven too close to an end, a dovetail pulling on narrow jaws, a pin too near the end) splits the member.
3. **Shear along the grain is the weakest mode.** It governs the length of wood needed beyond mortises, pins, keys and the heads of dovetails/kama.

For reference, the characteristic ("base") strengths commonly quoted from the Japanese MLIT notification for ungraded structural softwood (2000, No. 1452) give an order of magnitude — for example for sugi (杉) compression ≈ 17.7, bending ≈ 22.2, tension ≈ 13.5, shear ≈ 1.8 N/mm², and embedment (めり込み) base strength ≈ 6.0 N/mm²; for hinoki (檜) shear ≈ 2.1 and embedment ≈ 7.8 N/mm². These are regulatory characteristic values, not mean test strengths; always check the current notification (→ Ch. 17).

### 4.2 Compression perpendicular to grain — embedment (めり込み, *merikomi*)

When a member is pressed across its grain — a beam resting on a post, a nuki pushing on the side of its mortise, a wedge driven into a slot, a tenon rotating in its mortise — the fibres are crushed locally. This is **embedment** (めり込み).

Key features:

- **It is ductile.** After the proportional limit the wood does not break; it continues to deform while the load keeps rising slowly as the crushed cells densify. This contrasts sharply with the brittle failure of wood in tension or shear.
- **Partial loading is stronger than full loading.** When the loaded area is in the middle of a longer member (with unloaded wood continuing beyond on either side along the grain — the 余長 effect), the fibres beyond the loaded zone help carry the load (like a rope pulled down in the middle), so the apparent embedment stiffness and strength increase. Japanese embedment theory (developed notably by Inayama Masahiro, c. 1990s) quantifies this; it is the basis of modern calculation of nuki and wedge joints.
- **It is partly irreversible.** Crushed wood does not fully recover; joints that have been loaded into embedment become loose unless re-wedged. (Contrast with kigoroshi, §8.1, where the pre-compression is small and the fibres are allowed to recover by moisture.)
- **It dissipates energy.** Repeated embedment in cyclic (earthquake) loading converts kinetic energy into heat and permanent deformation — the key to the ductility of traditional frames (§4.6).

**R06-005** — **Must.** Where one member bears across the grain of another (beam on post top, rafter on plate, nuki on mortise walls), the bearing area must be sized so that the embedment stress under long-term load stays well below the embedment strength of the softer species. *Why:* excessive long-term embedment causes progressive settlement ("sinking" of beams into post tops, tilting of posts) and loosens joints.

**R06-006** — **Should.** Where possible, arrange bearing so that the loaded area lies **away from the end** of the member being crushed (i.e. with an unloaded length of member beyond it, the *yochō*). *Why:* embedment near an end grain is both weaker and stiffer-softer (fibres can splay out at the end).

### 4.3 Shear along the grain and end distance (余長, *yochō*)

Whenever force is transferred into a member through a surface that is **across** the grain — the back of a mortise, the face of a pin, the bearing face of a dovetail head, the jaw of an okkake splice — the wood beyond that surface resists by **shear along the grain** on planes running from the loaded surface to the end of the member. If this length is too short the block **shears out** (a clean failure along the grain, often sudden).

Practical end-distance rules (representative; confirm by calculation for primary members, → Ch. 17):

| Situation | Minimum end distance (along grain) | Remarks |
|---|---|---|
| Pin (込み栓 *komisen*) through a tenon, measured from pin centre to end of tenon | ≈ 2.5–3 × pin size, and ≥ ≈ 30 mm | short tenons with pins too near the end shear out; → Ch. 08/09 |
| Pin through a mortised member, pin centre to end of that member | ≈ 4–7 × pin size | e.g. sill end beyond a corner post |
| Wood beyond a mortise at a member end (e.g. sill or plate projecting past a corner post, the *hana* or *mochidashi*) | ≈ 1 × member depth, preferably 1.5 ×; traditional minimum often 3–5 sun (90–150 mm) | "never cut a mortise right at the end" |
| Head of a dovetail/kama: head length beyond the neck | ≈ 1.0–1.5 × neck width | short heads shear off along the neck lines |
| Female jaws (口脇, *kuchiwaki*) either side of a dovetail/kama socket | each ≥ ≈ 1/4–1/3 of member width | narrow jaws split under the wedging action of the head |
| Stepped scarf (okkake, kanawa): length of each lap from step to tip | ≥ ≈ 1 × depth | this is why these splices are ≈ 3 × depth long |
| Key (sen, shachi) in a splice: length of wood beyond the key on each side | ≥ ≈ 6–8 × key thickness | the key bears across grain; the block beyond resists in shear |

**R06-007** — **Must.** Never place a mortise, pin, key or dovetail/kama socket so close to the end of a member that the block beyond it can shear out along the grain; observe at least the end distances in the table above, and increase them for low-density species (sugi) and for primary members. *Why:* shear along the grain is the weakest property of wood, and shear-out failures are sudden.

**R06-008** — **Must.** Keep pins and keys out of **checks (割れ)** and out of the pith zone where drying checks will form; position the hole so that the expected check line (usually running radially from the pith to the face at the back-cut, 背割り *sewari*, → Ch. 03) does not pass through it. *Why:* a check through a pin hole cuts the shear planes that the pin relies on.

### 4.4 Shrinkage, swelling and how joints change with drying

Below the fibre saturation point (≈ 25–30 % moisture content), wood shrinks as it dries and swells as it wets:

- **Longitudinal** shrinkage is negligible (≈ 0.1–0.3 % from green to oven-dry).
- **Radial** shrinkage ≈ 2–5 %.
- **Tangential** shrinkage ≈ 5–10 %, i.e. roughly **twice** the radial.

Hence: a square timber sawn with the pith inside (心持ち材, *shinmochi-zai*) develops surface checks and becomes slightly lozenge-shaped or crowned; a flat-sawn board cups away from the heart (it becomes concave on the bark side, *kiomote*); a quarter-sawn board (柾目, *masame*) stays flatter (→ Ch. 03).

**How joints change as they dry — the key principle.** In any joint, look at each **direction** across the joint and ask whether each piece has **long grain** or **cross grain** in that direction:

- If both pieces have **cross grain** in that direction (e.g. the width of two sills spliced end to end), both shrink similarly and the fit is roughly **preserved**.
- If one piece has **long grain** and the other **cross grain** in that direction (e.g. a beam tenon in a post: the tenon's *height* is cross grain but the mortise's *height* runs along the post's grain), the cross-grain piece shrinks and the long-grain piece does not → **a gap opens** in that direction.
- A tenon's **thickness** shrinks while the mortise's **width** (across the post's grain) also shrinks → these stay roughly matched, sometimes tightening.

Practical consequences:

- **Splices (tsugite)** between two members of similar species and moisture content are **relatively stable**, because both halves have the same grain direction. Differences arise mainly when one half is quarter-sawn and the other flat-sawn, or when one is much wetter.
- **Connections (shiguchi)** at right angles **loosen in the direction where grain directions differ**, typically the height of a tenon in a post mortise, and the depth of a beam in a housing. Wedges (楔) and draw-pins (込み栓) are placed to act in the direction that can be re-tightened, and **nuki wedges are re-driven** (増し締め *mashijime*) after the frame has dried (commonly after one or two seasons).
- **Tapered joints tighten under load but loosen with drying**: a kama or dovetail drawn tight across the grain relaxes as the head shrinks across its width; this is one reason koshikake-kama and koshikake-ari are placed over supports, where loosening does not cause sagging.
- **Pins** should be of drier, denser wood than the frame (e.g. oak, 樫 *kashi*), so that they do not shrink loose (→ Ch. 09).

**R06-009** — **Must.** For every joint, check the direction(s) in which the two pieces have different grain orientation and design the joint so that shrinkage in that direction either does not matter, is taken up by a wedge or key that can be re-tightened, or is hidden. *Why:* 5–10 % tangential shrinkage on a 120 mm member is up to 6–12 mm — far larger than any cutting tolerance.

**R06-010** — **Should.** Fabricate joints with timber at a moisture content close to the equilibrium it will reach in service (in Japan typically ≈ 12–18 %, depending on exposure), or, when cutting partly-seasoned timber in the traditional way, allow for the expected shrinkage by placing re-tightenable wedges/keys and by planning a re-wedging visit. *Why:* joints cut wet in fixed-geometry forms (plain ari, housings) will open; joints with wedges can be re-tightened.

**R06-011** — **Should.** Match the grain figure of the two halves of a splice (both flat-sawn with the same face up, or both quarter-sawn) and match their *kiomote/kiura* orientation where the member is visible. *Why:* different shrinkage and cupping of the two halves produces a visible step (目違い) at the joint.

### 4.5 Friction, wedging and draw

Traditional joints are held by three mechanisms besides pure geometry:

1. **Friction** between tightly fitting faces. Useful, but it decreases as the wood dries and as it is compressed; never rely on friction alone for a primary force (R06-003).
2. **Wedge action.** A slight taper — the **sliding slope** (滑り勾配, *suberi-kōbai*) of a kama or ari, the slope of okkake laps, or a separate wedge — converts a small driving force into a large clamping force. A taper of about 1:10 multiplies the driving force roughly tenfold (less friction losses). This is how okkake splices draw tight (the pieces "chase" one another along the slope), and how kama heads draw the two members together as they are driven down.
3. **Draw** (引き寄せ, *hikiyose*) by offset holes or tapered keys. A draw-pin (込み栓) through slightly offset holes in tenon and mortise, or a tapered shachi key through offset slots, pulls the shoulder (胴付き *dōtsuki*) tight as it is driven (→ Ch. 09). The offset is small — typically ≈ 1–2 mm in structural members (varying with species and size).

**R06-012** — **Must.** Wedge tapers, sliding slopes and draw offsets must be moderate: too steep and the joint will not draw (and wedges spring out); too shallow and the driving force splits the female. Representative ranges: wedges ≈ 1:8–1:15; sliding slopes on kama/ari ≈ 1:25–1:40 in depth; okkake laps ≈ 1:10; draw offsets ≈ 1–2 mm (softwood) and less in hardwood. *Why:* the mechanical advantage of a taper cuts both ways — the same multiplication that clamps the joint also splits it.

**R06-013** — **Must.** Drive wedges and tapered keys so that the splitting force they create acts **along** the grain of the member being wedged, or is contained by enough end distance/side wood; never drive a wedge that spreads fibres across the grain near an end. *Why:* wood's tensile strength across the grain is very low.

### 4.6 Why Japanese frames are ductile

A traditional frame of posts, nuki, sashimono and heavy roof does not rely on diagonal bracing or rigid connections. Under earthquake loading:

- Posts rotate; the **nuki and sashimono tenons rotate in their mortises**, crushing the mortise walls and the tenons locally (embedment). This produces a restoring moment that increases slowly with rotation.
- **Wedges** keep the nuki pre-compressed; they add stiffness at small drifts and re-engage after unloading.
- **Pinned splices** (okkake, kanawa) keep horizontal members continuous so that the frame acts as a whole.
- The **heavy roof** and the **posts' rocking on foundation stones** (in 石場建て *ishiba-date*) further contribute, with some sliding and uplift acting as base isolation (→ Ch. 10, Ch. 17).

The resulting hysteresis is of a slip (pinched) type with large deformation capacity: laboratory tests of nuki frames commonly show resistance maintained to very large storey drifts (of the order of 1/30–1/15 rad or more) without collapse, whereas braced frames reach higher stiffness but fail more abruptly. The frame's ductility depends absolutely on joints **failing in embedment rather than in shear or splitting**.

**R06-014** — **Must.** Proportion joints in lateral-resisting frames (nuki, sashimono, their splices) so that the **first failure mode is embedment** (ductile), not shear-out, splitting or neck fracture (brittle). In practice: generous end distances, sufficient jaw widths, pins not too close to ends, and tenon cross-sections that are not reduced to a narrow neck. *Why:* the frame's energy dissipation and collapse prevention depend on ductile joint behaviour.

**R06-015** — **Should.** Do not stiffen part of a traditional nuki frame with rigid metal fittings without considering the whole frame. *Why:* mixing very stiff and very ductile elements concentrates forces in the stiff elements and their connections, which then fail first; → Ch. 17.

## 5. Male and female (男木 *ogi* ・ 女木 *megi*)

### 5.1 Definitions

- **Male** (男木, *ogi*): the member whose end carries the projecting part — the tenon, the tongue, the dovetail or kama head, the sao.
- **Female** (女木, *megi*): the member that receives — with the mortise, socket, slot, and usually the **seat** (腰掛け, *koshikake*).

In seated splices the male is also the **upper piece** (上木, *uwaki*) and the female the **lower piece** (下木, *shitaki*); the male is lowered onto the female's seat. In symmetrical scarf splices (okkake-daisen, kanawa) the two halves are nearly identical in shape, but one is still designated male (the piece placed second, whose part lies on top or which is slid in) and the other female.

### 5.2 Structural logic

The fundamental logic: **the female receives; the male hangs on it.** In a seated splice (koshikake-ari, koshikake-kama, koshikake-sao-shachi) the female member passes over the support (post, sill-top, crossing beam) and projects a short distance beyond it as a cantilever (持ち出し, *mochidashi*); the male member rests its end on the female's seat. Structurally this is a **Gerber (cantilever-and-suspended-span) system**: the female is continuous over the support and carries the male's end reaction as a short cantilever; the splice itself is a hinge that transfers shear and a limited tension, but no significant moment.

```
          male / 上木 (ogi)                female / 下木 (megi)
  ==============================+ +==================================
                                | |<- seat (koshikake) carries shear
  ==============================+-+====================+=============
                                <---- mochidashi ----> |
                                  (≈ 150 mm, see §6)   | post
                                                       |
```

**R06-016** — **Must.** In a seated splice the **female (lower piece) is the member that is continuous over the support** and the **male (upper piece) rests on the female's seat**; the male is never the one continuous over the post. *Why:* the seat must be supported from below by the female's cantilever; if reversed, the male's tongue would be hanging from nothing but the joint's hook.

**R06-017** — **Must.** The female is placed first; the male is dropped or slid in afterwards. *Why:* the recess must exist before the projecting part can enter; and the female, being the continuous member over the support, is the one that must be fixed (pinned to the post, bolted to the foundation) first.

**R06-018** — **Should.** Where one member of a pair is heavier, larger, or of better quality, make it the **female**. *Why:* the female carries the cantilever and the male's reaction and loses cross-section to the socket, whereas the male's neck is the usual tension-critical section; the heavier piece better tolerates both.

**R06-019** — **Should (modern practice).** In sills (dodai) anchored to a concrete foundation, place the **anchor bolt near the end of the male (upper piece)**, so that tightening the bolt clamps the male down onto the female's seat and thus holds both; do not rely on a bolt in the female alone. Posts must not stand on the joint itself. *Why:* this is the mainstream Japanese specification logic ("押さえ勝手" — the upper piece holds the lower down); a bolt only in the female leaves the male free to lift. Typical positioning is within about 150–200 mm of the joint on the male side (check current specification, → Ch. 10, Ch. 17).

### 5.3 Direction customs: moto/sue and erection sequence

Beyond structural logic, customs assign which end of a member is male and which female, and in which direction splices "point" along the building. These vary; the following are representative.

- **Moto (butt) as female.** A widely reported custom assigns the **butt end (元口, *motokuchi*)** of a member to be the **female** and the **top end (末口, *suekuchi*)** to be the **male**. Rationale: the butt end is thicker (in round or slightly tapered timber), denser and stronger, and is therefore suited to carry the cantilever and the socket. In a run of members all laid in the same moto→sue direction, each joint then consists of the sue-end (male) of one member resting on the moto-end (female) of the next — the members "chase" one another along the run.
- **Erection direction.** Because the female must be placed first (R06-017), the direction of the splices follows the **order of erection** (建て方, *tatekata*): if the plates are erected from the east bay toward the west, each female faces west to receive the next male. Thus workshop marking (墨付け) must be coordinated with the erection plan decided by the master carpenter (棟梁, *tōryō*) (→ Ch. 15).
- **Orientation relative to the building.** Some workshops and regions orient moto ends consistently toward a particular side (e.g. toward the east or south, or toward the "upper"/front side of the house), especially for exposed members such as the ridge (棟木), plates and exposed beams. These are **variants** of local custom and not universal; → Ch. 03 for moto/sue customs and the taboo on inverted posts (逆柱, *sakabashira*).

**R06-020** — **Should.** Decide, before marking begins, the direction of all splices in each line of horizontal members, consistent with (a) the planned erection sequence and (b) the workshop's moto/sue custom; record it on the plan (板図, *itazu*, → Ch. 02). *Why:* a splice whose female faces the wrong way cannot be assembled in sequence and must be recut or forced.

**R06-021** — **May / Variant.** Where the moto/sue custom and the erection sequence conflict, the erection sequence governs for concealed members; for exposed ceremonially important members (ridge, tokobashira-adjacent members) follow the workshop's custom and adapt the erection order. *Why:* structural and sequencing necessity outranks custom, but visible symbolic members are traditionally treated with care.

### 5.4 Which way up (seated splices)

In the ordinary seated splice the male is on top and is lowered onto the female's seat. Exceptions:

- In **sills** (dodai), the koshikake seat is on the female's lower part and the male's end overhangs it from above (the male is 上木). The joint thus resists uplift of the male only through the anchor bolt, and resists uplift of the female through the male bearing down on it.
- In **joints loaded upward** (e.g. uplift of a tie-beam in a wind-exposed roof, or a hanegi-tail), the seat logic reverses; such joints should be pinned or keyed rather than seated.

**R06-022** — **Must.** Orient the seat of a seated splice so that the **dominant vertical load presses the male onto the seat**. Where load reversal (uplift) is possible, add a pin, bolt, strap or choose a keyed/scarf splice. *Why:* a seat works only in one direction.

## 6. Placement rules

### 6.1 Splices close to supports

The traditional and modern Japanese rule: **splices in horizontal members are placed near a support, on the cantilever side, not over the support itself and not at mid-span.**

- The standard offset is **about 150 mm (≈ 5 sun) from the post centre** for ordinary house members (dodai, dōsashi, keta, moya); in practice ≈ 100–250 mm, or roughly **1 to 1.5 × the member depth**, depending on member size and joint type.
- The splice is not placed **directly over** the support, because (a) the post mortise, the splice socket and pins would all weaken the same section; (b) in a member continuous over a post the bending moment is largest (hogging) at the support; (c) the post tenon would have nothing solid to enter.
- It is not placed **at mid-span**, because bending moment there (sagging) is largest and most splices are poor in bending.
- The small offset places the splice where shear is high but moment is low **if** the female is short (Gerber principle, §5.2). Note that the cantilever carries the male's reaction: the longer the offset, the larger the moment in the female at the post; do not over-extend.

**R06-023** — **Must.** Place seated splices (koshikake-ari, koshikake-kama, koshikake-sao-shachi) close to a support — normally ≈ 150 mm (≈ 5 sun) from the support centre, within about 100–250 mm or ≈ 1–1.5 × member depth — with the female continuous over the support. *Why:* these splices are hinges; they must lie where the moment is small and where the female can carry the male's reaction as a short cantilever.

**R06-024** — **Must.** Never place a splice at or near mid-span of a member in bending unless it is a **bending-capable** splice (okkake-daisen, kanawa, shiribasami, or a daimochi-type splice over a secondary support), and even then prefer a position near the quarter-span/inflection region or over an intermediate support (束 *tsuka*, crossing beam). *Why:* even the best traditional splices transmit only a fraction of the bending capacity of the solid member.

**R06-025** — **Must.** Never place a splice directly over an opening (door, window, 開口部) in a member that spans that opening (lintel, 鴨居 *kamoi*, 差鴨居 *sashigamoi*, dodai under a threshold span without support, head plates above openings). *Why:* there is no post below to support the seat, and openings are where deflection is most visible and most damaging to sliding doors (tategu).

**R06-026** — **Must.** A post must not stand directly on a splice in a sill, nor may a post tenon enter a splice socket. Keep splices at least about one member depth clear of post positions and of the mortises of crossing members. *Why:* the combined cross-section loss causes crushing or splitting, and the concentrated post load would push the joint apart.

**R06-027** — **Should.** In a line of plates (keta) or tie-beams on posts, place the splices in the **same bay position relative to the post** (e.g. all on the east side of their post) unless the erection plan dictates otherwise. *Why:* consistent placement simplifies marking, erection and inspection.

### 6.2 Staggering splices (千鳥, *chidori*; 乱継ぎ, *ranzugi*)

**R06-028** — **Must.** Stagger splices in parallel members so that no two splices fall at the same grid position: e.g. the front and rear plates, adjacent sill lines, upper and lower tiers of nuki, a pair of stacked beams (重ね梁), adjacent purlins (母屋) and rafters (垂木). A minimum offset of **one bay (1 ken / one post interval)** is the normal aim in framing; in boards and battens, offset joints by at least two or three joist/rafter spacings. *Why:* aligned splices create a line of weakness (a "hinge line") across the frame or floor.

**R06-029** — **Should.** In a member spliced more than once (e.g. a long plate of three pieces), keep the pieces as long as possible and place the splices so that the **shortest piece still spans at least two supports**. *Why:* a short piece between two hinges cannot stand by itself and turns the plate into a mechanism.

**R06-030** — **Should.** Place splices in the ridge (棟木) and purlins (母屋) near struts (束) on the cantilever side, staggered between adjacent purlins; in rafters (垂木) place splices only over a purlin or plate, staggered between neighbouring rafters (→ Ch. 07 §6, Ch. 11).

### 6.3 Cross-section loss (断面欠損, *danmen kesson*)

Every mortise, housing, socket and pin hole removes wood. When several members frame into a post or beam **at the same height from several directions**, the net section can drop dramatically.

Illustrative (4-sun ≈ 120 mm post, sugi):

| Case | Members framing in at same height | Approx. remaining net section | Assessment |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | one beam, tenon 30 mm thick | ≈ 75 % | acceptable |
| B | two beams from opposite sides, through tenon or two short tenons in line | ≈ 75 % (same slot) | acceptable if pins placed well |
| C | two beams at 90° (corner), two tenons 30 mm | ≈ 50–55 % | marginal; stagger heights |
| D | four beams (四方差し, *shihō-zashi*), housed (大入れ) and tenoned | ≈ 25–40 % | **unacceptable** without enlarging the post or staggering |

(Figures are approximate and depend on housing depths, tenon lengths and whether housings of 10–15 mm are cut on all four faces.)

After major earthquakes in Japan (notably 1995), damage surveys reported breakage of through-posts (通し柱, *tōshibashira*) at the second-floor level where girders (胴差 *dōsashi*) framed in from several directions — a classic case of excessive cross-section loss at a single height.

**R06-031** — **Must.** Do not frame more than two members into a post at exactly the same height when the post is 4 sun (≈ 120 mm) or less; where three or four members must meet, **stagger their heights** (段違い, *dan-chigai*) so that the mortises overlap little or not at all, or **enlarge the post** (commonly to 5 sun ≈ 150 mm or more for through-posts receiving four-way dōsashi). *Why:* the net section at one height is otherwise too small for bending and for the tenon forces themselves.

**R06-032** — **Should.** As a working guide, keep the net section of a post at any horizontal cut through joints at **not less than about one-half to two-thirds of the gross section**, and mortise widths at **not more than about one-third of the post width**. Where this cannot be met, verify by calculation (→ Ch. 17). *Why:* these are commonly used rules of thumb reflecting both strength and splitting risk.

**R06-033** — **Should.** When staggering heights, offset by **at least the depth of a housing plus a margin**, and preferably so that the tenons of orthogonal members are separated vertically by ≥ ≈ 1 sun (30 mm) of solid wood; better still, let one member pass through (e.g. a through-tenon or a nuki) and hang the others on it with a seat. *Why:* partial overlap still removes most of the section.

**R06-034** — **Should.** Do not combine a splice and a connection in the same short length of a member unless the joint is specifically designed for it (e.g. the plate splice combined with a beam crossing in a *kyōro-gumi* corner, → Ch. 07 §6, Ch. 08). *Why:* the combined loss of section can exceed that of either alone.

### 6.4 Joints and openings, eaves, and exposure

**R06-035** — **Should.** Keep splices and end-grain-exposed joints out of zones exposed to weather (the outer ends of eaves members, sill ends at the external face) or protect them with a cover, a drip, or orientation that sheds water (end grain downward-facing where possible). *Why:* end grain absorbs water ≈ 10–15 times faster than side grain; exposed joints decay first (→ Ch. 16).

## 7. Hierarchy, sequence and assemblability (納まり, *osamari*)

### 7.1 The idea of osamari

*Osamari* (納まり) is the carpenter's word for "how things resolve" — how members meet, how one passes another, how faces align, which one is continuous and which is cut, and how it all goes together. Good osamari is at once structurally sound, **buildable in a definite order**, and visually calm. Many apparently decorative choices in Japanese joinery are in fact solutions to assembly problems.

### 7.2 Hierarchy of members

In any junction there is an implicit ranking:

1. **The continuous member** (通し, *tōshi*) — passes through or over the junction uncut or least cut (e.g. a through-post 通し柱, a sill continuing past a post, a nuki passing through a post).
2. **The receiving member** — has recesses but continues on at least one side (a plate receiving a beam dovetail).
3. **The terminating member** — ends at the junction and carries the tenon, dovetail or head (a beam ending on a plate).

**R06-036** — **Should.** In each junction decide explicitly which member is continuous, which receives and which terminates. Give continuity to the member that carries the larger or more important force (or is longer, or is visible), and let the smaller or secondary member terminate. *Why:* the continuous member keeps its full strength; the terminating member is the one reduced to a tenon.

### 7.3 The order of arrival

Each joint admits only certain directions of assembly (§2.3). The **order in which members arrive** during erection therefore limits what joints are possible:

- A member that must be **dropped from above** into a dovetail socket needs its receiving members to be in place and the space above to be free.
- A member already **captured in two directions** (e.g. a sill already locked at both ends and bolted down) cannot receive a joint that requires movement of that member; the new member must bring a joint that can be inserted from the free direction (side-sliding kanawa, a loose tenon with shachi, a yatoi-sao).
- A piece to be **slid axially** into its partner (okkake, sao) needs room along its axis to be offset and then drawn home.
- In repair (→ Ch. 16), both parts of the joint are usually already fixed; joints that can be assembled **laterally** (kanawa, shiribasami, isuka for light members) or with a **loose tongue** (雇い, *yatoi*) are chosen for that reason.

**R06-037** — **Must.** Verify **assemblability** (組み立て可能性) of every joint in the context of the whole erection sequence: for each member, list the joints at both ends, the direction of insertion each requires, and confirm that a single movement (or a defined sequence) brings the member into place without deforming or disassembling others. *Why:* a joint that cannot be assembled in sequence will be forced, cut down or omitted on site — all silent failures.

**R06-038** — **Must.** The **last member** into any closed loop (e.g. the fourth side of a sill rectangle, the final nuki in a bay, a plate closing between two already-standing frames) must have a joint that can be inserted in the only free direction and then **locked** by a pin, wedge or key (e.g. sao-shachi, yatoi-sao with shachi, kanawa with sen, a nuki locked by wedges). *Why:* geometric joints (dovetails, kama) need a direction of freedom that the last member does not have.

**R06-039** — **Should.** Prefer joints with **one direction of insertion** and a positive lock at the end of that movement. Avoid joints that must be "sprung" into place by bending members or opening a frame. *Why:* springing members damages tenons and leaves residual stresses.

### 7.4 Typical sequence logic

The general order in a house frame (detail in → Ch. 15):

1. Sills (dodai), with splices dropped female-first, anchored.
2. Posts stood into sills (tenons), braced temporarily.
3. Nuki threaded through posts (in bays) and loosely wedged — or, in modern practice, beams first.
4. Girders/plates (dōsashi, keta, hari) dropped onto post tenons, their splices assembled female-first in erection direction; dovetails of secondary beams dropped in.
5. Frame plumbed (建て入れ直し, *tateire-naoshi*), pins driven, wedges tightened.
6. Roof framing (tsuka, moya, munagi), with ridge-raising ceremony (上棟, *jōtō*).

**R06-040** — **Should.** Drive permanent pins and final wedges only **after** the frame has been plumbed and squared. *Why:* pins fix geometry; pinning a frame out of plumb locks the error in.

## 8. Tolerances and fit

### 8.1 Kigoroshi (木殺し, "killing the wood")

**Kigoroshi** is the deliberate pre-compression of the male part of a joint with the hammer (玄能, *gennō*) so that it enters its socket more easily and then **swells back** (by elastic recovery and moisture uptake) to make a tight fit. The gennō has a flat face (平面) for general striking and a slightly convex face (木殺し面, *kigoroshi-men*) used for kigoroshi and for final blows that should not leave a crescent mark.

How it is done:

1. Cut the joint accurately to the line (see §8.3). Kigoroshi does not correct a badly cut joint.
2. With the convex face, strike along the **faces that will bear** (tenon cheeks, the sides of the dovetail or kama head and neck, the edges near the leading end), compressing the surface uniformly. Work in overlapping blows; do not dent locally.
3. Pay special attention to the **arrises (角)** at the leading end, which are compressed most (角を殺す) so that they do not shave or split the socket mouth on entry.
4. Assemble; a light wipe of water (or simply time and ambient humidity) brings the compressed fibres back.

Rules of thumb (representative; vary with species and fit):

- Compression depth ≈ **0.3–1.0 mm** per face in softwoods (hinoki, sugi, hiba, matsu); more near the leading end, tapering to little at the shoulder.
- Least or none in **hard, dense species** (keyaki, kuri, kashi) — there the joint must be cut accurately and eased by paring; hard species do not recover well.
- Not on **end grain** (it does not recover) nor on faces that will be **visible** (the marks remain unless the face is re-planed).

**R06-041** — **Should.** Apply kigoroshi to the bearing faces of the **male** part of a tight joint in softwood, compressing about 0.3–1.0 mm, heaviest at the leading end and arrises, then assemble without delay. *Why:* the male enters without shaving the socket, and recovery produces a tight, gap-free fit.

**R06-042** — **Must not.** Do not use kigoroshi to force a grossly oversized joint (more than about 1 mm per face) or on hardwood members, end grain, or finished visible surfaces. *Why:* over-compressed fibres do not recover fully and crush; hardwoods may split the female; visible marks remain.

**R06-043** — **Should.** Do not apply kigoroshi to parts of the **female**, except to ease the mouth arris of a socket when necessary. *Why:* the female's recesses cannot be swollen back into the joint; compressing them only enlarges the gap.

### 8.2 Shimari (締まり, fit)

*Shimari* is the tightness of a joint after assembly. Practitioners speak of a joint that goes in "with the gennō, not with the hand" and draws its shoulders (胴付き) closed with no visible gap. Targets differ by joint type:

| Joint part | Target fit (softwood, after kigoroshi) | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Tenon cheeks in mortise | line-to-line to slight interference (0 to ≈ +0.5 mm) | too tight splits the post (especially near the post top) |
| Tenon width (along the mortise length) | slight clearance at the ends of the mortise if wedged; snug if pinned only | leave room for wedges where used |
| Dovetail/kama head sides | snug, drawn by the sliding slope | head must bear on its inclined faces, not bottom out |
| Dovetail/kama neck | slight clearance at the tip of the head (≈ 1 mm) | ensures the shoulders (dōtsuki) close |
| Seats (koshikake) | full bearing; zero gap | the seat carries shear; a gap means the head carries it |
| Scarf laps (okkake, kanawa) | faces in full contact; draw by slope or key | the joint should close with the final blows |
| Shoulders (dōtsuki) | zero visible gap on visible faces; slight undercut (inward bevel, 逃げ *nige*) permitted on hidden parts of shoulders | undercut ensures the visible line closes |

**R06-044** — **Must.** Seats, shoulders and bearing surfaces must be in **full contact** after assembly; the hook parts (heads, jaws) must seat against their bearing faces, **not** bottom out at their tips. *Why:* the intended load path goes through bearing faces; if the tip bottoms out first, the shoulders stay open and the joint carries load in the wrong place.

**R06-045** — **Should.** On visible shoulders, cut the concealed part of the shoulder very slightly undercut (逃げ, *nige*) so that the visible arris closes tightly; do not undercut bearing seats. *Why:* guarantees a clean visible line without weakening load-bearing faces.

### 8.3 The line: leave it, take it, split it (→ Ch. 02)

Marking in Japanese carpentry uses a fine ink line (墨, *sumi*) from the *sumitsubo* and *sumisashi*. The line has a width (typically ≈ 0.3–0.5 mm). The cutter must know on which side of the line to cut:

- **墨を残す** (*sumi o nokosu*) — leave the line (cut outside it, the line remains on the piece);
- **墨を払う / 墨を取る** (*sumi o harau / toru*) — take the line (cut through it so that it disappears);
- **墨を割る** (*sumi o waru*) — split the line (cut down its centre).

A common teaching is that the **male leaves the line** and the **female takes (or splits) the line**, so that the two cuts together yield a slight interference fit that kigoroshi then accommodates; but conventions differ between workshops and between joint types, and some masters specify "split the line" for both.

**R06-046** — **Must.** Before cutting, the whole team must follow one declared convention for each joint type (which piece leaves, takes or splits the line). *Why:* if both pieces leave the line the joint will not go together; if both take it the joint is loose by up to a line-width per face.

### 8.4 Trial assembly (仮組, *karigumi*)

**R06-047** — **Should.** Trial-assemble (仮組) complex joints, all splices of exposed members and every joint in primary frames that will be difficult to adjust on site, in the workshop, after kigoroshi-free test fitting; then mark the pairs with matching assembly marks (合印, *aijirushi*, → Ch. 02) and disassemble. *Why:* site adjustment is costly and dangerous at height; trial assembly exposes errors while tools and benches are at hand.

**R06-048** — **Should.** For joints that are driven tight (tenons with kigoroshi, drawn kama), do the trial fit **before** kigoroshi and only part-way home; the final full drive is done once, on site. *Why:* repeatedly driving a tight joint home and out damages the fibres and loosens it.

### 8.5 Species and moisture considerations

| Species | Behaviour relevant to fit | Practice |
|---|---|---|
| Hinoki (檜) | fine, even texture, recovers well after kigoroshi, stable | ideal for tight joints; kigoroshi normal |
| Sugi (杉) | soft, low density, crushes easily, recovers well | generous end distances; more kigoroshi possible; avoid narrow jaws |
| Hiba (ヒバ) | similar to hinoki, slightly harder | as hinoki |
| Matsu (松, akamatsu/kuromatsu) | resinous, stronger, tougher; common for beams | kigoroshi moderate; resin may affect planed faces |
| Keyaki (欅) | hard, dense, high shrinkage, prone to movement | cut precisely; minimal kigoroshi; season well before cutting |
| Kuri (栗) | hard, durable, used for sills | cut precisely; little kigoroshi |
| Kashi (樫) | very hard; used for pins, keys, shachi | never the female of a kigoroshi joint; use for keys |

**R06-049** — **Should.** Where a joint combines two species, make the **harder species the key or male** only if the softer female has adequate end distance and jaw width; otherwise reverse or add a key. *Why:* a hard male driven into a soft female crushes and splits the female.

## 9. Cross-section loss and proportioning guidance

### 9.1 Effective section at a joint

The strength of a joint is limited by its **weakest section** — which is often not the obvious "neck" but a shear plane or a jaw:

- **Dovetail/kama splice in tension:** (1) neck of the male (net area = neck width × depth); (2) shear-out of the male head along the neck lines; (3) splitting or shear-out of the female jaws (kuchiwaki); (4) embedment of the bearing faces. Tests on seated dovetail and kama splices commonly report failure by **splitting of the female jaws** or **fracture at the male neck**, at tensions far below the solid member's capacity.
- **Okkake-daisen / kanawa in bending:** the reduced depth at each lap (roughly half the depth), the pins or key in shear and embedment, and shear along the grain beyond the central step.
- **Tenoned connection:** the net section of the mortised member (§6.3), the tenon's cross-section and the pin/end-distance relation (→ Ch. 08).

A simple estimate (illustrative only, not a design method): for a 105 × 105 mm sugi sill with a koshikake-kama neck 30 mm wide, the neck's net area is ≈ 30 × 105 ≈ 3,150 mm² — about 29 % of the gross section. The joint therefore cannot exceed roughly a quarter to a third of the member's tensile capacity even before considering splitting of the jaws; tests typically show lower values. This is why **koshikake splices must not be relied upon for significant tension** in primary members and why modern practice adds straps or bolts where tension is expected (→ Ch. 17).

**R06-050** — **Must.** Evaluate every tension or bending splice at **all** of its potential failure sections (neck, head shear, jaw splitting, lap depth, pins, embedment), not only at its narrowest visible part. *Why:* the weakest mechanism governs, and it is often the female jaws or a shear plane.

### 9.2 Proportioning rules of thumb

The following representative proportions recur in Japanese carpentry teaching for **horizontal members of moderate size** (W = member width, D = member depth/成 *sei*). Schools differ; see Ch. 07 for joint-specific values.

| Element | Representative proportion | Typical absolute (105–120 mm members) | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dovetail (ari) neck width | ≈ 1/3 W (0.28–0.35 W) | 30–40 mm | narrower → neck fails; wider → jaws fail |
| Dovetail head width (max) | ≈ 0.45–0.55 W | 45–60 mm | leaves jaws ≥ ≈ 1/4 W each |
| Dovetail flare (蟻の勾配) per side | ≈ 1:3 to 1:6 (≈ 10–18°) | — | steeper = more positive hook but more splitting |
| Dovetail length (shoulder to tip) | ≈ 0.6–1.0 W | 60–100 mm | |
| Kama neck width | ≈ 1/3 W | 30–40 mm | |
| Kama head width (max) | ≈ 0.5–0.6 W | 50–65 mm | |
| Kama total length (neck + head) | ≈ 1.2–2.0 D | 130–240 mm | longer on deep plates |
| Sliding slope (滑り勾配) on kama/ari faces | ≈ 1:25–1:40 over the depth | 3–5 mm narrower at bottom on 120 mm depth | draws joint tight when driven down |
| Seat (腰掛け) length | ≈ 1/7–1/4 W | 15–30 mm (5 bu–1 sun) | longer seats on deeper beams |
| Seat height (from bottom) | ≈ 1/3–1/2 D (varies by school) | 35–60 mm | must carry the male's end shear by bearing |
| Okkake-daisen total length | ≈ 3–3.5 D | 315–420 mm | shorter than 2.5 D loses most of its bending strength |
| Kanawa total length | ≈ 2.5–3 D | 260–360 mm | |
| Tenon thickness | ≈ 1/3–1/4 W of the mortised member (commonly 27–30 mm in 105–120 posts) | 27–30 mm | → Ch. 08 |
| Pin (komisen) size | ≈ 12–18 mm square (house scale) | — | → Ch. 09 |
| Daisen (splice pin) | ≈ 15–24 mm (square or round) | 18–21 mm | → Ch. 07, Ch. 09 |

**R06-051** — **Should.** Proportion hook-type splices so that the male neck and the two female jaws have **comparable strength**: as a rule of thumb neck ≈ 1/3 W and each jaw ≥ 1/4–1/3 W. *Why:* a balanced joint fails at a higher load and in a more predictable mode; an unbalanced one fails early at its weakest part.

**R06-052** — **Should.** Give dovetail and kama drawing faces a small **sliding slope** (滑り勾配) over their depth so that the joint tightens as it is driven down, typically about 1:25–1:40. *Why:* it closes the shoulders and compensates for small cutting errors, and it keeps the head bearing on its inclined faces.

## 10. Visible versus hidden joints

### 10.1 Faces and their status

Every face of every member is either **visible** (見え掛かり, *miegakari*; 化粧, *keshō*) or **concealed** (見え隠れ, *miegakure*) in the finished building. The status of each face is decided at the marking stage (→ Ch. 02) and governs how joints are oriented and which variant is chosen.

- In **exposed-structure** buildings (temples, minka with exposed roof framing, sukiya), plates, beams, posts, nuki and even rafters are visible; joint lines are architecture.
- In **concealed-structure** work (houses with ceilings and plastered walls), most splices are hidden, and function dominates.

### 10.2 Aesthetic rules

**R06-053** — **Should.** Orient splices so that the **simplest joint line** (a single straight line or the line of a seat) appears on the visible face, and the complex parts (the kama head, the dovetail flare, scarf steps, pins) are on the concealed face — usually the **top** of a plate or beam and the **back** of a member against a wall. *Why:* a clean single line is the traditional ideal of a visible splice; complicated outlines on visible faces are considered unrefined in formal architecture.

**R06-054** — **Should.** For visible members, prefer **hidden variants** (隠し, *kakushi*): hidden kama (隠し鎌), hidden dovetail (隠し蟻), shiribasami (mechigai inside rather than showing on the face), and splices whose visible trace is a straight line or a mitre (宮島継ぎ, いすか継ぎ) (→ Ch. 07). *Why:* the structure is shown but the mechanism is not.

**R06-055** — **Should.** Place the visible joint lines of adjacent exposed members **in a deliberate relation** (aligned on a module line, or deliberately staggered as required by R06-028), not randomly. *Why:* in exposed-structure buildings, joint lines form part of the composition.

**R06-056** — **Should.** Where pins or keys must be visible, align them on a common line and use pins of consistent section and species; set them flush or with a slight uniform projection according to the building's style. *Why:* visible pins are part of the finish; irregular pins look like repairs.

**R06-057** — **Must.** Do not let aesthetic concealment reduce the structural capacity required at a joint: a hidden variant must provide the same bearing, end distance and jaw widths as the plain variant, or the joint must be relocated to a less-loaded position. *Why:* hidden variants remove wood from the concealed part and can halve the effective head or jaw.

### 10.3 Weather faces

**R06-058** — **Should.** Orient splice lines on exterior members so that **water runs off rather than into** the joint: the upper piece laps over the lower (like shingles) and sloped joint lines drain outward. *Why:* capillary water trapped in splices causes decay long before the rest of the member (→ Ch. 16).

## 11. Choosing a joint: design checklist and decision table

### 11.1 Checklist

Before specifying any joint, answer the following in order:

1. **Member and role.** What member is it (sill, post, plate, girder, beam, tie, purlin, ridge, rafter, nuki, finish member)? What are its dimensions and species?
2. **Forces.** Compression, tension, bending, shear, torsion — which dominate? Reversible (earthquake, wind uplift)? (R06-002)
3. **Position.** Is there a support within ≈ 1–1.5 D? Is the position at mid-span, near an opening, near other mortises? (§6)
4. **Assembly.** Which direction of insertion is available at the moment this joint is made? Is it the last member in a loop? Is it a repair with both parts fixed? (§7)
5. **Visibility.** Which faces are visible? Is there a customary refined form for this member? (§10)
6. **Exposure.** Is it exposed to weather or damp? (R06-035)
7. **Section.** After cutting, what net sections remain in both pieces and in any member it frames into? (§6.3, §9)
8. **Fit and moisture.** Species, moisture content, need for re-tightenable keys? (§4.4, §8)
9. **Code.** Does modern regulation require hardware or tested joint values? (→ Ch. 17)

### 11.2 Decision table (splices)

| Member | Dominant load | Position | Assembly available | Visibility | Recommended splice (→ Ch. 07) | Avoid |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

| Sill, last piece closing a loop | as above | between fixed pieces | slide sideways or axial only | concealed | 竿車知継ぎ, 腰掛け竿車知, 雇い竿 | kama/ari (cannot drop) |

| Boards (floor, ceiling, wall) | alignment | over joists (butt joints) | laid | visible | 本実 (edge), butt over joist, staggered (→ Ch. 14) | aligned end joints |

**R06-059** — **Must.** Record the chosen joint for every junction on the drawings or the workshop plan (板図) together with its orientation (which piece is male/female, direction of insertion) and its locking device. *Why:* marking and erection are carried out by different people at different times; an unrecorded decision is re-made differently.

**R06-060** — **Should.** Prefer the **simplest joint that meets all the requirements** of load, position, assembly and appearance. *Why:* complex joints consume more section, more labour and more opportunities for error; complexity is justified only when it solves a real problem.

## 13. Notes on sources

This chapter synthesizes common Japanese carpentry teaching and published general references; see → Ch. 01 for full bibliographic discussion. Works in English that treat the subject and are widely available include Kiyosi Seike, *The Art of Japanese Joinery* (Weatherhill, 1977); Yasuo Nakahara, *Japanese Joinery: A Handbook for Joiners and Carpenters* (English ed. Hartley & Marks, 1983); and Torashichi Sumiyoshi & Gengo Matsui, *Wood Joints in Classical Japanese Architecture* (Kajima Institute, c. 1989–1991). In German, Wolfram Graubner, *Holzverbindungen* (DVA), compares Japanese and European joints. The Takenaka Carpentry Tools Museum (竹中大工道具館, Kobe) publishes material on the history of saws (ōga) and planes. For modern Japanese practice, the Japan Housing Finance Agency's *Wooden Housing Construction Specification* (木造住宅工事仕様書, revised periodically) sets out mainstream splice placement and anchor-bolt rules. Numerical values in this chapter are representative and should be checked against current regulations and test data for design (→ Ch. 17).

**Uncertainties flagged in this chapter:** dates for the introduction of the ōga (given as 14th–15th century) and the daiganna; the attribution of ryaku-kama to c. 1200 and of the mature okkake-daisen to c. 14th–15th century; the moto-as-female custom (widely reported but not universal); and all proportional figures, which vary by school.
