# Chapter 10 — Structural Frame and Foundations (軸組と基礎 / *jikugumi to kiso*)

## Scope

This chapter deals with the part of a Japanese timber building that carries load from the roof to the ground and resists earthquakes and typhoons: the prepared ground, the podium and foundation stones or modern footings, the sill, the posts, and the horizontal members that tie the posts into a frame. It also covers the floor structure and the layout rules that make a frame work structurally. The chapter sets out *what* members exist, *where* they go, *how big* they are and *why*. For the joints themselves see the catalogues in → Ch. 07 (splices) and → Ch. 08 (connections). Pins and wedges are in → Ch. 09, roof framing above the wall plate is in → Ch. 11, bracket sets (*kumimono*) in → Ch. 12, and the order in which all this is erected in → Ch. 15. How modern law treats these frames is covered in → Ch. 17.

All dimensions are **representative**. Historical proportion systems (*kiwari*, → Ch. 02, Ch. 12) differ between schools, regions and periods, and modern members are governed by calculation and law. Where a figure is a rule of thumb this is stated. Where it depends on law, the current text must be checked (→ Ch. 17).

## 1. The frame as a system: vocabulary and load paths

A Japanese timber building is a **post-and-beam frame** (軸組 *jikugumi*). The walls do not carry the building. They are infills of earth, boards or paper set between the members. The frame has three horizontal zones:

| Zone | Japanese | Typical members |
|---|---|---|
| Base | 足元 *ashimoto* | foundation stones or footing, *dodai* or *ashigatame*, *jinuki*, *jinageshi*, *jifuku* |
| Wall body | 軸部 *jikubu* | posts, *nuki*, *nageshi*, *sashigamoi*, *dōsashi*, lintels (*kamoi*) and sills (*shikii*) of openings |
| Top / roof base | 小屋組下部 | *keta*, *hari*, *kashiranuki*, *daiwa*, bracket sets, then the roof frame (→ Ch. 11) |

**Gravity load path.** Roofing and roof boards go to the rafters (*taruki*), then the purlins (*moya*) and ridge (*munagi*), then the roof struts (*koyazuka*), then the roof beams (*koyabari*), then the wall plates (*keta*), then the posts, then the stones or sill, then the prepared ground.

**Lateral load path.** Inertia forces sit mostly in the roof mass, since a tiled roof is by far the heaviest part of the building. They are collected by the roof diaphragm and the beam grid and passed into the vertical frames. Those frames resist through embedment (*merikomi*) at *nuki* and *sashigamoi* joints, through the shear stiffness of earth walls, and, in modern practice, through braces and panels. The forces then go down to the base, where they are resisted by friction at the stones or by anchor bolts into the footing.

The two directions of a rectangular building have fixed names that are used throughout this book:

- **梁間 *harima*** (beam direction, span): the short direction, crossed by the beams (*hari*).
- **桁行 *ketayuki*** (plate direction): the long direction, parallel to the ridge, along which the wall plates (*keta*) run.

**R10-001** — **Must.** Every member in the frame must have an identifiable continuous load path to the ground for both gravity and lateral loads. A post landing on a beam must be traced to where that beam is supported.
*Why:* Traditional frames are forgiving in deformation but not in missing supports. Most historical failures of machiya and minka alterations come from posts removed or shifted without re-routing the load.

**R10-002** — **Should.** The designer and the *tōryō* think of the frame in three layers: base, wall body and roof base. Each layer should be tied horizontally in both directions before the next is loaded.
*Why:* Each layer works as a horizontal ring (*wa*) that keeps the posts in plan position. The erection order (→ Ch. 15) follows the same logic.

## 2. Site and ground work (地業 *jigyō*)

*Jigyō* is all work done to make the ground able to carry concentrated post loads without uneven settlement. In a traditional building the load comes down at points (stones), not along lines. The ground under each stone matters more than the average bearing capacity of the site.

### 2.1 Site assessment

Traditional builders chose sites by experience:
- They preferred slightly elevated ground (微高地), older terraces and firm loam.
- They avoided filled valleys, old riverbeds, and land that stays wet after rain.
- They observed where water stands.
- They probed with an iron rod (*sagurebō*, 探り棒) or dug test pits.

Modern practice requires a ground survey. For detached houses this is normally a Swedish weight sounding test (SWS試験, now 「スクリューウエイト貫入試験」 in JIS since 2020), because foundation design depends on allowable bearing capacity (→ Ch. 17).

**R10-003** — **Must.** Before placing stones or footings, confirm bearing capacity and uniformity of the ground under every post position, not only the average for the site.
*Why:* Differential settlement tilts posts and opens joints. A frame that is plumb at *tatemae* and settles 20 mm at one corner will jam *tategu* (sliding doors) within a year.

### 2.2 Rammed-earth layering (版築 *hanchiku*)

*Hanchiku* is the ancient continental technique of building ground or walls in thin layers of earth, each compacted hard with wooden rammers.

- **Material:** clayey soil mixed with sand or gravel. Some Nara-period podia alternate clayey and sandy layers.
- **Layer thickness:** each layer is spread about 3–10 cm thick before compaction. Excavated temple podia typically show compacted layers of about 3–6 cm. This is representative; thickness varies by site.
- **Tools:** 突き棒 *tsukibō* (ramming poles) or 杵 *kine* (pestles). For walls, shuttering boards are used.
- **Use:** podia (*kidan*) of Asuka and Nara temples (e.g. at Asuka-dera and Hōryū-ji; the stratigraphy is visible in excavation reports), *tsuijibei* earthen walls, and the core of *kamebara* (§3.3).

Two variants exist.
- **掘込地業 *horikomi-jigyō*** (excavated ground work): the whole footprint is dug out to firm soil and refilled with rammed layers up to podium level.
- **積土地業** (built-up ground work): layers are rammed on the existing surface.

**R10-004** — **Should.** When rammed earth is used for a podium or under stones, compact in thin layers (≈ 1 sun, ~30 mm, after compaction) and never compact a thick lift in one pass.
*Why:* Hand ramming is effective only to a shallow depth. A thick lift is hard on top and loose underneath, and it settles later.

### 2.3 Point foundations under each stone (壺地業 *tsubo-jigyō*)

For most buildings on stones, a pit is dug under each stone position:
1. Dig to firm ground. Typically 1.5–3 shaku (~45–90 cm) deep and roughly 2–3 times the stone's width across. This is representative.
2. Fill with alternating layers of rubble or gravel (栗石 *kuriishi*, 砂利 *jari*) and earth, ramming each layer.
3. Set **根石 *neishi*** (small bedding stones) to wedge and bed the foundation stone at its final height.

For a continuous line of stones, such as a wall line or a *doma* edge, a trench is used instead (布掘り *nuno-bori*).

### 2.4 Ramming: *takotsuki* and *dōtsuki*

- **蛸突き *takotsuki*** ("octopus ramming"): a heavy log or stone rammer with several handles or ropes radiating from it like an octopus's arms. Three to eight people lift it together and drop it.
- **胴突き *dōtsuki*** (also どうづき, 土突き): a heavy weight (log or iron) hung from a tripod or shear legs (三又 *mitsumata*). A team hauls it up on ropes and drops it. Village teams did this in rhythm to work songs, the best known being the *yoitomake* chants; hence *yoitomake* as a name for the work.
- **Modern:** plate compactors (プレート) and rammers (ランマー) replace both.

**R10-005** — **Should.** Ram each lift until the rammer rebounds without leaving a visible depression. Water the soil lightly if dry, but never ram saturated clay.
*Why:* Saturated clay pumps and loses strength under ramming.

### 2.5 Broken-stone bed (割栗石 *warikuri-ishi*)

*Warikuri* is split fist-to-head-sized stone, about 10–20 cm, laid in the bottom of the pit or trench.
- It is traditionally laid **on edge** (小端立て *kobadate*), packed tightly and rammed.
- Voids are filled with **目潰し砂利 *metsubushi jari*** ("eye-blinding gravel", i.e. gap-filling gravel) and rammed again.
- From roughly the Meiji era to the 1990s it was the standard base under concrete footings of houses. It has since been largely replaced by graded crushed stone (砕石地業 *saiseki-jigyō*), which compacts more reliably with machines.

**R10-006** — **Should.** Lay *warikuri* on edge and fill the gaps with *metsubushi* gravel before ramming. Stone laid flat and unfilled acts as a loose layer, not a bed.

**R10-007** — **Must.** Keep organic matter (roots, topsoil, wood waste) out of the *jigyō*. Strip topsoil to mineral ground.
*Why:* Organic material rots, causing settlement. Buried wood also attracts subterranean termites (*shiroari*) to the building.

## 3. Podium (基壇 *kidan*) and ground-level sills (地覆 *jifuku*)

### 3.1 Podium — *kidan* (基壇)
- **Class:** substructure / platform
- **Typical use:** temples, pagodas, gates and major halls from the Asuka period onward. Some shrines, e.g. halls in continental style. Rarely in minka, where the *doma* and the raised floor on stones take its place.
- **Load behaviour:** spreads post loads through the rammed core. Lifts the building above flood and splash level. Protects the post bases from rain and puddles.
- **Proportions:** height varies from ~1 shaku for small halls to over 1 m for major Nara halls. Hōryū-ji Kondō and Pagoda stand on **double podia** (二重基壇 *nijū kidan*). The podium projects beyond the eaves line or close to it, so that the drip line (雨落ち *amaochi*) falls on the paved or graveled surround and not against the podium face. Representative.
- **Facing types:**

| Facing | Japanese | Description | Period / use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rubble | 乱石積 *ranseki-zumi* | Irregular natural stones fitted without coursing | Common, including Nara; simple halls |
| Cut stone | 切石積 *kiriishi-zumi* | Dressed rectangular blocks in courses | Mid-/later periods, gates, walls |
| Framed | 壇上積 *danjō-zumi* | Timber-like "frame" in stone: base course 地覆石 *jifuku-ishi*, vertical posts 束石 *tsuka-ishi*, infill panels 羽目石 *hame-ishi*, capping 葛石 *kuzu-ishi* | Formal temple halls (e.g. Tōshōdai-ji Kondō); the classic Nara–Heian high-status type |
| Tile-stacked | 瓦積 *kawara-zumi* | Horizontal stacked roof tiles or tile fragments | Some Asuka–Nara sites (continental) |
| Brick | 塼積 *sen-zumi* | Fired bricks | Rare, Nara-period continental influence |

- **Surface:** the top is paved with stone slabs or brick, or finished in rammed earth or 三和土 *tataki* (lime–clay–water floor). Nara halls usually had earth or stone floors (土間 *doma*) inside, without raised wooden floors. Raised floors became standard in later *wayō* halls.
- **Rules:**

**R10-008** — **Must.** The podium top must fall slightly outward (≈ 1–2% is typical) and the drip line must lie on the paving beyond the podium edge, not on the podium top.
*Why:* Water standing at post bases is the main cause of rot at the base of temple posts.

**R10-009** — **Should.** In *danjō-zumi*, place a *tsuka-ishi* at every change of direction and at regular intervals that echo the bay rhythm of the hall above. The *kuzu-ishi* capping should overhang the *hame-ishi* slightly.
*Why:* The overhang throws drips clear of the panel joints.

- **Common errors:** building the podium too small, so that the eaves drip hits the podium top; using modern concrete cores without drainage, which traps moisture under stones.

### 3.2 Ground-level sill in temples — *jifuku* (地覆)
- **Class:** horizontal member / sill between posts
- **Typical use:** lowest horizontal timber in temple and shrine walls, running **between** posts just above the podium or floor. It holds the lower edge of plank walls (板壁), of the lower rail of doors (*tobira* pivots, 藁座 *waraza*), and of earth walls.
- **Load behaviour:** ties post bases together weakly. Mainly a closure and support member. It is **not** a continuous sill under posts, which is the important difference from *dodai*.
- **Proportions:** height ≈ 0.4–0.6 × post diameter; thickness a little less than the post diameter so the wall face is recessed. Representative.
- **Orientation rules:** tenoned or housed into the posts (大入れ + 短ほぞ, or 雇い). Often seated on a stone sill course 地覆石 *jifuku-ishi*. The posts always stand on their own stones.
- **Rules:**

**R10-010** — **Should.** Keep *jifuku* just clear of the stone course or podium surface (a few mm to ~1 cm, or on a bed of lime mortar), or set it on a raised stone course. It must not be buried in soil or paving.
*Why:* The underside of the *jifuku* is the most rot-prone surface in a temple wall. Ventilation and drainage decide its life.

### 3.3 Plastered mound — *kamebara* (亀腹)

*Kamebara* ("turtle belly") is the rounded, lime-plastered mound of rammed earth, or earth over a stone core, on which some buildings sit. Examples are *tahōtō* two-storey pagodas and some shrine halls with raised floors on stilts. The plaster sheds water away from the base. Structurally it is a small podium.

**R10-011** — **Should.** Maintain the lime skin of a *kamebara* (patch cracks every few years).
*Why:* Once water enters the earth core it softens and slumps, and the stones set into it tilt.

## 4. Foundation stones and the *ishibadate* system

### 4.1 Foundation stone — *soseki* (礎石) / *tsukaishi* (束石)
- **Class:** foundation / bearing stone
- **Typical use:** *soseki* (also 礎 *ishizue*) is the bearing stone under each structural post in temples, shrines, minka and machiya before the modern era. *Tsukaishi* is the smaller stone under a floor strut (*yukazuka*). In a minka, the stones under the principal posts are sometimes called 根石 or 沓石 *kutsuishi*. *Kutsuishi* is also the modern name for small precast concrete or granite blocks for porch posts.
- **Load behaviour:** carries axial compression. Resists horizontal load only by friction between post and stone, unless a dowel (*dabo*) or raised tenon (*dehozo*) locks them.
- **Proportions:**
  - Stone plan size ≈ **2–3 × the post width** is a representative minimum for house posts, e.g. 1.2–1.5 shaku stones for a 4–5 sun post.
  - Temple *soseki* are much larger: the top face is often 3–4 × the column diameter, especially where a raised seat (*hashira-za*) is carved.
  - Height above finished ground is typically 3 sun – 1 shaku (≈ 90–300 mm) for minka posts on the *doma* or garden side. Floor struts on *tsukaishi* stand on stones 3–5 sun high. Representative.
- **Stone types:** natural river or field boulders with one flattish face (自然石 *shizen-ishi*), used for minka and sukiya; dressed granite (御影石 *mikage-ishi*), used for temples and formal work; tuff (凝灰岩, e.g. 大谷石 in Kantō); and, from the 20th century, precast concrete *tsukaishi*.

- **Surface seat forms (temple stones):**

| Form | Japanese | Description |
|---|---|---|
| Plain | 平 | flat, roughly dressed top |
| Raised round seat | 円形柱座 *enkei hashira-za* | a low circular platform carved in the stone for a round post, ~1–3 cm high; Nara–Heian |
| Raised tenon | 出枘 *dehozo* | a stone boss projecting upward into a mortise in the post base |
| Socket | 枘穴 *hozo-ana* | a hole in the stone receiving a tenon at the post base |
| Sill seat | 地覆座 *jifuku-za* | shallow ledges on the stone for the *jifuku* ends |

- **Rules:**

**R10-012** — **Must.** Every structural post in a stone-based building stands on its own stone, set on compacted *jigyō*. Posts must never be set directly on earth.
*Why:* Timber in contact with soil rots and is attacked by termites within years.

**R10-013** — **Must.** The top of each stone is set so that its highest bearing point lies at the design height from the site datum (±2–3 mm is a representative tolerance at setting). The top face should be as near level as the stone allows.
*Why:* The post is scribed (§5) to the actual stone, but a strongly inclined stone gives a sliding plane that the post will run down in an earthquake.

**R10-014** — **Should.** The top face of a natural stone should be slightly convex or flat, never concave or dished.
*Why:* A dished face collects water under the post base.

**R10-015** — **Should.** Size the stone so that the post centre can move at least ~1–2 post widths in any direction without leaving the stone.
*Why:* In an unanchored *ishibadate* building, sliding in a strong earthquake is expected (§4.3). A post that slides off its stone drops and the frame racks. Designers now working with the *ishibadate* design methods (→ Ch. 17) often size stones with the predicted sliding displacement in mind.

### 4.2 Post standing on stones — *ishibadate* (石場建て)
- **Class:** foundation system
- **Typical use:** all Japanese timber building before the late 19th century. Temples, shrines (except the earth-fast *hottate-bashira* 掘立柱 buildings; Ise still uses buried posts, → Ch. 16), minka, machiya and sukiya. It revived from the 2000s for traditional new-build under special design routes (→ Ch. 17).
- **Load behaviour:**
  - Posts bear directly on stones and are not fixed against uplift.
  - The frame must be tied at the base by *ashigatame*, *jinuki* or *jinageshi* (§6) so that the posts act together.
  - Horizontal force passes through friction. When the base shear exceeds the friction limit, the whole frame, or individual posts, slide.
  - Overturning moments cause posts to lift off their stones briefly (rocking, 浮き上がり).
- **Proportions:** governed by the stones (§4.1) and the base ties (§6).
- **Orientation rules:** each post is scribed to its own stone (§5) and marked with its grid address (*bantsuke*, → Ch. 02) on the post base *and* on the stone.
- **Rules:**

**R10-016** — **Must.** An *ishibadate* frame must have a continuous base tie system (*ashigatame* in both directions, or *jinuki* / *jinageshi* / *jifuku* with adequate depth) connecting all posts just above the stones.
*Why:* Without base ties each post slides independently in an earthquake. With them, the base behaves as one horizontal "raft" that slides as a unit and keeps the frame's geometry.

**R10-017** — **Must.** Do not combine unanchored *ishibadate* posts with very stiff local bracing elements (diagonal *sujikai*, structural plywood) that create large uplift forces at single posts.
*Why:* A stiff braced bay concentrates overturning at its end posts. These lift off the stones, lose friction, and can walk off. Stiff elements belong with anchored bases, ductile ones with free bases (§11.6).

**R10-018** — **Should.** Mark each stone with the grid address and centre lines (芯墨 *shinzumi*) during setting, and keep these marks visible until the post is set.

### 4.3 Sliding behaviour in earthquakes

The idea that *ishibadate* acts as "natural base isolation" (免震) is widespread. The physical picture is as follows. Figures are representative, and research is still evolving (→ Ch. 17).

- **Friction.** Wood on stone has a static friction coefficient of the order of **0.3–0.5**, depending on stone dressing, moisture and the end-grain surface. The base cannot transmit a shear force larger than μ × the vertical load. So the building "sees" a limited lateral acceleration once it slides.
- **Threshold.** Sliding begins only in strong shaking, when the ground acceleration effectively exceeds ~0.3–0.5 g. Under moderate earthquakes an *ishibadate* frame behaves like a fixed-base frame and the upper structure must resist the full load.
- **Displacement.** Sliding displacement is not predictable with precision. It depends on the pulse character of the ground motion. Full-scale shake-table tests at E-Defense in 2008–2011 on traditional frames, including *ishibadate* specimens, observed sliding and rocking at the post bases (→ Ch. 17 for the test programme; magnitudes should be taken from the test reports).
- **Rocking and uplift.** Individual posts lift when the frame racks. Heavy roofs help keep posts seated.
- **Consequences:** limited force is good; residual displacement, damage to services (drains, gas lines) and the risk of posts leaving stones are bad.

**R10-019** — **Should.** In *ishibadate* design, provide for sliding. Make generous stones, use flexible service connections at the building edge, and avoid rigid attachments (concrete steps, adjoining buildings) that would restrain the base locally.
*Why:* A frame that can slide as a raft is safe. A frame restrained at one corner is twisted.

### 4.4 Dowels and tenons into stone — *dabo* / *dehozo*
- **Class:** hozo / dowel (→ Ch. 09 for *dabo* generally)
- **Typical use:** to locate a post on its stone and resist small lateral movements. Temples use them at post bases and at *daito* (大斗) on post tops (→ Ch. 12). Modern *ishibadate* designs use steel or hardwood dowels to meet legal routes (→ Ch. 17: the 2016 amendment to Enforcement Order Art. 42 added a route in which posts are joined to the foundation by *dabo* under Notification No. 690 of 2016).
- **Load behaviour:** a dowel prevents sliding up to its shear capacity, then either shears, splits the post end, or lifts out if the post rocks higher than the dowel length. A **short dowel** acts only as a locator. A **long dowel** changes the base from sliding to pinned.
- **Proportions:** historic *dabo* in stone are of the order of 1–2 sun in section and 1–3 sun long. The 2016 notification describes, per published summaries, a dowel fixed to the foundation and embedded **≥ 90 mm** into a post of **≥ 105 mm** width, or an equivalent. The designer must also verify that the post does not develop harmful tension (uplift) that would unseat the dowel. Check the current text of the notification.
- **Cutting sequence (stone):**
  1. Mark the post centre on the stone from the grid.
  2. Drill with a stone chisel or modern drill to depth.
  3. Set the dowel with lead, sulphur, or modern resin/mortar.
  4. After scribing, bore the matching hole in the post end, slightly oversized in the direction that allows for timber shrinkage.
- **Rules:**

**R10-020** — **Should.** Decide consciously whether the base is to **slide** (no or short dowel), **locate** (short dowel) or **pin** (long dowel + uplift check). Size the stones, base ties and upper frame for that choice.
*Why:* A half-way solution, where a dowel is long enough to stop sliding but too weak to survive the resulting force, splits post bases.

**R10-021** — **Must.** A dowel hole in a post base must not remove more than a small fraction of the bearing area (≲ 10–15 % is a representative limit), and must be centred.
*Why:* The dowel hole reduces bearing area and is a potential water trap at the most rot-prone point of the post. Keep it small, keep the stone top draining away from it, and do not seal the post end airtight — end grain at the base must be able to dry.

## 5. Scribing posts to stones — *hikaritsuke* (光付け)

### Scribing — *hikaritsuke* (光付け, also ひかり付け, 光る *hikaru*)
- **Class:** fitting technique (scribing)
- **Typical use:** fitting the base of a post (*hashira*) or floor strut (*tsuka*) to the irregular top of a natural stone. The same verb, 光る *hikaru* ("to scribe"), is used for fitting any member to an irregular surface: a sill against a natural stone step, a board against a log post (面皮柱 *menkawa-bashira* in sukiya), wall boards against a crooked beam, or a *tokobashira* against a natural *tokogamachi* (→ Ch. 13/14).
- **Load behaviour:** a well-scribed base spreads the post load over a large bearing area and gives friction over the whole contact. A badly scribed post bears on one or two high points, crushes locally (end-grain crushing), rocks, and can split.
- **Tools:**

| Tool | Japanese | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Scribing gauge | ひかり / 光り定規 / ひかり板 *hikari-ita* | A thin wooden or bamboo lath with a pointed end (the "tracer") and a marking point (ink brush *sumisashi*, pencil or knife) at a fixed offset. It is run over the stone while the marker transfers the profile to the post at a constant vertical distance. Carpenters make their own; forms vary by region and school. |
| Profile slats | ひかり板 (set of slats) | Alternative method: many thin slats held in a frame, pushed down to touch the stone and then fixed. The frame is laid against the post end to transfer the profile. |
| Plumb bob | 下げ振り *sagefuri* | Plumbing the post in two directions during scribing |
| Ink line / square | 墨壺 *sumitsubo*, 差金 *sashigane* | Centre lines on post and stone |
| Level / water level | 水準器, 水盛り | Height of reference line |
| Temporary supports | 仮筋交い, 楔 *kusabi*, 飼木 *kaiki* (packers) | Holding the post plumb above the stone during scribing |
| Cutting tools | 鋸 *nokogiri*, 鑿 *nomi*, 突き鑿 *tsukinomi*, 釿 *chōna* (for large temple posts) | Cutting to the scribed line |
| Marking powder / ink | 墨, 弁柄 *bengara*, chalk | Blued stone to find high spots at trial fitting |

- **Proportions:** the post is cut **long** in the workshop by the maximum relief of the stone plus a margin, typically 1–2 sun (30–60 mm) for natural stones. The scribing offset (gap between stone high point and post end during scribing) is set equal to this excess.

- **Procedure** (representative; details vary between workshops):
  1. **Set and mark the stone.** The stone is bedded (§2.3) with its highest bearing point at design height. Grid lines (通り芯 *tōrishin*) are carried from the batter boards (*yarikata*, → Ch. 15) by string lines and plumb bob, and inked on the stone as centre marks (芯墨).
  2. **Prepare the post.** The post has its four centre lines (芯墨) and a **datum line** (基準墨, e.g. a level line at 1 shaku, or the floor line) marked from the story pole (*shakuzue*, → Ch. 04). The base is left long.
  3. **Stand the post over the stone** on packing (*kaiki*) or wedges so that the lowest point of the post end is clear of the highest point of the stone by the planned offset.
  4. **Plumb and align.** Plumb the post on two adjacent faces with the *sagefuri*. Align its centre lines with the stone's centre marks. Brace it temporarily in two directions.
  5. **Set the height.** Adjust the packing until the datum line on the post is at the exact design height relative to the site datum (from the water level or modern level) **plus** the scribing offset. Every post is scribed relative to the same datum, so after cutting the frame will be level whatever the stones do.
  6. **Scribe.** Hold the *hikari* gauge vertical and trace around the stone's surface along each face of the post and across the end. The marker transfers the stone's profile to the post faces, lowered by the constant offset. On large temple posts, points are also transferred across the end grain so the cut can be made in the correct three-dimensional form.
  7. **Remove and cut.** Take the post down. Saw and pare to the line. Common practice is to **relieve the centre** of the end slightly (中すかし, a few mm), so that bearing is on a continuous ring near the perimeter and the post cannot rock on a central high point. Some carpenters prefer full-face bearing on hard stones. This is a **variant**; both are found.
  8. **Trial fit.** Re-stand the post. Look for daylight (光 *hikari*, "light") between post and stone. One popular explanation of the name is that a good fit shows no light. The other derives it from the tool. Rub ink, *bengara* or chalk on the stone to reveal contact spots on the post end. Pare the high spots. Repeat until contact is even around the perimeter.
  9. **Check height again** against the datum line. Correct if necessary by further paring. Excess length removed by paring must be compensated at the next post by the same datum procedure, not by eye.
  10. **Dowel** (if used, §4.4): bore the post end, centred on the stone's dowel.
  11. **Mark** the *bantsuke* on the post end and on the stone.

- **Assembly:** the post, now fitted, is removed and stored until *tatemae*. It is stood again in sequence (→ Ch. 15). In practice, many carpenters scribe all posts on the first day of raising, in grid order, when the stones are finally set.
- **Rules:**

**R10-022** — **Must.** Scribe every post to its **own** stone, in its final position, with the post plumbed in two directions and set to a common height datum. Posts are not interchangeable after scribing.
*Why:* Scribing adapts both the shape and the length of the post to its stone. Swapping posts destroys both.

**R10-023** — **Must.** Scribing is done relative to a level datum on the post, never by measuring down from the top of the post.
*Why:* Post tops must end up in one plane for the beams. The irregularity of the stones is absorbed entirely at the post base.

**R10-024** — **Should.** Achieve continuous contact around the perimeter of the post end. Where full contact is impossible, bearing must at least be distributed over three well-separated zones.
*Why:* Point bearing on end grain crushes the fibres, causing settlement and tilting, and starts splits.

**R10-025** — **Should.** Cut the post end square to the post axis wherever the stone permits. Where the stone slopes, keep the scribed surface as close to horizontal as possible and never let the post "sit" on a slope steeper than about 1 in 10.
*Why:* A sloping bearing produces a horizontal thrust component that walks the post off the stone.

**R10-026** — **Should.** Protect the scribed end grain from splitting by chamfering its outer edge slightly (a few mm) after fitting.
*Why:* A sharp edge splinters under concentrated load. The small chamfer also sheds splashed water.

- **Common errors:**
  - Scribing without plumbing the post first, so the post leans after cutting.
  - Measuring the scribe offset from the top of the post.
  - Scribing to a stone that is not yet firmly bedded; it later settles.
  - Leaving a hollow in the stone that holds water beneath the post.
  - Forgetting the thickness of any packing or lead sheet (historically some carpenters placed thin lead or copper under temple posts; a **variant**) when setting heights.

- **Variants:**
  - 束の光付け: scribing floor struts to *tsukaishi*, with the same method on a small scale.
  - 敷石・沓脱石への光付け: scribing sill boards or *engawa* posts to garden stones.
  - Scribing to a **dressed** granite stone with a flat top, where the procedure reduces to squaring the post end and checking bearing.

```
      post (plumbed, datum line D at design height + offset t)
       |          |
       |  D ------|---  datum
       |          |
       |          |
       |__________|  <- post end (left long)
          ^  t  ^           hikari gauge: tracer on stone,
    ~~~~~/\~~~~/\~~~~       marker on post at constant offset t
   stone top (irregular)
   after cutting along the scribed line, lower by t → full contact
```

## 6. Ground-level ties: *ashigatame*, *negarami*, *jinageshi*

### 6.1 Foot-tie beam — *ashigatame* (足固め)
- **Class:** horizontal tie / sashimono (差物) — a deep member inserted between posts
- **Typical use:** in *ishibadate* minka, machiya and temples with raised floors. A horizontal beam just above the stones, typically at or below floor level, running between posts in **both** directions. It also carries the *ōbiki* (§12) of the floor.
- **Load behaviour:**
  - Ties post bases so they move together (raft action, §4.2).
  - Forms, with the posts, a moment-resisting joint at the bottom of the storey. In an unanchored base it prevents the post bases from spreading or rotating independently.
  - Carries floor loads from the *ōbiki* and *neda*.
- **Proportions:** representative house-scale sections are 4 × 5–6 sun (120 × 150–180 mm) up to 4.5 × 7 sun in large minka, i.e. depth ≥ 1.2–1.5 × the post width. The top is usually set 1–1.5 shaku above the stone tops, just below the floor framing. Representative.
- **Orientation rules:** the member runs post to post and is inserted into the posts from the side. Where two *ashigatame* meet at one post from perpendicular directions they must be set at **different heights** (段違い *danchigai*, typically offset by 1–2 sun), or joined by interlocking tenons inside the post, so that the post section is not cut through at one level.
- **Joints** (→ Ch. 08, Ch. 09):
  - 雇いほぞ + 込み栓 / 鼻栓 (loose tenon with pegs);
  - 竿車知 *sao-shachi* (stub-and-rod through the post locked by *shachi* keys);
  - 小根ほぞ差し + 割楔 (tenon with shoulder, wedged);
  - 渡りあご over a sill in some regions.
- **Rules:**

**R10-027** — **Must.** In *ishibadate*, *ashigatame* (or equivalent base ties) must run in both principal directions on every structural grid line that has posts. Two perpendicular *ashigatame* entering one post must not be at the same level unless the joint is specifically designed for it (e.g. interlocking *sao* tenons).
*Why:* The post section at a same-level double mortise can drop below half its area and break under the moment the *ashigatame* is supposed to resist.

**R10-028** — **Should.** Use a pegged or keyed joint (*komisen*, *hanasen*, *shachi*) that resists pull-out.
*Why:* A plain tenon pulls out when the frame racks. *Ashigatame* are in alternating tension and compression during an earthquake.

**R10-029** — **Should.** Set the underside of *ashigatame* at least ~30 cm above ground. Keep the crawl space open for ventilation and inspection (§8.3).

- **Common errors:** *ashigatame* only in one direction; shallow members (3.5 × 3.5 sun) that act as mere floor bearers; nailing instead of pegging.
- **Variants:** In some regions an *ashigatame* is replaced by a heavy **地貫 *jinuki*** (nuki at the base) or by *jinageshi* (below). The design methods for traditional frames developed from 2008 (→ Ch. 17) treat the *ashigatame* as an essential part of the *ishibadate* system.

### 6.2 Base nuki — *jinuki* (地貫)

The lowest *nuki* (§10.1), passing through the posts just above the stones or sill. In walls it also forms the lowest support for the *komai* (wattle, → Ch. 14) of earth walls. In a *dodai* building the *jinuki* is set a little above the sill. Its structural role there is minor, because the sill is the tie.

### 6.3 Strut ties — *negarami* (根がらみ / 根搦み)

*Negarami* are thin boards or *nuki* (typically 15–24 × 90–105 mm) nailed or let into the **floor struts** (*yukazuka*) near their base, connecting them in rows, and often diagonally.

**R10-030** — **Should.** Provide *negarami* on floor struts taller than about 1.5 shaku (≈ 450 mm), in both directions or diagonally.
*Why:* Tall struts on loose stones topple sideways when the floor is loaded unevenly or the building shakes.

### 6.4 Ground *nageshi* — *jinageshi* (地長押)

*Jinageshi* is a *nageshi* (§10.2) at the base of the wall, clasping the posts just above the podium or floor. In early temple architecture (Nara–Heian, *wayō*), before *nuki* spread, *jinageshi* together with *uchinori-nageshi* formed the main horizontal ties. They were nailed to posts that were notched to receive them. See §10.2.

## 7. Continuous footings and the sill (布基礎 *nuno-kiso* / 土台 *dodai*)

### 7.1 Strip footing — *nuno-kiso* (布基礎) and mat footing (べた基礎 *beta-kiso*)
- **Class:** modern foundation
- **Typical use:** almost all Japanese timber houses since the mid-20th century. First concrete strip footings (with or without reinforcement). Since the 1990s–2000s, reinforced strip footings or mat foundations (*beta-kiso*), which became the dominant choice for new houses.
- **Load behaviour:** carries line loads from the sill. Resists uplift through anchor bolts. Ties the base horizontally. The ground-level diaphragm is formed by the footing and floor, not by base timbers.
- **Proportions:** set by MLIT Notification No. 1347 (2000) for footing design, and by specification standards. For example, the widely used *Flat 35* timber-house specification (住宅金融支援機構『木造住宅工事仕様書』) gives upstand height ≥ 300–400 mm above ground and upstand width ≥ 120–150 mm. These are representative; check the current editions, → Ch. 17.
- **Rules:** see §7.2 and → Ch. 17. Traditional carpenters working with modern footings keep the traditional logic of the frame (continuous sill, *nuki*, earth walls) above it.

### 7.2 Sill — *dodai* (土台)
- **Class:** horizontal member / base sill
- **Typical use:** continuous sill laid on a footing (or historically on a line of stones or a stone kerb), into which the posts are tenoned. *Dodai* were used in some traditional buildings: storehouses (*kura*), some urban machiya and later minka, especially in Kantō. They became universal with concrete footings.
- **Load behaviour:**
  - Distributes post loads onto the footing.
  - Ties the post bases together.
  - Transfers shear to the footing via anchor bolts (and friction).
  - Resists uplift only through post-to-sill connections and anchor bolts.
  - Loaded **across the grain** in bearing under each post. Sill crushing (*merikomi*) under heavily loaded posts is a known serviceability issue.
- **Proportions:** 3.5 sun (105 mm) square minimum in modern houses; 4 sun (120 mm) square is common in quality or traditional work; up to 4.5–5 sun in heavy frames. The sill width is normally equal to or greater than the post width.
- **Species:**

| Species | Japanese | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Hinoki (Japanese cypress) | 檜 | Traditional first choice; heartwood durable and moderately termite-resistant |
| Hiba (Thujopsis) | 檜葉 / 青森ヒバ | Highly decay- and termite-resistant heartwood (hinokitiol); much used in Tōhoku and for sills everywhere |
| Chestnut | 栗 *kuri* | Very durable, hard; traditional for sills and *tsuka* in rural areas and for railway sleepers |
| Kōyamaki | 高野槙 | Water-resistant; used in wet places, bath rooms |
| Keyaki | 欅 | Strong and durable heartwood; for heavy work and temple sills; prone to movement |
| Pressure-treated sugi/hinoki or imported hemlock | 防腐処理材 | Modern standard where heartwood species are not used |

- **Orientation rules:**
  - Heart side (木表/木裏 conventions → Ch. 03): the choice varies by school. Many carpenters lay the sill **bark side (木表) up** so that cupping throws water off. Others argue for heart side up for bearing. A **variant** — follow the school.
  - The butt end (*moto*) is conventionally placed toward the building's main corner or the direction from which laying starts; this is also school-dependent.
  - Splices are placed near posts but **not under** them, and the **upper piece (上木 *uwaki*)** of the splice is held down by an anchor bolt (§7.3).
- **Joints** (→ Ch. 07, Ch. 08):
  - Splices: 腰掛け鎌継ぎ *koshikake-kama-tsugi* (standard), 腰掛け蟻継ぎ *koshikake-ari-tsugi*.
  - Corners: 大留め, 片蟻 / 大入れ蟻掛け, 相欠き with a through tenon of the corner post.
  - T-junctions: 大入れ蟻掛け.
  - Post-to-sill: 短ほぞ (modern precut, with hardware), 長ほぞ + 込み栓 *nagahozo-komisen* (traditional, resists uplift without hardware).
- **Rules:**

**R10-031** — **Must.** Posts stand on the sill through tenons. The sill is not cut through by two mortises from different directions at one point. Corner and T-junctions are arranged so that the post tenon passes through the upper sill piece and into or through the lower one.
*Why:* The sill at a corner is the weakest point of a *dodai* frame. Lapped corners without the post tenon pinning them open under racking.

**R10-032** — **Should.** Use *nagahozo-komisen* (long tenon with draw-pin) for post-to-sill connections in traditional work, with the tenon length ≈ sill depth minus a small clearance, and the peg (*komisen*) of hardwood about 15–18 mm square. Representative; → Ch. 08/09.
*Why:* It provides a tested uplift resistance (it is recognised in the connection tables of Notification 1460 for low-demand locations; → Ch. 17) without steel.

**R10-033** — **Should.** Keep sill splices in positions where the sill is continuously supported by the footing. Never splice over an opening in the footing (e.g. an access opening, 人通口).

### 7.3 Anchor bolts and splice positions

**R10-034** — **Must** (modern *dodai* construction). An anchor bolt holds the **upper (male) piece** of each sill splice close to the splice (within ~ 200 mm is a common specification), and is placed near both end posts of each braced wall. Spacing along the sill is limited, commonly to ≤ 2.7 m (2-storey) or ≤ 3.6 m in older/one-storey specifications. Representative figures from housing specifications; check the current code-related specifications, → Ch. 17.
*Why:* The lower piece of a *kama* or *ari* splice is held down by the upper piece. If the upper piece is not bolted, the splice can open vertically under uplift.

**R10-035** — **Must.** Anchor bolts must be positioned so that they do not coincide with post mortises, splice tongues or the *komisen* holes. Coordinate their layout with the carpenter's *itazu* (→ Ch. 02) **before** the concrete is poured.
*Why:* An anchor bolt set by the concrete crew without the carpenter's plan often lands in a mortise. Bolts must never be pushed into fresh concrete after pouring (田植え *taue*, "rice planting"); they must be fixed in position before the pour.

### 7.4 Sill on a stone kerb (historic variant)

In storehouses (*dozō*) and some urban machiya, a sill lay on a continuous stone kerb (布石 *nunoishi*, 延べ石 *nobeishi*) or on a low wall of cut stones. Posts were tenoned into the sill, and the sill was not anchored. This is the historic prototype of the modern *dodai* system. It behaves like *ishibadate* in sliding, but the whole sill slides as one piece.

## 8. Choosing between *ishibadate* and *dodai*; moisture and termites

### 8.1 Comparison

| Aspect | *Ishibadate* (posts on stones + *ashigatame*) | *Dodai* on continuous footing |
|---|---|---|
| Base condition | Free (sliding, rocking) or dowel-located | Fixed by anchor bolts |
| Force into building in severe quake | Limited by friction once sliding starts | Not limited; full ground motion |
| Deformation demand in upper frame | Reduced if sliding occurs; similar below threshold | Full |
| Uplift | Posts may lift; needs heavy roof or ties | Resisted by anchors and hold-downs |
| Durability of base | Post bases exposed, dry and inspectable; each post end in air | Sill in contact with concrete; moisture can accumulate; hidden |
| Repair | Post base can be spliced (*netsugi*, → Ch. 16) individually | Sill replacement requires jacking the whole wall |
| Termites | Bases visible; tubes easily seen | Tubes hidden behind footings and insulation |
| Legal route in Japan today | Special: Art. 42 exceptions, calculation route (→ Ch. 17) | Standard specification route |
| Stiff bracing compatible? | No (R10-017) | Yes |
| Site tolerance | Stones set individually; forgiving of slopes | Needs level footing |

**R10-036** — **Should.** Choose *ishibadate* when (a) the frame is designed as a ductile *nuki*/earth-wall system with *ashigatame*, (b) the roof is heavy enough to keep the posts seated, (c) the site allows sliding, with no rigid attachments, and (d) the design route (→ Ch. 17) can be followed. Choose *dodai* on a footing when stiff bracing or panel walls are used, the site is constrained, or a standard route is required.
*Why:* The two systems are internally consistent. Mixing them, e.g. an *ishibadate* base with stiff braced walls, produces the worst of both (R10-017).

**R10-037** — **May / Variant.** A hybrid is common in modern "traditional" houses: a concrete footing with a sill, but the upper frame built with *nuki*, *sashigamoi* and earth walls. Another hybrid is *ishibadate* on concrete "stones" (独立基礎 isolated pads) with an *ashigatame* frame. Both are legitimate but must be designed as systems.

### 8.2 Height of timber above ground

**R10-038** — **Must.** Keep the lowest timber (sill, *ashigatame*, post base) well clear of splash and ground moisture. Traditional practice: post bases on stones at least ~3 sun (≈ 90 mm) above finished ground outside, more in snow regions. Modern practice: footing upstand ≥ 300–400 mm above ground, and first-floor finish height ≥ 45 cm above ground unless the ground under the floor is sealed. The latter follows Enforcement Order Art. 22, as commonly summarised; → Ch. 17.
*Why:* Rain splash reaches about 30 cm. Rot and termite attack concentrate within 1 m of the ground. The Enforcement Order (Art. 49) requires anti-decay measures and, where needed, anti-termite measures for posts, braces and sills within 1 m of the ground.

### 8.3 Underfloor ventilation (床下換気 *yukashita kanki*)

Traditional buildings on stones have an open underfloor. The spaces between stones are open or closed only with loose boards or lattice (*nezumi-ita*), so air flows freely. With continuous footings, ventilation must be designed:
- Vent openings through the footing (traditionally, per Art. 22, openings of ≥ 300 cm² per ≤ 5 m of external wall, with rodent mesh; representative summary; check current text).
- Or a continuous ventilating spacer between footing and sill (基礎パッキン *kiso packing*, since the 1990s).

**R10-039** — **Must.** The underfloor must either be ventilated (open stones, vents or ventilating spacers) or be deliberately sealed and conditioned. Never leave it partly enclosed and unventilated.
*Why:* A damp, still crawl space with timber in it is where rot and termite colonies start.

**R10-040** — **Should.** Keep the underfloor accessible (≥ 330–450 mm clear height, with an inspection hatch), and keep it free of timber offcuts, formwork remains and organic debris after construction.

### 8.4 Termites (白蟻 *shiroari*)

Japan has two main subterranean termites. *Yamato shiroari* (ヤマトシロアリ, *Reticulitermes speratus*) is found almost everywhere. *Ie shiroari* (イエシロアリ, *Coptotermes formosanus*) occurs in warm coastal regions from Kantō westward and is more destructive.

**R10-041** — **Should.** Use naturally durable heartwood (hinoki, hiba, kuri, kōyamaki) or treated timber for all members within 1 m of the ground. Keep sapwood (白太 *shirata*) out of the base zone.
*Why:* Sapwood of all Japanese species is non-durable. Heartwood durability ratings (JAS / JIS classes) place hiba, hinoki and kuri among the most durable domestic species (→ Ch. 03).

**R10-042** — **Should.** Design the base so that termite shelter tubes would be **visible**: exposed post bases on stones, no timber buried in soil, no earth-filled gaps against sills, inspection space under floors.
*Why:* Traditional termite defence was inspection plus durable wood, not poison.

## 9. Posts (柱 *hashira*)

### 9.1 Continuous post — *tōshibashira* (通し柱) and storey post — *kudabashira* (管柱)
- **Class:** vertical member
- **Typical use:**
  - *Tōshibashira*: a single post running from base to the upper wall plate through two storeys, in two-storey houses, machiya and some minka.
  - *Kudabashira*: a post one storey high. At the ground floor it runs from the base to the second-floor girder (*dōsashi*) or beam. At the upper floor it runs from the floor girder to the wall plate.
- **Load behaviour:**
  - The *tōshibashira* provides continuity at the level where the second-floor girders (*dōsashi*) and floor beams frame in. It helps hold the two storeys together against uplift and keeps corners from separating.
  - The price of this is a heavy **section loss** at the floor level, where up to four members may be jointed into one post. This is where *tōshibashira* break in earthquakes.
  - *Kudabashira* are simpler, with no section loss mid-height, but they rely on the post-to-girder joints for continuity.
- **Proportions:**
  - *Tōshibashira*: typically 4–5 sun (120–150 mm) square in houses; 4 sun (120 mm) is a common modern minimum in quality work. 5 sun is used where four girders frame in.
  - *Kudabashira*: 3.5 sun (105 mm) in modern mass housing; 4 sun (120 mm) in traditional or *nuki* work.
  - Traditional machiya often use 4 sun throughout, with larger posts at key points.
  - Representative; legal minimum sizes are set by Enforcement Order Art. 43 as a ratio to storey height (→ Ch. 17).
- **Orientation rules:**
  - Posts stand with the **butt (*moto*) down**. Inverting a post (逆柱 *sakabashira*) is a taboo; → Ch. 03.
  - The heart-side/bark-side orientation and the face with the best grain are turned to the room (→ Ch. 03).
  - A post with a back-kerf (背割り *sewari*) has the kerf turned to a face that will be hidden in the wall.
- **Rules:**

**R10-043** — **Must.** In a two-storey building, the outer corners of the building are *tōshibashira*, or the corner joints are reinforced to equivalent strength. This is also a legal requirement (Enforcement Order Art. 43; → Ch. 17).
*Why:* The corners carry the largest uplift and are where the two directions of girders meet. A spliced corner opens first.

**R10-044** — **Should.** Where a *tōshibashira* receives girders from more than two directions at one level, increase the post to ≥ 4.5–5 sun, or stagger the joint heights (*danchigai*) by at least the depth of a tenon, so that the net section at any level is not less than about half of the gross section.
*Why:* Enforcement Order Art. 43 requires reinforcement where more than one third of a post's required section is cut away (→ Ch. 17). Tradition and testing show that fractures concentrate at such cut sections.

**R10-045** — **Should.** Place *tōshibashira* at the building corners and at the ends of principal walls in both directions, and at intersections of main girder lines, not at random positions. A common guideline for houses is at least one *tōshibashira* at each external corner plus key internal intersections.

### 9.2 Great central post — *daikoku-bashira* (大黒柱) and *ebisu-bashira* (恵比寿柱)
- **Class:** principal post (minka, machiya)
- **Typical use:**
  - The *daikoku-bashira* is the largest post in a minka. It usually stands at the junction between the earth-floored work area (*doma*) and the raised living rooms, at the intersection of the main beams.
  - The *ebisu-bashira* is a secondary principal post, typically on the other side of the same beam line or at the *doma* side. It is smaller than the *daikoku*.
  - The names come from the gods of fortune Daikoku and Ebisu (七福神). Regional names include 大黒柱, 中柱 *nakabashira*, and 大極柱 in some texts.
- **Load behaviour:** collects the heaviest beam loads (*ushibari*, *sashigamoi* from two or more directions). Resists lateral load by bending as a stout cantilever-like post between *ashigatame* and the beams. Symbolically and practically the "pillar" of the house.
- **Proportions:** typically 6 sun to over 1 shaku square (≈ 180–300+ mm), in keyaki (zelkova), matsu (pine), kuri, or sugi/hinoki in some regions. The *ebisu-bashira* is typically 5–7 sun. Representative; wealthy farmhouses and machiya have *daikoku* over 1 shaku.
- **Rules:**

**R10-046** — **Should.** Frame the principal *sashigamoi* and beams into the *daikoku-bashira* at staggered heights. Use keyed joints (*sao-shachi*, *hanasen*) that do not cut the post by more than about one third at any level.
*Why:* The *daikoku* receives many members. Its size is only useful if it is not mortised through at one height.

**R10-047** — **Should.** The *daikoku-bashira* stands on its own large stone with a proper *jigyō*, deeper than for ordinary posts.
*Why:* It carries a multiple of an ordinary post's load. Settlement of the *daikoku* distorts the whole house.

### 9.3 Round and square posts; chamfers; *chimaki*; entasis
- **Round posts (丸柱 *marubashira*):** used in temples and high-status shrines. They are turned or hand-worked from octagonal (八角 *hakkaku*) through sixteen-sided to round with *yariganna* and planes (→ Ch. 04). Round posts carry *kashiranuki* and *daito* bracketing.
- **Square posts (角柱 *kakubashira*):** used in houses, shoin and most shrines (e.g. *shinmei-zukuri* uses round posts at Ise; many shrine styles use square chamfered posts in subsidiary halls).
- **Chamfers (面取り *mentori*):** the arrises of square posts are chamfered. The size of the chamfer relative to the post width became smaller over time, and this is one of the main dating criteria for Japanese buildings. Representative sequence, as usually cited in architectural history (values vary between buildings):

| Period | Chamfer width / post width (approx.) |
|---|---|
| Nara–early Heian | ~1/5–1/7 (large, "ōmen" 大面) |
| Kamakura | ~1/7–1/10 |
| Muromachi | ~1/10–1/14 |
| Momoyama–Edo | ~1/14–1/20 and smaller |
| Sukiya | very small "thread" chamfer (糸面 *itomen*) or natural log surface (面皮 *menkawa*) |

Chamfer profiles include 几帳面 *kichōmen* (a chamfer with small fillets, typical of shinden and shoin details), 大面, 糸面, 猿頬面 *saruzura-men* and others (→ Ch. 14).

- **Tapered ends — *chimaki* (粽):** the top (and sometimes bottom) of a round post is rounded off in a curve, like the pointed end of a *chimaki* rice dumpling. This is characteristic of *zenshūyō* (Zen style) halls (→ Ch. 12). The curve starts at a fixed fraction of post height and reduces the diameter at the top to meet the *daiwa*/*daito*.
- **Entasis — *dōbari* (胴張り):** a swelling of the post shaft. It is well known in Asuka-period buildings, especially the Hōryū-ji Kondō and Middle Gate, where the maximum diameter lies roughly in the lower third of the shaft and the post tapers noticeably to the top. The comparison with Greek entasis is a Meiji-era idea and not established historically; it should be treated as an analogy only. *Dōbari* disappears in later *wayō* buildings.
- **Rules:**

**R10-048** — **Should.** In restoration or period-accurate new work, match the chamfer proportion, the *chimaki* curve and any *dōbari* to the period and style of the building (→ Ch. 12, Ch. 16).
*Why:* These are defining features of each style. A wrong chamfer is immediately visible to anyone trained, and it falsifies the record in heritage work.

**R10-049** — **Must.** Chamfers and *chimaki* are cut after all joinery marking is complete and are laid out from the post's centre lines, not from the arrises.
*Why:* The arrises disappear when chamfered. Centre lines are the permanent reference (→ Ch. 02).

### 9.4 Post size and spacing
- **Houses:** posts normally stand on a **1-ken grid** (6 shaku ≈ 1,818 mm in *inaka-ma*; tatami-based dimensions in *kyō-ma*; → Ch. 02). Intermediate posts at the **half-ken** (3 shaku, ≈ 909 mm) are used at door and window jambs and wherever beams need support. Wall studs (間柱 *mabashira*, typically 1 × 3.5 sun) at the half-ken are *not* posts and carry no beams.
- **Temples:** bay widths (柱間 *hashirama*) vary. The central bay (中の間 *naka-no-ma*) is often the widest, e.g. 10–20 shaku in major halls, and side bays diminish. Column diameter in *kiwari* systems is often proportioned to the central bay. A representative range is ~1/8–1/10 of the central bay width. The *Shōmei* (匠明) and other kiwari texts give school-specific formulas (→ Ch. 01, Ch. 12).
- **Post lean (柱の転び *korobi*):** in some structures posts are inclined inward (転び). This is typical of torii, bell towers (鐘楼 *shōrō*, often 四方転び *shihō-korobi*, leaning in both directions), gates and some pagoda framing. The lean gives visual stability and a geometric bracing effect. The geometry is worked out with *kikujutsu* (→ Ch. 05). Ordinary house and temple hall posts are vertical.

**R10-050** — **Should.** In a house, place a post at every beam end and at every beam splice support. Never let a principal beam bear on a wall plate mid-way between posts without a post or a checked calculation.
*Why:* A plate spanning one ken can carry a beam end only if sized for it. The traditional grid places posts where loads arrive.

**R10-051** — **Should.** Keep bay spacing regular (1 ken, or its halves). Where a larger opening is needed (2 ken or more), span it with a *sashigamoi* or deep beam designed for the load, not with an ordinary *kamoi* lintel.

## 10. Horizontal members

### 10.1 Penetrating tie — *nuki* (貫)
- **Class:** horizontal tie passing through posts (through-*nuki* 通し貫 *tōshi-nuki*) or tenoned into them
- **Typical use:** all traditional frames after the introduction of *daibutsuyō* (the "Great Buddha style" used by Chōgen for the rebuilding of Tōdai-ji in the late 12th century), which popularised systematic *nuki*. Before that, *kashiranuki* at post tops existed, but the wall-body ties were mainly *nageshi* (§10.2). *Nuki* run through mortises in the posts and are wedged. In earth walls they also carry the bamboo lath (*komai*).
- **Levels and names** (house, bottom to top; not every building has all):

| Name | Japanese | Height (representative, house) | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Base nuki | 地貫 *jinuki* | just above stone/sill, ~0.5–1 shaku | lowest tie; may be replaced by *ashigatame* |
| Foot-tie nuki | 足固め貫 | at *ashigatame* level | in some regions a *nuki*-sized *ashigatame* |
| Body nuki | 胴貫 *dōnuki* | 1–2 levels in the wall body, spaced ~2–3 shaku | main wall ties and *komai* support |
| Head-height nuki | 内法貫 *uchinori-nuki* | just above the lintel (*kamoi*) | ties the posts at the top of the openings |
| Ceiling nuki | 天井貫 *tenjō-nuki* | near ceiling level | supports ceiling hangers or *komai* top |
| Flying nuki | 飛貫 *hinuki* | high in temple walls, below *kashiranuki* | temples, gates |
| Head nuki | 頭貫 *kashiranuki* | top of posts, under *daito* / *daiwa* | temples, shrines |

- **Load behaviour:**
  - Transmits axial tension and compression between posts.
  - Forms **semi-rigid moment joints** with the posts. When the frame racks, the *nuki* rotates within the post mortise and is pressed into the post at diagonally opposite corners of the mortise, and vice versa. The resulting embedment (めり込み *merikomi*) and friction generate a resisting moment.
  - The moment grows as the rotation grows, with no brittle failure until very large deformations (§11). Initial stiffness is low, especially if there is play in the mortise.
- **Proportions:**
  - Representative house *nuki*: **thickness 7 bu – 1 sun (21–30 mm) × depth 3.5–4 sun (105–120 mm)**. Common modern sizes are 15 × 105 mm (lath-carrying *nuki* in plastered walls, minimal structure), 18–24 × 105 mm, and 27–30 × 105–120 mm for structural *nuki* frames.
  - Structural *nuki* in traditional new-build often use ~1 sun × 4 sun (30 × 120 mm) or larger in 4–5 sun posts.
  - Temple *nuki* are much heavier; *kashiranuki* depth ≈ 0.5–0.7 × column diameter.
  - The mortise (貫穴 *nuki-ana*) in the post: its width equals the *nuki* thickness (a tight fit). Its height exceeds the *nuki* depth by the wedge allowance, typically 3–5 bu (≈ 9–15 mm) total on one side, or split top and bottom depending on the wedging method.
- **Orientation rules:**
  - *Nuki* run continuously through several posts in one direction (通し貫).
  - Perpendicular *nuki* at the same post are set at **different heights**, offset by at least their own depth, or cross with a half-lap inside the post only where specified.
  - The *moto* of a long *nuki* conventionally faces the start of the line.
- **Splices:** *nuki* splices are placed **inside a post mortise** (never mid-bay). Typical splices are 略鎌 *ryaku-kama* (simplified hook), 相欠き *aigaki* (half-lap) with a pin, or 鼻栓 *hanasen* locked splices (→ Ch. 07).
- **Wedging (楔 *kusabi*):** wedges are driven into the gap in the mortise, usually from **both faces** of the post, alternately above and below or both on one side depending on the school. They lock the *nuki* vertically and remove play (→ Ch. 09). Wedges are hardwood (kashi, keyaki) or the same species.
- **Rules:**

**R10-052** — **Must.** *Nuki* splices are located inside posts, and each spliced piece must pass at least through the full post depth.
*Why:* A splice in the open bay has no bending capacity and becomes a hinge.

**R10-053** — **Must.** Two *nuki* from perpendicular directions must not occupy the same height in one post (unless designed as a lapped crossing and checked).
*Why:* Two full-depth mortises at one level cut away most of the post section.

**R10-054** — **Should.** Drive *nuki* wedges from both sides of the post, symmetrically, and re-tighten them (増し締め *mashijime*) after the first drying season (→ Ch. 15).
*Why:* The moment capacity of a *nuki* joint at small rotations depends on the tightness of the fit. Play left by shrinkage produces an initial slip, and the frame racks visibly before the joint engages.

**R10-055** — **Should.** In a structural *nuki* frame, provide at least three *nuki* levels per storey (e.g. *jinuki* or *ashigatame*, *dōnuki*, *uchinori-nuki*), with body *nuki* spaced no more than ~3 shaku (≈ 900 mm).
*Why:* The rack resistance of a *nuki* frame is roughly additive over the number of *nuki* levels. For earth walls, the *nuki* spacing also governs the span of the bamboo lath (→ Ch. 14). The wall-factor recognition of earth walls in Notification 1100 is tied to specified *nuki* arrangements (→ Ch. 17).

**R10-056** — **Should.** The depth of a structural *nuki* should be at least ~0.8–1.0 × the post width, and its thickness at least ~1/5–1/4 of the post width. The post must not be weakened below ~0.6 of its width by the *nuki* mortise (post 120 mm, *nuki* 30 mm → 90 mm remaining shell: 2 × 45 mm).
*Why:* A thin *nuki* crushes and twists. A thick *nuki* in a thin post splits the post at the mortise, which is the typical failure mode of *nuki* frames at large drift.

- **Common errors:**
  - Mortises cut oversize "for easy assembly", then filled with long wedges.
  - Wedges driven from one side only.
  - *Nuki* too thin (15 mm lath *nuki*) relied upon as structure.
  - Never re-wedged after drying.

### 10.2 Clasping beam — *nageshi* (長押)
- **Class:** horizontal member applied to the faces of posts
- **Typical use and levels:**

| Name | Japanese | Position |
|---|---|---|
| Ground *nageshi* | 地長押 *jinageshi* | at base of wall / floor level |
| Waist *nageshi* | 腰長押 *koshinageshi* | at window-sill height (temples) |
| Head-height *nageshi* | 内法長押 *uchinori-nageshi* | on top of the lintel (*kamoi*) — the most common today |
| Ant-wall *nageshi* | 蟻壁長押 *arikabe-nageshi* | below the narrow frieze (蟻壁 *arikabe*) under the ceiling cornice in formal shoin rooms |
| Ceiling *nageshi* | 天井長押 *tenjō-nageshi* | just under the ceiling cornice (*mawaribuchi*) in formal rooms |

- **Load behaviour (historical):**
  - In Nara and Heian buildings (*wayō*), *nageshi* were **structural**. Heavy timbers were fixed on both faces of the posts (or one face), notched over them and nailed with large wrought nails (和釘 *wakugi*, → Ch. 09). They held the posts in line and resisted racking by clamping.
  - After *nuki* came into use (from the late 12th century), the tie function passed to the *nuki*. *Nageshi* became decorative and indicators of room rank.
  - In shoin rooms the *uchinori-nageshi* is a sign of formality; sukiya rooms often omit it or use a thin, naturalised version.
- **Proportions:** representative *kiwari* (→ Ch. 02, Ch. 13):
  - *Uchinori-nageshi*: face height ≈ **0.8–0.9 × the post width**.
  - *Tenjō-nageshi*: ≈ 0.6–0.8 × post width.
  - Thickness at the bottom edge ≈ 0.2–0.3 × post width, with a tapered (splayed) section so the top edge sits closer to the wall.
  - Schools vary.
- **Orientation rules:** the *nageshi* is notched (*nageshi-shakuri*) to clasp the post corners. It is scribed to the post faces. At corners it is mitred with the characteristic 枕捌き *makura-sabaki* or 片捌き returns (→ Ch. 14). It is fixed by nails from the top (hidden) or by hidden *dabo*.
- **Rules:**

**R10-057** — **Should.** In new structural design, treat modern *uchinori-nageshi* as non-structural and do not count on them for racking resistance. In restoration of early buildings, retain structural *nageshi* and their nailing as original structure.
*Why:* Modern *nageshi* are thin applied mouldings. Historical *nageshi* in Nara–Heian buildings were primary structure, and replacing them with decorative ones weakens the frame.

### 10.3 Deep structural lintel — *sashigamoi* (差鴨居)
- **Class:** sashimono / deep horizontal member between posts
- **Typical use:** minka and machiya of the Edo period and later, especially from the 18th century. A deep lintel at head height (*uchinori*) replaces the separate thin *kamoi* lintel plus *nuki*. Its underside carries the grooves for sliding doors. It spans wide openings between the *doma* and rooms and around the main rooms, allowing large open spaces with few posts.
- **Load behaviour:**
  - Carries the loads of the upper wall (小壁 *kokabe*), floor beams and sometimes posts standing on it.
  - Forms **moment-resisting joints** with the posts. These are much stiffer and stronger than *nuki* joints because of the depth of the member and the keyed/pegged joints.
  - A *sashigamoi* frame is the principal lateral system of many Edo minka.
- **Proportions:** depth typically **1–1.5 shaku (300–450 mm)**, occasionally more; width equal to or slightly less than the post width, e.g. 4 sun (120 mm). The bottom face carries two grooves (樋端 *hibata*) for *fusuma* or *shōji*. Representative.
- **Joints:** 雇いほぞ差し + 込み栓 / 鼻栓; 竿車知継ぎ *sao-shachi* through the post to meet the opposite *sashigamoi*; 小根ほぞ + 割楔 (→ Ch. 08/09).
- **Rules:**

**R10-058** — **Should.** A *sashigamoi* frame requires posts of adequate size (≥ 4 sun, preferably 4.5–5 sun) and joints that transfer moment without splitting the post: keyed or pegged, with staggered heights where members meet from two directions.
*Why:* The deep lintel makes the post the weak element. Posts split or break just below the *sashigamoi* joint in strong shaking (a short-column effect; §11.5).

**R10-059** — **Should.** Do not remove a *sashigamoi* when renovating a minka without replacing its function (both gravity and lateral).
*Why:* It is frequently the main lateral resisting element in its direction, even though it looks like a door lintel.

### 10.4 Second-floor girder — *dōsashi* (胴差し)
- **Class:** horizontal perimeter girder
- **Typical use:** at second-floor level on external walls of two-storey houses, framed into *tōshibashira* and carried by the ground-floor *kudabashira*. It carries the second-floor joists or beams and the upper-floor *kudabashira*.
- **Proportions:** 4 sun wide × 6–1 shaku+ deep (120 × 180–360 mm) depending on span and load. Representative.
- **Joints:** to *tōshibashira*: 傾ぎ大入れ短ほぞ *katagi-ōire tanhozo* + hardware (modern), or 竿車知 / 雇い + 込み栓 in traditional work. Splices: 追掛け大栓継ぎ *okkake-daisen-tsugi* or 金輪継ぎ *kanawa-tsugi* near a post; 腰掛け鎌 on a post in simple work (→ Ch. 07).

**R10-060** — **Should.** Splice *dōsashi* over or immediately beside a post (within ~150 mm of support for *koshikake* splices; → Ch. 07), and use high-strength splices (*okkake-daisen*, *kanawa*) where the *dōsashi* carries floor beams near the splice.

### 10.5 Wall plates and beams — *keta* (桁) and *hari* (梁)
- **Keta (桁):** members running in the *ketayuki* direction (parallel to the ridge) at the top of the wall.
  - 軒桁 *nokigeta* (eaves plate): carries rafters (and beams in *kyōro-gumi*).
  - 敷桁 *shikigeta*: a plate laid on beams to carry rafters or upper posts.
  - 妻梁 *tsumabari*: the gable-end beam in the *harima* direction at the top of the gable wall. It is called 梁 but functions as the end plate.
  - In temples: 丸桁 *gangyō* (the round eaves purlin carried on brackets, → Ch. 12).
- **Hari (梁):** members spanning in the *harima* (short) direction.
  - 小屋梁 *koyabari*: roof beam.
  - 床梁 *yukabari*: floor beam, second floor.
  - 二重梁 *nijūbari*: second-tier roof beam.
  - 牛梁 *ushibari* ("ox beam"): a very large longitudinal beam in minka roofs, running in the *ketayuki* direction and supporting the ends or middles of *koyabari* in deep plans.
  - 丸太梁 *marutabari*: log beam, used with natural curvature in minka and machiya roofs; in pine (松 *matsu*) traditionally.
  - 登り梁 *noboribari*: sloping beam following the roof.
  - 繋ぎ梁 *tsunagibari* / 繋虹梁 *tsunagi-kōryō*: short beams tying the *hisashi* posts to the *moya* posts (§13.4).
- **Beam–plate relationship** (→ Ch. 11 for roof consequences):
  - **折置組 *orioki-gumi*:** posts carry the **beams** directly; the plate (*keta*) sits on the beam ends. The beam rests on the post, a direct load path. Roof beams can only be placed on post lines, so the beam spacing equals the post spacing. Older and rural practice.
  - **京呂組 *kyōro-gumi*:** posts carry the **plates**, and beams sit on the plates (joined with *watari-ago* or *kabuto-ari*). Beams can be placed independently of posts, which is more flexible in plan. It became the dominant urban and modern practice. The plate must then be sized for beam loads between posts.

**R10-061** — **Should.** In *kyōro-gumi*, place roof beams over posts wherever possible. Where a beam lands on the plate between posts, size the plate for that point load (and consider a *hanegi*/strut or a post below).
*Why:* A 3.5–4 sun plate spanning a ken with a heavy tiled-roof beam on it deflects and rolls. *Orioki-gumi* avoids the problem by construction.

- **Beam sizing (rules of thumb):** traditional carpenters sized beams by experience. The following ratios are **rules of thumb only** and must be checked by calculation or published span tables (for example, the span tables for timber frame houses published by the Japan Housing and Wood Technology Center, 日本住宅・木材技術センター):

| Member | Rule of thumb (depth *h* vs span *L*) | Representative sizes (width 4 sun / 120 mm) |
|---|---|---|
| Sawn floor beam (2F *yukabari*) | *h* ≈ *L*/10 – *L*/12 | 1 ken span: 150–180 mm; 1.5 ken: 210–240; 2 ken: 270–300; 2.5 ken: 330–360 |
| Sawn roof beam, point loads from struts | *h* ≈ *L*/12 – *L*/15 (light roofs) | 2 ken: 240–270 |
| Plate (*keta*) spanning 1 ken, no beam point load | 4 × 4–4.5 sun | 120 × 120–135 |
| Plate spanning 1 ken with beam point load | *h* ≥ 1.5 × width | 120 × 180–240 |

  - **Log beams (丸太梁)** are sized by their **top-end diameter** (末口 *suekuchi*, → Ch. 03). A representative tradition for tiled roofs:

| Span | *Suekuchi* (top diameter) — representative |
|---|---|
| 1.5 ken (≈ 2.7 m) | 5–6 sun (150–180 mm) |
| 2 ken (≈ 3.6 m) | 6–8 sun (180–240 mm) |
| 2.5 ken (≈ 4.5 m) | 8–9 sun (240–270 mm) |
| 3 ken (≈ 5.5 m) | 9 sun – 1 shaku+ (270–300+ mm) |

**R10-062** — **Must.** Beam sizes from rules of thumb are provisional. Every principal beam in new construction is checked for strength and deflection (long-term deflection including creep) by calculation or recognised span tables (→ Ch. 17).
*Why:* Rules of thumb assume the loads, spans and species of the tradition in which they arose. A modern roof with solar panels and insulation, or a longer span, invalidates them.

**R10-063** — **Should.** Log beams are placed with their **natural camber upward** (むくり *mukuri*, "belly up"). The *moto* (butt) end goes to the side where the greater load or the principal post is.
*Why:* The upward curve counteracts deflection. The butt end has the larger section (→ Ch. 03).

**R10-064** — **Must.** No notch, mortise or hole is cut in the **tension (lower) face of a beam near mid-span**. This is also stated in Enforcement Order Art. 44 (→ Ch. 17). Joists and cross-beams bear on beams via housings in the **upper** part (*watari-ago*, *ari-kake*), and their depth of cut is limited.
*Why:* A notch in the tension zone halves the capacity and starts splits.

### 10.6 Temple head members: *kashiranuki* (頭貫), *daiwa* (台輪), *daito* (大斗)
- ***Kashiranuki*** ties the tops of the columns in each wall line. It is inserted from above into slots cut in the column heads, and splices lie over columns (→ Ch. 07). It carries the intermediate brackets (中備 *nakazonae*) in *wayō*.
- ***Daiwa*** (台輪) is a wide flat plate laid **on top of** the *kashiranuki* and column heads, projecting beyond them. It is characteristic of *zenshūyō* and found in some *wayō* pagodas and gates. It provides a continuous base for the bracket sets placed both on and between columns (詰組 *tsumegumi*, → Ch. 12).
- ***Daito*** (大斗) is the large bearing block on each column head. It is located with a *dabo* and carries the bracket set.

**R10-065** — **Must.** In temple frames, *kashiranuki* ends at corners cross over the corner column with lapped ends (and in many buildings project as decorated ends, 木鼻 *kibana*). The column head must retain enough section around the slot to hold the *daito* dowel.
*Why:* The corner column carries brackets in two directions and the diagonal corner bracket. Over-cutting there is a common cause of corner sag (隅の垂下) in old halls.

## 11. Lateral resistance philosophy

### 11.1 Two families of lateral resistance

| Family | Elements | Behaviour |
|---|---|---|
| **Ductile, deformation-type** (変形性能型) | *nuki* frames, *sashigamoi* frames, *ashigatame* bases, earth walls (as secondary), *ishibadate* sliding | Low initial stiffness; resistance increases with drift; large deformation capacity (~1/30–1/10 rad) before collapse; energy dissipated by embedment, friction and cracking of earth walls |
| **Stiff, strength-type** (強度型 / 耐力壁型) | diagonal braces (*sujikai*), structural panels (plywood, OSB, gypsum), hold-down hardware | High initial stiffness; peak strength at small drift (~1/60–1/30 rad for braced walls); degrades after peak, sometimes suddenly (brace buckling, nail failure) |

Japanese traditional frames belong to the first family. Modern conventional frames (在来軸組構法 *zairai jikugumi*) belong to the second (→ Ch. 17).

### 11.2 *Nuki* frames (貫構造)

The resisting moment in a *nuki* joint comes from:
1. **Embedment (めり込み *merikomi*)** of the *nuki* into the post (compression perpendicular to the post grain) and of the post into the *nuki* at the edges of the mortise. The theory of embedment of timber perpendicular to grain, including the "extended area" contribution of fibres beyond the loaded area, was developed in Japan notably by **Inayama Masahiro** (稲山正弘) in the 1990s. His embedment formulas underlie much later analysis of *nuki* and *sashigamoi* joints. Researchers such as Suzuki Yoshiyuki (鈴木祥之) and colleagues at Kyoto/Ritsumeikan applied them to traditional frames. → Ch. 17 for details and values.
2. **Friction** between wedges, *nuki* and post.
3. **Wedge action**: tightly wedged joints engage at small rotations; loose joints slip first.

Characteristics:
- **Low initial stiffness.** A *nuki* frame racks visibly under moderate loads. It needs earth walls or *sashigamoi* for serviceability stiffness.
- **Increasing resistance with rotation** up to large drifts. Failure typically comes when the post splits at the mortise, or the *nuki* shears or crushes, at drifts of 1/15–1/10 rad and beyond.
- **Pinched hysteresis.** After a large cycle, the crushed zone leaves a gap, and the next cycle slips before re-engaging. Hence re-wedging after earthquakes (→ Ch. 16).
- **Self-centring tendency.** Heavy roof loads on the posts create a restoring P-Δ-like effect together with post-end rotation at the base (*ishibadate* posts rock on their edges). Traditional frames often return close to plumb after strong shaking, as seen in the 2008 E-Defense tests (→ Ch. 17).

### 11.3 Diagonal braces (筋交い *sujikai*)

Diagonal braces were used occasionally in pre-modern Japan, e.g. hidden in some storehouse walls. They were promoted after the 1891 Nōbi earthquake, when the Imperial Earthquake Investigation Committee (震災予防調査会) recommended bracing and hardware for timber buildings. They were regularised in regulations after the 1923 Great Kantō earthquake and made the basis of the required-wall-quantity system (壁量 *kabe-ryō*) of the 1950 Building Standard Law (→ Ch. 17).

Braces are very stiff and strong in compression, but brittle after peak (buckling, end connections pulling out). A braced wall reaches its peak at small drift, while a *nuki* frame at that drift has hardly engaged.

### 11.4 Earth walls (土壁 *tsuchikabe*)

Earth walls on bamboo lath (竹小舞 *take-komai*, → Ch. 14) are the stiffness element of traditional frames:
- Initially stiff, until the plaster cracks, typically at ~1/120–1/60 rad.
- After that, the earth panel works as a compression strut and by friction against the frame, dissipating energy through progressive cracking.
- Capacity is retained to large drifts, with gradual degradation.
- Thickness governs capacity. Enforcement Order notifications (→ Ch. 17) grade earth walls by thickness, from 0.5 up to 1.5 in wall-factor terms. The earth wall is also the fire resistance and thermal mass of the building.

### 11.5 Hanging walls and short-column effects (垂れ壁 *tarekabe*, 小壁 *kokabe*, 腰壁 *koshikabe*)

The small walls above openings (*tarekabe* / *kokabe*, between the *kamoi* or *sashigamoi* and the plate) and below windows (*koshikabe*) stiffen the frame considerably. They force the post to bend over the short clear height of the opening. Results:
- The frame is stiffer and stronger (useful; since 2025, codes count some such walls; → Ch. 17).
- The post takes large bending and shear concentrated at the edge of the *tarekabe*. In full-scale tests of traditional houses, post bending fractures at such locations have been observed (→ Ch. 17).

**R10-066** — **Should.** Where heavy *tarekabe* or *koshikabe* are used, increase post size (≥ 4.5 sun in heavy earth-walled buildings) and avoid mortises and *nuki* holes at the level where the wall ends (the hinge zone).
*Why:* The post fails where the stiff wall ends and the post is cut by a mortise. Both factors together make a predictable fracture point.

### 11.6 Mixing stiff and ductile elements

**R10-067** — **Must.** Do not mix stiff, brittle elements (braces, panels) and ductile, deformation-type elements (*nuki*, earth walls, *sashigamoi*) in one storey and one direction without an analysis that follows the load–drift behaviour of both.
*Why:* At small drift the stiff elements take almost all of the load and fail at ~1/30 rad, before the ductile elements have engaged. The survivors must then carry everything, often with a twisted, eccentric layout. Strength does not simply add up; it adds only at a common drift.

**R10-068** — **Should.** Keep the lateral system of one storey **uniform in type** across the plan: all ductile or all stiff, and balanced in both directions and around the centre of mass (→ §13).
*Why:* Uneven stiffness produces torsion. Traditional houses with an open front (machiya shop front, minka *doma* side) and heavy walls at the rear rotate in earthquakes. The shop fronts of machiya are the classic damage location.

**R10-069** — **Should.** When strengthening an existing traditional building, prefer ductile additions that work at the same drift as the existing frame: extra *nuki*, *sashigamoi*, new earth walls, lattice walls (面格子壁 *mengōshi-kabe*), thick board walls (落とし込み板壁 *otoshikomi-itakabe*), or dampers. Avoid stiff plywood panels unless the whole system is redesigned.

### 11.7 Base as isolation

In the traditional system the *ashigatame* and the stones form a slip layer (§4.3). The frame above is designed to deform largely but survive. The base limits the force in the greatest earthquakes. This combination — deformation capacity above, force limitation below — is the core of the modern design methods for traditional frames (→ Ch. 17).

**R10-070** — **Should.** In an *ishibadate* design, make the upper frame deformable (*nuki*, earth walls) but continuous and tied (*ashigatame*, plates, beams in both directions), so that the frame remains a coherent unit when the base slides.

## 12. Floor framing

Japanese raised floors (床 *yuka*) are framed as a light grid independent of the walls, supported on struts and stones or on the sill. The main difference from Western framing is the combination of heavy sleepers (*ōbiki*) on many short struts with light joists (*neda*). Floor finishes are tatami, boards or *engawa* boards (→ Ch. 14).

### 12.1 Floor sleeper — *ōbiki* (大引)
- **Class:** primary floor bearer
- **Typical use:** ground floor. It spans between sills, *ashigatame* or beams, and is supported at intervals by struts (*yukazuka*).
- **Proportions:** 3.5 sun (105 mm) square minimum; 4 sun (120 mm) common. Spaced **@ 3 shaku (≈ 910 mm)**. Supported by *yukazuka* @ 3 shaku along its length. Representative.
- **Joints:** to the sill or *ashigatame*: 大入れ蟻掛け *ōire-arikake*, or seated on a housing (腰掛け) plus nail/clamp. Splices: 腰掛け蟻継ぎ **over a strut** (→ Ch. 07).

**R10-071** — **Should.** Run *ōbiki* in the direction that gives the shortest span between walls. Splice them only over a *yukazuka* or a support, and orient them so that the *neda* run perpendicular.

### 12.2 Floor strut — *yukazuka* (床束) on *tsukaishi* (束石)
- **Proportions:** 3–3.5 sun (90–105 mm) square in wood, typically hinoki or treated sugi. Adjustable steel or plastic *tsuka* (鋼製束, プラ束) are common in modern houses. Stones (*tsukaishi*) are about 6 sun – 1 shaku (180–300 mm) square/round.
- **Joints:** top: 目違いほぞ or 短ほぞ into the *ōbiki* + かすがい *kasugai* (cramp) or nailed plate. Bottom: scribed to the stone (§5), or on a flat precast *tsukaishi*.
- **Ties:** *negarami* (§6.3).

**R10-072** — **Should.** Set floor struts vertical and scribed or seated firmly, with a slight positive bearing (no gap). Wedge-adjust after the floor is loaded if they are adjustable.
*Why:* A strut with a gap lets the floor sag and "bounce", and the tatami edges open.

### 12.3 Joists — *neda* (根太)
- **Proportions and spacing** (representative, traditional/pre-2000s practice):

| Floor finish | *Neda* section | Spacing |
|---|---|---|
| Tatami (畳敷き) | 1.5 × 1.5 sun (45 × 45 mm) | @ 1.5 shaku (≈ 455 mm) |
| Board floor (板張り), 15 mm boards | 1.5 × 1.5–2 sun (45 × 45–60 mm) | @ 1 shaku (≈ 303 mm) |
| Board floor, thick boards (≥ 1 sun) | 1.5 × 2 sun | @ 1.5 shaku |
| *Engawa* | stout *neda* or none (*engawa* boards span between *ōbiki*-like *engawa* beams) | — |

- **Height differential:** tatami are ~5–6 cm thick (traditional straw core), so the *neda* under tatami rooms are set **lower** than those under board floors by the tatami thickness minus the board thickness. This keeps finished floors flush (→ Ch. 14).
- **Wall-side joists:**
  - 根太掛け *nedagake*: a ledger (e.g. 24–30 × 90–105 mm) nailed or let into the posts along a wall, carrying the ends of *neda* that run perpendicular to the wall.
  - 際根太 *kiwaneda*: the joist along a wall parallel to the joists, carrying board ends.
- **Laid-flat joists** — 転ばし根太 *korobashi-neda*: joists laid directly on sleepers or a concrete slab without *ōbiki*/*tsuka*; used in *doma*-level rooms, modern slabs and renovations.
- **Joints:** *neda* sit on the *ōbiki* with a shallow housing (渡りあご or 大入れ, small), nailed. Splices are made on an *ōbiki* with a butt joint (突付け) or scarf, staggered joist to joist.

**R10-073** — **Should.** Stagger *neda* splices so that no two adjacent joists are spliced over the same *ōbiki*.

**R10-074** — **Should.** Set tatami-area *neda* lower by the tatami thickness minus the board thickness. Confirm the actual tatami thickness with the tatami maker before cutting, since modern thin tatami (15–30 mm) change the calculation.

### 12.4 Second-floor framing
- **Floor beams** (床梁 *yukabari*) at 1 ken (6 shaku) spacing span between *dōsashi* and internal beams. Sizes as in §10.5.
- **Joists** — representative traditional sizes 1.5 × 3.5 sun (45 × 105 mm) @ 1 shaku (303 mm) to 1.5 shaku (455 mm), spanning 1 ken between beams. They are laid on the beams with *watari-ago* (lapped seating) or dropped in between beams with housings (落とし込み *otoshikomi*).
- In machiya and minka, the second floor was often a low storage or sleeping loft (厨子二階 *tsushi-nikai*) with thick floorboards laid on beams or joists. The ceiling of the ground floor was then the exposed underside of these boards and joists (根太天井 *neda-tenjō*).
- **Diaphragm:** traditional upper floors resist in-plane shear through the *watari-ago* grid and thick boards. Modern practice uses structural plywood (24–28 mm, 根太レス *nedaless*) or horizontal angle braces (火打梁 *hiuchibari*) at corners of beam grids (→ Ch. 17).

**R10-075** — **Should.** In a traditional two-storey frame, provide an in-plane-stiff second floor and roof base (thick boards on a lapped beam grid, *hiuchi* braces, or panels compatible with the design concept).
*Why:* The walls can only share lateral load in proportion to their capacity if the floor above them keeps its shape. A flexible floor lets each wall line fend for itself.

**R10-076** — **Should.** Lay joists over beams with *watari-ago* (lapped seat) rather than hanging them in shallow housings where the floor is to act as a diaphragm; → Ch. 08.
*Why:* *Watari-ago* interlocks the grid in both directions.

## 13. Structural layout rules: grid, alignment, *moya–hisashi*

### 13.1 Grid regularity

**R10-077** — **Should.** Lay out all posts on the building grid (1 ken with half-ken subdivisions, → Ch. 02). Principal wall lines should be continuous from one side of the building to the other in both directions.
*Why:* Continuous wall lines give continuous *nuki* and plates, and allow the roof and floors to deliver load to a small number of strong frames. Broken lines create load transfers through beams.

**R10-078** — **Should.** Distribute lateral resisting walls evenly in both directions and around the perimeter. At minimum they should be balanced between the two halves of the plan in each direction. The modern code's quarter-division method (四分割法, → Ch. 17) formalises this.
*Why:* Torsion (§11.6).

### 13.2 Vertical alignment (直下率 *chokka-ritsu*)

*Chokka-ritsu* ("direct-below ratio") is the proportion of upper-storey posts (or walls) that have a post (or wall) directly below them.

**R10-079** — **Should.** Align second-floor posts with ground-floor posts wherever possible. A frequently quoted design guideline is post alignment ≥ 50% and braced-wall alignment ≥ 60%. These are guideline values from practice and design software, not legal requirements; representative.
*Why:* Every unaligned post puts a point load on a beam, requiring deeper beams, causing deflection and damage to finishes, and interrupting the lateral load path. Traditional two-storey machiya and minka achieved near-total alignment because the upper storey was simply the upper part of the same grid.

**R10-080** — **Must.** An upper-storey post that does not align with a lower post must stand on a beam sized for that point load (with the load path traced to the ground, R10-001). It must not stand on a joist, *kamoi* or thin plate.

### 13.3 Openings, *sashigamoi* and corners

**R10-081** — **Should.** Keep openings away from building corners. Leave at least a wall of ~1 ken (or 半間 at the very least) on each side of each external corner.
*Why:* Corners carry the largest overturning uplift. A corner with openings on both faces has neither wall nor tie. Historic machiya shop fronts that are open to the corner rely on massive *sashigamoi* or *ōdo* beams and are a known weak point.

**R10-082** — **Should.** Span wide openings (> 1 ken) with a deep *sashigamoi* or a beam sized for the upper load, not with a thin *kamoi*. Stand the adjacent posts on good stones and tie them with *ashigatame*.

**R10-083** — **Should.** Place *tōshibashira* at external corners (R10-043) and where main girder lines cross. Do not place *tōshibashira* where it forces four deep members into one post at one level; use a larger post or offset the girders instead.

### 13.4 The *moya–hisashi* system (身舎・庇)

Classical Japanese architecture (temples, shrines, palaces, shinden) is organised in a core-and-aisle system:
- **身舎 *moya*** — the core. A rectangle of posts spanned in the short direction by the principal beams (大梁 / 虹梁 *kōryō*). Its beam span is limited by available timber; spans of about 2 ken (roughly 4–6 m in temple units) are typical of early halls. Larger interiors are made by adding aisles, not by lengthening the beams.
- **庇 *hisashi*** — the aisle surrounding the core on one to four sides. It is formed by a second row of lower posts, connected to the *moya* posts by tie beams (繋ぎ虹梁 *tsunagi-kōryō*, 繋梁) and roofed by rafters running from the *moya* plate down over the *hisashi* plate.
- **孫庇 *magobisashi*** — a further outer aisle beyond the *hisashi*.
- The **間面記法 *kenmen kihō*** ("bays-and-sides notation"), used in Heian-period and later records, describes a building by the number of *moya* bays in the long direction and the number of sides with *hisashi*: e.g. 三間四面 *sangen shimen* = a *moya* of three bays with *hisashi* on all four sides. The notation does not state the *moya* depth, which is conventionally two bays.
- In **minka** the equivalent pair is **上屋 *jōya*** (the tall core with the principal beams and the main roof frame) and **下屋 *geya*** (lower lean-to aisles around it, with their own lower posts, 下屋柱). Many regional minka types can be read as a *jōya* with *geya* on two, three or four sides. The later "sashimono" minka of the 18th–19th century removed many *jōya* posts by using *sashigamoi* and *ushibari*.

- **Load behaviour:**
  - The *moya* frame is tall and carries the main roof.
  - The *hisashi* posts are shorter and carry the lower roof slope. Via tie beams they brace the *moya* posts at mid-height against lateral movement.
  - The two parts have different heights and stiffness, so earthquake behaviour depends on how well they are tied.

**R10-084** — **Should.** In a *moya–hisashi* building, tie every *hisashi* post to its corresponding *moya* post with a tie beam (*tsunagibari*/*tsunagi-kōryō*) or *nuki* at plate level of the *hisashi*. Align the *hisashi* posts with the *moya* posts on the same grid lines.
*Why:* The tie beam carries the *hisashi* roof load, braces the tall *moya* posts at mid-height, and makes the two frames act together. An unaligned *hisashi* post cannot be tied.

**R10-085** — **Should.** Keep the *hisashi* depth (from *moya* post to *hisashi* post) within what the rafters can span in one length, typically ≤ 1–1.5 ken in houses and one bay in temples. Deeper aisles require an intermediate purlin support.

**R10-086** — **May / Variant.** In minka the *geya* posts may be omitted at the *doma* or *engawa* side by using cantilevered beams (*hanegi*, 出し梁 *dashibari*) or *sashigamoi* spanning to the *jōya*. This is a regional technique and must be sized for the cantilever.

### 13.5 Storey height and member hierarchy

**R10-087** — **Should.** Keep a clear hierarchy (→ Ch. 06): stones → base ties → posts → *nuki*/*sashigamoi* → plates/girders → beams → roof. A lower-rank member should never carry a higher-rank member's load without being upgraded in section and joint.
*Why:* Traditional sizing is proportional and relative. The eye and the saw work within this hierarchy, and deviations are where errors hide.

**R10-088** — **Should.** Use a story pole (*shakuzue*/*bakabō*, → Ch. 04) and a sectional height drawing (矩計 *kanabakari*, → Ch. 02) to fix all horizontal member heights (stone top, *ashigatame*, floor, *nuki*, *kamoi*, *nageshi*, *dōsashi*, plate, beam) before cutting. Check that no two mortises in one post conflict.
