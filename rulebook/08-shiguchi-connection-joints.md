# Chapter 08 — Catalogue of Connection Joints (仕口 / shiguchi)

**Scope.** This chapter lists the **connection joints** (仕口, *shiguchi*), meaning the joints where two or more members meet at an angle, usually 90°. The splices that lengthen a member along its axis (継手, *tsugite*) are in → Ch. 07. The general principles of joinery (male/female, the hierarchy of members, tolerances, *kigoroshi* 木殺し compression fitting, joint placement) are in → Ch. 06, and this chapter assumes them. Pins, wedges, keys and hardware get only a short mention in each entry here. Their full treatment is in → Ch. 09. The frame as a whole (sill, posts, *nuki*, beams, bracing philosophy) is in → Ch. 10, roof framing in → Ch. 11, bracket sets in → Ch. 12, and board joints of fittings in → Ch. 14.

The catalogue covers both **traditional hand-cut practice** (手刻み, *tekizami*) and, where it matters, **the modern post-and-beam system** (在来軸組構法, *zairai jikugumi kōhō*) with its machine-cut (プレカット, *purekatto*) joints. Dimensions are **representative**. They vary by school, region, period and building type. Where a figure is uncertain it is marked "c." or "approx." or given as a range.

## 1. Conventions, anatomy and general rules

### 1.1 Symbols and member sizes used in this chapter

| Symbol | Meaning |
|---|---|
| *b* | width of the tenoned (inserted) member, measured across the face that the tenon's thickness is measured on |
| *h* | depth (成, *sei*) of a horizontal member |
| *B* | width of the receiving (mortised) member, e.g. the post face or the sill width |
| *H* | depth of the receiving horizontal member |
| *t* | tenon thickness (枘厚, *hozo-atsu*) |
| *w* | tenon width (枘幅, *hozo-haba*) |
| *l* | tenon length (枘長さ, *hozo-nagasa*) |

The three reference post sizes are **3.5 sun (≈106 mm, sold as 105 mm)**, **4 sun (≈121 mm, sold as 120 mm)** and **5 sun (≈152 mm, sold as 150 mm)**. The 3.5-sun post is the standard of the post-war house. The 4-sun post is the standard of good traditional minka and machiya work and of "traditional-method" (伝統構法, *dentō kōhō*) new builds. Temples, shrines and large minka use 5 sun and above (→ Ch. 10, → Ch. 12).

### 1.2 Anatomy of a tenon joint

```
          receiving member (女木 megi)
   ┌───────────────────────────────────┐
   │        ┌─────┐  ← mortise (枘穴 hozo-ana)
   │        │     │     depth = l + 2–5 mm clearance at the bottom
   │        │     │
   └────────┤     ├────────────────────┘
  shoulder →│  t  │← tenon (枘 hozo), thickness t
 (胴付き    ═╧═════╧═   ← shoulder line: the bearing face
  dōtsuki)  │       │
            │ tenoned member (男木 ogi)  width b
            │       │
```

- **Shoulder (胴付き, *dōtsuki*)**: the end face of the tenoned member around the tenon. **The shoulder is what carries compression**, not the end of the tenon.
- **Haunch / step (腰, *koshi*)**: a reduced step at the root of the tenon (see 小根枘 *kone-hozo*, §2.6). The term 腰付き (*koshi-tsuki*) is used for a tenon or housing whose root carries such a step or seat.
- **Stub alignment tongue (目違い, *mechigai*)**: a small projection or recess, a few mm to c. 1 sun, that stops two faces from sliding relative to each other or twisting.
- **Relish (in Japanese usually just 木口までの距離 or 端あき, *hashi-aki*)**: the length of solid wood between a mortise or pin hole and the end grain of the member. Too little relish lets the end shear out.

### 1.3 Male and female in shiguchi
In a shiguchi the **tenoned or inserted member** is the male piece (男木, *ogi*). The **receiving member**, the one that stays continuous and is cut into, is the female piece (女木, *megi*). The receiving member is almost always the higher-ranking member in the hierarchy of → Ch. 06 §(hierarchy): post over tie, sill over post, beam-carrying girder over the beam, and so on. At a post, however, the *post* is usually the continuous member, and horizontal members are tenoned into it. The exception is the post top and post foot, where the post carries the tenon and the horizontal member is mortised.

### 1.4 General rules for all shiguchi

**R08-001** — (Must) The **continuous member is the higher-ranking member**, and the member that is cut to a tenon, dovetail or cog is the lower-ranking one. Where both rank equally (e.g. two beams crossing), the one that carries the other is the continuous one.
*Why:* the more important member keeps its full section. The load path goes from the lesser member into the greater (→ Ch. 06).

**R08-002** — (Must) **The long axis of every mortise runs parallel to the grain of the mortised member.** In a sill, beam or keta, the tenon's width *w* lies along the member's length. In a post, the mortise for a horizontal member is taller than it is wide.
*Why:* a mortise cut with its long side across the grain severs many fibres and splits the member along the grain when the tenon swells or a wedge is driven.

**R08-003** — (Should) Tenon thickness *t* ≈ **1/3 of the width of the member being mortised**, with a customary range of 1/4 to 1/3. Never make it more than about 2/5 of that width. For a 3.5-sun (105 mm) post or sill this gives 30 mm (1 sun). For 4 sun it gives 30–40 mm, and for 5 sun 36–50 mm.
*Why:* this is the balance between the strength of the tenon (bending and shear) and the strength of the two cheeks of the mortise that remain.

**R08-004** — (Must) Mark every shiguchi from the **reference lines**: the centreline (芯墨, *shinzumi*) and the level line (陸墨, *rokuzumi*). Do not mark from the arrises of the timber. The marking symbols and the i-ro-ha grid are in → Ch. 02.
*Why:* timbers are not perfectly square, straight or equally thick. The frame fits only if every joint refers to the same centrelines.

**R08-005** — (Must) A **mortise is cut 2–5 mm deeper** than the tenon is long (ほぞ穴の逃げ). A blind tenon must never bottom out before its shoulder closes.
*Why:* the shoulder has to bear. A tenon that bottoms carries the load on a small end-grain area and leaves a gap at the visible shoulder that shrinkage then widens.

**R08-006** — (Should) Shoulders are cut dead square at their visible arris and **very slightly relieved inward** (胴付きをさらう). The relief is a fraction of a millimetre to c. 1 mm over the inner part, so that the visible line closes tight. Do not over-relieve a *bearing* shoulder, such as a post foot, because the bearing area there must remain.

**R08-007** — (Should) **Before any joint is cut, compare the rank of the members, the relish and the section loss.** Where a mortise would leave less than c. 1 sun (30 mm) of wood to the end grain of the receiving member, choose a joint that is designed for member ends: fan tenon, stepped tenon, dovetail housing, or a projecting "horn" (角, *tsuno*) that is cut off after assembly.

**R08-008** — (Must) **Dry-assemble (仮組み, *karigumi*) all complex shiguchi before tatemae**: four-way post junctions, corner keta, bracket sets. Mark the pairs with the grid symbols (→ Ch. 02, → Ch. 15).

**R08-009** — (Should) Joints are made "tight but not forced". The tolerance for a well-seasoned member is about 0–0.5 mm on tenon thickness, fitted with *kigoroshi* (木殺し) on the male piece where the species allows it (→ Ch. 06). On a green member, allow for shrinkage across the grain. **Do not compress hard species** (keyaki, kashi) the way you compress hinoki or sugi.

**R08-010** — (Must) The shiguchi must be **located so that no two major joints overlap in the same short length of a member** unless they have been deliberately designed together, as in §10.
*Why:* the net section of the receiving member is the critical section. Two mortises at the same height through a post can cut away more than half of it.

## 2. Group A — Tenons and mortises (枘 hozo / 枘穴 hozo-ana)

The tenon (枘 or ほぞ, *hozo*; sometimes written 臍) is the basic locating element of Japanese framing. In most Japanese frames **the tenon is primarily a locating and anti-sliding device.** Pull-out resistance comes from pins (込み栓), wedges (楔), keys (車知), from the weight of the structure above, or in modern work from metal. The capacity of an unpinned tenon to resist pull-out is negligible.

### 2.1 Short tenon — 短枘 (tan-hozo)
- **Class:** hozo (blind stub tenon)
- **Typical use:** the foot of ordinary posts into the sill (柱脚, *chūkyaku*, into 土台 *dodai*). Post tops into keta and beams (柱頭, *chūtō*). Short struts (束, *tsuka*) into beams, and floor struts (床束) onto the post-foot beam (大引, *ōbiki*). Studs (間柱, *mabashira*).
- **Load behaviour:** resists horizontal shear (sliding of the post foot) and locates the member. It gives **practically no resistance to withdrawal**, so uplift must be resisted by other means. It transfers compression through the shoulder.
- **Proportions:**
  - thickness *t* ≈ 1/3 *B* (R08-003): 30 mm for a 105 post
  - width *w* ≈ *b* − 15 to 30 mm, or c. 0.8–0.9 *b*. For a 105 post the tenon is usually 90 mm wide in modern practice. In older hand-cut work *w* is often less, c. 0.6–0.7 *b*.
  - length *l* ≈ 50–60 mm (c. 1.5–2 sun), i.e. roughly **1/2 of the receiving member's depth**. Short tenons for tsuka into beams are c. 40–60 mm.
  - the representative modern tenon for a 105 post into a 105 sill is **30 × 90 × 60 mm**
- **Orientation rules:** at a post foot the width *w* lies along the sill (R08-002). At a post top the width *w* lies along the keta or beam that receives it. Where the post stands under the *junction* of two members, the tenon is aligned with the member that is continuous over the post.
- **Marking:** transfer the post centrelines (two faces) to the end. Mark *t* symmetric about the centreline and *w* symmetric about the other centreline. Mark the shoulder line from the height reference on the story pole (→ Ch. 04 bakabō). On the sill, the mortise is laid out from the sill centreline and the grid-line cross mark.
- **Cutting sequence:** (1) Cut the post to length at the shoulder line, all four faces. (2) Saw the tenon cheeks with a rip saw (縦挽き). (3) Saw the shoulders with a crosscut saw (横挽き) to the cheek lines. (4) Chamfer the tenon end slightly (面取り) to ease entry. (5) Bore out and chop the mortise (角のみ *kakunomi* or chisel) 2–5 mm deeper than *l*. Pare the walls of the mortise so that they are dead straight.
- **Assembly:** drop the post onto the sill mortise and seat the shoulder with a mallet (掛矢, *kakeya*). The post is then held vertical by *nuki*, walls or braces.
- **Rules:**
  **R08-011** — (Must) A short tenon alone is **not an uplift connection**. Where uplift or overturning can occur (corner posts, posts at wall ends, posts under braces or walls that resist racking), use a long tenon with komisen (§2.2), a stepped or wedged tenon, or approved metal.
  *Why:* withdrawal of an unpinned tenon is resisted only by friction.
  **R08-012** — (Should) The foot of a post carrying heavy load bears on its **full shoulder**. A mortise in the sill must not be so wide that the remaining sill cheeks (c. 1/3 *B* each) crush under the shoulder.
- **Common errors:** tenon too long, so it bottoms (R08-005). Tenon width across the sill grain, which splits the sill. Tenon roots left undercut by overcutting the saw at the shoulder. Mortises bored out of plumb, which makes the post lean.
- **Variants:** tenon-less feet on stone (see §2.16, ishibadate → Ch. 10). Dowel-located foot (太枘 *dabo*) on a stone plinth.

### 2.2 Long tenon with draw-pin — 長枘差し込み栓打ち (naga-hozo-sashi komisen-uchi)
- **Class:** hozo + pin (込み栓, *komisen*; → Ch. 09 §1)
- **Typical use:** post feet into sills and post tops into beams or keta wherever **withdrawal must be resisted**. Tsuka (束) into beams at the ends of roof frames. Tie beams into posts (in that use the tenon is horizontal). It is the standard traditional alternative to post-to-sill hold-down hardware. In the modern Japanese framework (the notification that grades post-end connections, → Ch. 17) the long tenon with komisen is listed as one of the recognised connections, with a capacity comparable to a light L-shaped strap. This is approximately stated; check the current tables.
- **Load behaviour:** resists **withdrawal (tension)** through the pin in shear and bending and through the bearing of the pin on the tenon and the mortise cheeks. It also resists horizontal shear and a limited moment. Failure modes are: the pin bends or crushes, the tenon end splits out beyond the pin (insufficient relish), and the mortise cheeks split.
- **Proportions:**
  - *t* ≈ 1/3 *B*: 30 mm (105), 30–36 mm (120), 36–45 mm (150)
  - *w* ≈ *b* − 15 to 30 mm
  - *l* ≈ **0.8–1.0 × *H*** of the receiving member, or through. A representative value is 90 mm into a 105 sill, 105–120 into a 120 sill, and 120–150 into a 150 member. Traditional texts often give "c. 3 sun or more" for a 3.5-sun frame.
  - **komisen**: square hardwood pin of 15 mm (5 bu) square for 105–120 members and 18 mm (6 bu) for 120–150 members. Round pins of 15–18 mm diameter are also used. The pin centre lies c. 40–50 mm from the shoulder, so roughly at mid-depth of the receiving member. It must leave **at least 2–2.5 pin widths of wood between the pin hole and the tenon end** (relish).
  - **draw (引き, *hiki*)**: the hole in the tenon is offset **toward the shoulder by c. 1–2 mm**. Some carpenters use up to c. 3 mm in softwood (→ R09-004).
- **Orientation rules:** *w* along the receiving member (R08-002). The pin is driven **across the width of the receiving member**, through both cheeks and the tenon, perpendicular to *w*. On a sill the pin is driven from the face that will be visible or accessible after the walls are built, usually the inside. Where a moto/sue direction applies, the pin is driven so its larger end ends up on the face from which it can be re-driven.
- **Marking:** mark the pin centre on the receiving member first, measured from the shoulder line (the sill top). Then assemble dry, mark through, withdraw, and offset the tenon hole by the draw. Alternatively set the offset by measurement on both pieces.
- **Cutting sequence:** (1) Cut the tenon as for tan-hozo but longer. (2) Chop the mortise. (3) Bore or chop the pin hole through the receiving member, square or round. (4) Bore the tenon hole with the draw. (5) Chamfer the tenon hole's edge on the shoulder side lightly so the pin tip is not stopped dead.
- **Assembly:** seat the tenon and check plumb. Drive the pin, tapered point first, from one face. The draw pulls the shoulder down tight. Trim the pin flush or leave it a few mm proud where re-driving (増し締め) is intended.
- **Rules:**
  **R08-013** — (Must) The **relish beyond the pin** in the tenon must be at least c. 2 × the pin width (≥ 30–40 mm for a 15 mm pin).
  *Why:* the draw puts the tenon end under tension toward the shoulder. With too little wood beyond the hole, the pin shears out the end ("鼻が飛ぶ").
  **R08-014** — (Must) The **draw must not exceed what the pin and wood can take**, about 1–2 mm (maximum c. 3 mm in soft species). Excessive draw splits the tenon or breaks the pin while it is driven.
  **R08-015** — (Should) Pins are of a **harder species than the tenon**: kashi (樫, evergreen oak) is the usual one. Keyaki, kuri or seasoned hinoki are used for light work. Pin grain runs straight along the pin; a riven (割り) pin is preferred.
  **R08-016** — (Should) On a 3.5-sun sill, keep the long tenon's end at least c. 10–15 mm short of the sill bottom, **or take it right through**. Do not leave a paper-thin floor of wood.
- **Common errors:** pin placed too near the tenon end. No draw, so the joint stays loose. Draw made in the wrong direction, away from the shoulder. The pin hole in the tenon bored oversize "to make it easier". Pins of softwood.
- **Variants:** long tenon with **two pins** in deep members (pins staggered, c. 3 × pin width apart). **Through long tenon with wedges** (§2.8). **Long tenon with a nose pin** (§2.9).

### 2.3 Flat (plain) tenon — 平枘 (hira-hozo)
- **Class:** hozo
- **Typical use:** "Hira-hozo" is the generic name for the ordinary thin, wide rectangular tenon (as opposed to square, fan or stepped tenons). Most tan-hozo and naga-hozo are hira-hozo. The term is also used for the tenons of horizontal members entering posts: head ties (頭貫 in temples, though these are often slotted, see §2.12), sashimono, and lintels (鴨居 *kamoi*) into posts.
- **Load behaviour:** as tan-hozo or naga-hozo depending on its length and pinning.
- **Proportions:** thickness ≈ 1/3 of the receiving width (R08-003). Width up to *h* − 2 × c. 10–15 mm for a horizontal member, i.e. full depth less small shoulders top and bottom. On deep horizontal members, reduce *w* to about 0.6–0.7 *h*, or use a stepped tenon (§2.6), to limit the mortise height in the post.
- **Orientation:** for a horizontal member entering a post the tenon stands on edge (width vertical), so that the mortise is long in the direction of the post's grain (R08-002).
- **Rules:**
  **R08-017** — (Should) For horizontal members entering posts, leave **top and bottom shoulders** of at least c. 5 bu (15 mm) on the tenon, or a seat (腰掛け) below it. A tenon of full member depth leaves no shoulder to hide the mortise and no seat to carry shear.
- **Variants:** square tenon (角ほぞ) for small struts. Short or long flat tenon.

### 2.4 Fan tenon — 扇枘 (ōgi-hozo)
- **Class:** hozo (shaped)
- **Typical use:** the feet of **corner posts (隅柱, *sumibashira*)** and of posts standing near the end of a sill, where the sills meet at a corner lap and the mortise lies close to end grain.
- **Load behaviour:** as a short or long tenon. The flared or raked form reduces the risk of the end grain of the sill splitting out and gives some resistance to the post being pulled toward the outer corner.
- **Proportions:** thickness as tan-hozo (30 mm in 105 work). In elevation the tenon is **trapezoidal ("fan-shaped")**. One or both edges are raked so the tenon changes width between root and tip. The rake is typically cut on the side facing the sill end, so that more wood remains between the mortise and the end grain at depth. The exact form varies between sources: some show it widening toward the tip, like an open fan (a dovetail-like lock), others show it narrowing. Check local usage. Length c. 50–60 mm (short) or longer when pinned.
- **Orientation:** the rake is on the edge nearest the sill end grain.
- **Rules:**
  **R08-018** — (Should) Where a post-foot mortise would come within c. 1 sun (30 mm) of a sill end or corner lap, use a fan tenon, a stepped tenon (§2.6) or a horned sill end (角を残す). Do not use a plain full-width tenon.
- **Common errors:** the fan cut on the wrong side. The mortise cut square and the tenon fan-shaped, which leaves voids.
- **Variants:** 扇ほぞ with komisen. The corner post with 小根ほぞ into the sill corner (§5.6).

### 2.5 Twin tenon — 二枚枘 (nimai-hozo)
- **Class:** hozo (double)
- **Typical use:** **wide or deep members** where one tenon of adequate width would leave too little cheek, or would be weak in racking: deep sashigamoi, wide thresholds (敷居), deep floor beams into posts, and the feet of broad plank posts. It is also used where the receiving member is thin across its grain and a single thick tenon would split it.
- **Load behaviour:** better resistance to twisting and racking than a single tenon of the same total area. Two thin cheeks distribute the bearing.
- **Proportions:** each tenon ≈ 1/5–1/4 *B* thick, with a gap between them ≥ the tenon thickness. Alternatively, where the twin tenons are *stacked* along the member's depth, each is c. 1/3 *h* wide with a central gap of c. 1/3 *h*.
- **Orientation:** twin tenons side by side (across the width) are used where the receiving member is wide. Stacked (one above the other) tenons are used in deep horizontal members entering posts. In both cases each mortise follows R08-002.
- **Rules:**
  **R08-019** — (Should) With stacked twin tenons in a deep horizontal member, **pin or wedge the lower tenon** and let the upper one act as a guide, or pin both. Do not leave both unpinned where the member can be pulled.
- **Variants:** 二段ほぞ (two-step). *Kasane-hozo* (§2.7) is a different thing.

### 2.6 Stepped (haunched) tenon — 小根枘 (kone-hozo)
- **Class:** hozo (stepped)
- **Typical use:** the tenon of a **horizontal member entering a post through a mortise whose height must be limited**, especially sashigamoi, sashimono and nuki-like ties, often as a **through tenon wedged on the far face** (小根ほぞ差し割楔締め, *kone-hozo-sashi wari-kusabi-jime*). The feet of posts at **sill corners**, with a square or dovetail-shaped kone-hozo that pulls the post inward. Sill-to-sill T and L junctions.
- **Load behaviour:** the large root part (大根, *ōne*) resists twisting and shear close to the shoulder. The small tip (小根, *kone*) passes further or right through, giving withdrawal resistance when wedged or pinned while needing only a small hole on the far side.
- **Anatomy:** a flat tenon cut in **two steps**. The thick or wide root portion is the **ōne** (大根). The narrower tip is the **kone** (小根). The step between them is the **koshi** (腰).
- **Proportions:** representative for a 4-sun post receiving a sashigamoi: ōne height c. 0.6–0.8 *h*, entering c. 1/3–1/2 of the post width. Kone height c. 1/2 of the ōne height (e.g. 45–60 mm), running through the post. Thickness 1/3 *B* throughout.
- **Orientation:** the step is placed so that the **kone lies on the side away from the member's load**, usually the upper side of a sashigamoi. The larger bearing is then kept low where shear is transmitted.
- **Cutting sequence:** cut the full-width tenon first, then saw off the step. On the post, chop the kone mortise through and the ōne mortise to its stop depth, working from both faces for the through part.
- **Assembly:** insert, drive the wedges into the kone from the far side (§2.8), and trim.
- **Rules:**
  **R08-020** — (Should) Where a horizontal member enters a post through a mortise, reduce the **far-side hole with a stepped tenon**. Do not pass the full-depth tenon through.
  *Why:* the section loss in the post is greatest at the point of a through mortise. The stepped tenon keeps the through part small.
- **Common errors:** the koshi step placed on the loaded (lower) side. Wedges across the grain of the post (see R08-022).
- **Variants:** 蟻形小根ほぞ (dovetail-shaped kone) at sill corners. Kone-hozo with komisen instead of wedges.

### 2.7 Double tenon through a keta — 重枘 (kasane-hozo)
- **Class:** hozo (two-stage, passing through one member into another)
- **Typical use:** **orioki-gumi (折置組)**, where the post top carries the keta (軒桁 *nokigeta* or 敷桁 *shikigeta*) and the roof beam (小屋梁 *koyabari*) sits on the keta directly over the post. The post's tenon passes **through the keta and into the beam**. It is also used in some temple frames where a post passes through a tie beam into a bearing block or daiwa.
- **Load behaviour:** locks three members (post, keta, beam) on one vertical axis. It resists sliding of both horizontal members relative to the post.
- **Proportions:** the lower stage, inside the keta, has the full tenon section (e.g. 30 × 90 for a 105 frame, larger for 4-sun). The upper stage, entering the beam, is smaller, c. 2/3 the width, with a length of c. 30–50 mm. The keta mortise goes right through. The step between the stages sits at or slightly below the keta top.
- **Orientation:** the tenon width lies along the keta (R08-002). The upper stage may be turned to lie along the beam where it enters the beam, but this is uncommon and must be checked against the keta mortise.
- **Rules:**
  **R08-021** — (Should) In orioki-gumi, place the **keta splice (→ Ch. 07) off the post**, or if it must be over the post, cut the splice so that the kasane-hozo passes through the *male* piece's solid part, not through the joint plane.
- **Variants:** the orioki post top with **watari-ago beam seating and kasane-hozo** (see §5.3).

### 2.8 Wedged through-tenon — 通し枘割楔締め (tōshi-hozo wari-kusabi-jime)
- **Class:** hozo + wedge (割楔, *wari-kusabi*; → Ch. 09 §4)
- **Typical use:** horizontal members through posts (sashigamoi, tie beams 繋ぎ梁, *tsunagi-bari*, floor ties 足固め *ashigatame*). Sill junctions. Tsuka through tie members. Furniture and fittings.
- **Load behaviour:** strong in **withdrawal** because the flared tenon locks in a flared mortise. It is tight in racking. It cannot be taken apart without cutting out the wedges.
- **Proportions:** two wedge saw-kerfs (割り, *wari*) in the tenon, each c. 1/4–1/6 of *w* in from the tenon edges, running to c. 2/3–3/4 of the tenon length. For small work, one central kerf. Each end of the mortise on the exit side is flared (蟻形に開く) by c. 2–5 mm over its length (c. 1:10). Wedge thickness at the head is c. 3–6 mm more than the kerf. Wedge taper is c. 1:8 to 1:15 (representative; → Ch. 09).
- **Orientation rules:**
  **R08-022** — (Must) Wedges in a through-tenon are driven so that they **spread the tenon along the length of the mortise, i.e. toward the end-grain walls of the mortise, never toward its long-grain cheeks.** For a horizontal member through a post, the kerfs are therefore horizontal, the tenon spreads up and down, and the wedges' faces are horizontal.
  *Why:* the mortise ends are cut across the grain (end grain), so they resist spreading pressure. Pressure on the long-grain cheeks splits the post like firewood.
  **R08-023** — (Should) Stop the kerf short of the shoulder by at least c. 1/4 of the tenon length, and **bore a small stop hole** at the root of each kerf in brittle species.
- **Cutting sequence:** (1) Cut the tenon. (2) Saw the kerfs. (3) Cut the mortise and pare its exit ends to the flare. (4) Make the wedges from riven hardwood, grain along the wedge.
- **Assembly:** drive the member home. Dip the wedges in water or leave them dry (practice varies), then drive both wedges alternately so the tenon spreads evenly. Saw and pare flush.
- **Common errors:** wedge kerfs across the grain of the post (R08-022). Wedges too thick, which splits the tenon along the kerf into the shoulder. No flare in the mortise, which makes the wedge merely pack the tenon.
- **Variants:** the blind version (jigoku-hozo, §2.10). Wedges driven *beside* the tenon, between the tenon and the mortise end, instead of into kerfs (楔締め, as with nuki, §4.1). The stepped through-tenon (§2.6).

### 2.9 Through-tenon with nose pin — 鼻栓 (hanasen)
- **Class:** hozo + pin outside the member (→ Ch. 09 §3)
- **Typical use:** long tenons that **project through** the receiving member: sashigamoi and tie beams passing through a post, ashigatame, hijiki-like projecting members, sill-to-sill joints, and farm and storehouse (蔵) frames. It is also used where a projecting tenon is part of the design (e.g. through-tenons of 腕木 *udegi* and 出し梁 *dashibari*).
- **Load behaviour:** the pin bears against the **outer face** of the receiving member, so the tenon is locked in withdrawal. The pin can be knocked out, which makes the joint **demountable**.
- **Proportions:** tenon projection beyond the far face ≥ c. 2.5–3 × pin width plus the pin. For a 15–18 mm pin the projection is c. 60–90 mm. Pin centre c. 1–1.5 pin widths from the post face, with a draw of c. 1 mm toward the post.
- **Orientation:** the pin is vertical for horizontal tenons (driven from above) and horizontal for vertical tenons. The pin runs across the tenon thickness, i.e. through the short dimension of the tenon if possible, so that the long dimension carries the pin's bearing.
- **Rules:**
  **R08-024** — (Should) Use the nose pin where the joint **must be demountable** or visible, and the wedged tenon where it need not be.
  **R08-025** — (Must) The hanasen hole is set with **draw toward the receiving member** (1 mm or so), never away. Otherwise the joint stays loose.
- **Common errors:** projection too short, so the nose shears. The pin driven through the end grain of the receiving member instead of just outside it.
- **Variants:** komisen inside the post and hanasen outside on the same tenon. Paired hanasen on long tenons.

### 2.10 Blind fox-wedged tenon — 地獄枘 (jigoku-hozo, "hell tenon")
- **Class:** hozo + hidden wedges
- **Typical use:** where a tenon **cannot come through** but must not pull out: posts into stone-set or buried sills, some sill junctions, furniture and fittings. It is also used as a repair joint where no access exists from the far side. The name ("hell") refers to the fact that once it is in, it can never come out.
- **Load behaviour:** strong in withdrawal. **Irreversible.**
- **Proportions:** tenon *t* and *w* as for a short or long tenon. One or two kerfs, c. 2/3 of the tenon length. The mortise bottom is **flared (undercut)** toward the ends by c. 2–4 mm per side. Wedges are short (c. 1/2 of the tenon length), fitted loosely into the kerfs so their tips touch the mortise bottom when the tenon is c. 5–10 mm from home. Wedge thickness is sized so that the tenon spreads exactly to fill the flare. This is judged by test pieces.
- **Orientation:** as R08-022. The spread is toward the end-grain walls of the mortise.
- **Assembly:** insert the tenon with the wedges started in their kerfs and drive the member home. The mortise bottom drives the wedges in. The fit **must be right the first time**.
- **Rules:**
  **R08-026** — (Should) **Avoid jigoku-hozo in structural members of buildings intended to be repaired by dismantling** (解体修理, → Ch. 16). Where it is used, record it in the drawings and repair documentation.
  *Why:* the joint cannot be taken apart without destroying the tenon, which defeats the reversible, repairable logic of Japanese framing.
- **Common errors:** wedges too long, so they bottom early and split the tenon before it seats. Wedges too thin, so nothing locks. A mortise with no flare.
- **Variants:** 地獄ほぞ with a pin as a safety. The "blind dovetail" (§2.13) as a demountable alternative.

### 2.11 Collar or lip housing — 襟輪 (eriwa)
- **Class:** shallow housing / locating lip (also called 入輪, *iriwa*)
- **Typical use:** a small projecting lip or shallow step on the tenoned member, let into a matching recess in the receiving member, usually a post. It keeps the horizontal member from **twisting** (ねじれ) and **shares the load with the tenon**. It is used at tie beams (繋ぎ梁) into posts, at the post attachments of 長押 *nageshi* and 回縁 *mawaribuchi*, and on dovetailed members at sills. Where it is used in combination it is written 襟輪付き (e.g. 襟輪付き短ほぞ).
- **Load behaviour:** resists rotation of the horizontal member about its own axis and carries some vertical shear, relieving the tenon.
- **Proportions:** the depth of the recess into the post is **shallow, c. 10–15 mm (c. 3–5 bu); do not exceed c. 15 mm on a 105–120 post.** In plan the eriwa is often V-shaped or dovetail-shaped (逆ハの字), so it also locks laterally.
- **Rules:**
  **R08-027** — (Should) Keep the eriwa recess **≤ c. 15 mm deep** in a post of 105–120 mm, and do not add an eriwa on a face that already carries a mortise at the same height from another direction.
  *Why:* the eriwa removes the outer fibres of the post, which carry most of its bending stress. Cut deeply, it defeats its purpose.
- **Variants:** 襟輪欠き (eriwa-kaki). The 腰掛け seat as a more load-carrying alternative.

### 2.12 Slotted post top — 輪薙込み (wa-nagikomi)
- **Class:** open slot (bridle) in the post head
- **Typical use:** **post tops receiving a horizontal member that passes over or between them**: the head tie (頭貫, *kashiranuki*) of temples and shrines, which drops into slots in the post heads and is spliced over the posts. In minka and some orioki work, the keta or a beam drops into the post head. It is also used at torii (鳥居) for the tie (貫) and at gates.
- **Load behaviour:** the horizontal member is held laterally by the two cheeks of the post head. It carries vertical load in bearing on the bottom of the slot. It does not resist uplift unless pinned or loaded from above, which the bracket set or daiwa normally does.
- **Proportions:** slot width = the width of the member received (e.g. the kashiranuki thickness). Slot depth = the member's depth, sometimes less where a housing or cog in the member itself takes the rest. Each remaining cheek ≥ c. 1/4–1/3 of the post diameter.
- **Orientation:** the slot runs along the member. At a splice over the post, the splice (e.g. 鎌継ぎ in kashiranuki, → Ch. 07) sits inside the slot, so that the cheeks prevent the splice from spreading.
- **Rules:**
  **R08-028** — (Should) Where a splice lies in a wanagikomi slot, the **slot cheeks must enclose the whole splice**, and the splice's male piece is dropped in last.
  **R08-029** — (Should) The member dropped into a wanagikomi slot must be **held down** by the member above (daiwa, masu, keta) or by a pin (dabo). An open slot never resists uplift by itself.
- **Variants:** 輪薙込み with a *dabo* into the member above. Partial wanagikomi (the slot only part of the member depth, with the member notched to suit).

### 2.13 Loose tenon — 雇い枘 (yatoi-hozo)
- **Class:** hozo (separate, "hired" 雇い)
- **Typical use:** where **neither member can carry its own tenon**: two members meeting end-to-end or at an angle where the male piece would be too short or awkward to cut. Repairs, where the original tenon has rotted and a new one is let in (→ Ch. 16). Mitred corners, where a loose tenon or tongue passes across the mitre. Sashimono passing a post: a 雇い竿 (yatoi-zao) runs through the post and connects both side members (§4.3).
- **Load behaviour:** as a tenon, but the loose tenon must be fixed into *both* members, with pins, wedges, shachisen or glue in fittings.
- **Proportions:** the same section as an integral tenon. Length ≥ 2 × the housed length needed in each member. **The loose tenon's grain runs along its length.** It is made of a species as hard as or harder than the members (kashi, keyaki).
- **Rules:**
  **R08-030** — (Must) A yatoi (loose tenon, loose tongue, key) is always **long-grained in the direction of the force it carries**, and of **equal or harder species** than the members it joins.

### 2.14 Dovetail tenon and sliding dovetail tenon — 蟻枘 (ari-hozo) / 寄せ蟻 (yose-ari)
- **Class:** hozo (dovetail-shaped)
- **Typical use:** the **ari-hozo** is a tenon with dovetail-shaped (flared) sides. It locks against withdrawal where it can be slid in sideways from an open face, e.g. at sill corners (the dovetail kone-hozo of §2.6). The **yose-ari** ("brought-in dovetail") is a dovetail tenon on the end of a vertical member (hanging strut 吊束 *tsurizuka*, floor strut, stud, post inserted after the beam is in place). It is dropped into a larger pocket and then **slid sideways** into a dovetailed socket, and the pocket is then packed with a filler piece (埋木 *umeki*) or wedge.
- **Load behaviour:** resists withdrawal in the direction of the dovetail's flare. It can be assembled after both members are fixed.
- **Proportions:** dovetail flare c. 1:5–1:6 per side (representative). Pocket length ≈ dovetail length + the slide distance.
- **Rules:**
  **R08-031** — (Should) With yose-ari, the **filler piece is fixed** (pinned or glued in fittings) and must fill the pocket completely. Otherwise the member simply slides back and drops out.
- **Variants:** 寄せ蟻 in 吊束 (hanging struts of ceilings, → Ch. 14). 蟻ほぞ in 床束. 送り蟻 (okuri-ari, a sliding dovetail housing, also used for battens; see §7).

### 2.15 Box tenon — 箱枘 (hako-hozo) (uncertain term)
The name 箱ほぞ is used by some carpenters for a tenon that is **shouldered on all four sides** and sits in a mortise that is fully enclosed (a "boxed" tenon). Others use it for a tenon with a mortise cut *into* its end, forming a box or U that straddles part of the receiving member. Usage varies and is not uniform in the standard references this author could verify. **Treat the term as regional and define it on the drawings.** The rules for tenons in general apply (R08-002 to R08-005).

### 2.16 Rules for post-top (柱頭) and post-foot (柱脚) tenons

**R08-032** — (Must) At the **post foot on a sill**, the post shoulder bears on the sill top over its full area. The tenon locates the post. Where uplift or overturning must be resisted, the tenon is long and pinned (§2.2), or metal is added (→ Ch. 09 §9).

**R08-033** — (Must) At the **post top under keta or beams**, the tenon width *w* lies along the keta or beam (R08-002). Where two members meet over the post (keta splice, or keta plus beam), **the tenon passes into the lower member only**, unless it is designed as a kasane-hozo (§2.7).

**R08-034** — (Should) Mortises in the **soffit of a beam near mid-span** (for tsuka, studs or posts below) are kept **short (tan-hozo, c. 30–45 mm deep)**. Mortises for a post that *supports* the beam are best placed at a joint over the post rather than at mid-span.
*Why:* the soffit of a simply supported beam is in tension near mid-span. A deep mortise there acts as a notch and starts cracks.

**R08-035** — (Should) A post-foot mortise must **not be cut into a sill splice** (→ Ch. 07, e.g. koshikake-ari or koshikake-kama-tsugi), neither through the joint plane nor through the male tongue. The splice is placed just beyond the post, typically c. 150 mm (≈ 5 sun) off it, on the side that lets the post stand on uncut sill. Under modern practice the anchor bolt is set close to the splice on the **upper piece (上木, *uwaki*)** side, meaning the member whose tongue lies on the seat, because holding down the upper piece also holds the lower one (→ Ch. 07, → Ch. 17).
*Why:* the splice already reduces the sill to about half its section. A mortise through it leaves almost nothing, and the post load then splits the seat.

**R08-036** — (Should) **Posts on stone (石場建て, *ishibadate*)** have no sill tenon. The post foot is scribed (光付け, *hikaritsuke*; → Ch. 10) to the stone, and at most a *dabo* locates it in a shallow socket in the stone. **Floor ties (足固め, *ashigatame*) with through-tenons (§2.8–2.9 / §4.3) then do the work of the sill.**

**R08-037** — (Should) **Through-posts (通し柱, *tōshibashira*)** of two-storey frames get the greatest care in section loss (§10). Their foot tenon into the sill is long and pinned, or wedged through.

## 3. Group B — Housings and seats

A **housing** (大入れ, *ōire*, "big insertion") lets the *whole end* of a member into a shallow recess in the receiving member, so the end is supported on three sides and hidden. A **seat** (腰掛け, *koshikake*) is a ledge that the member rests on. A **cog** (腮 or 顎, *ago*, "jaw") is a notch-and-tooth interlock between two members lying one over the other. In Japanese framing these are rarely used alone. They are almost always combined with a dovetail (蟻, *ari*) or a tenon, which gives the joint its tensile and positional locking.

### 3.1 Plain housing — 大入れ (ōire)
- **Class:** shiguchi (housing)
- **Typical use:** the ends of secondary members into posts, sills or beams: floor joists (根太 *neda*) into beams, ōbiki into sills, studs, braces (§8.6), fire-strut ends, thresholds (敷居 *shikii*) and lintels (鴨居 *kamoi*) into posts. In practice it is almost always combined as 大入れ短ほぞ差し, 大入れ蟻掛け (§3.3) or 大入れ釘打ち.
- **Load behaviour:** carries **vertical shear by bearing** on the bottom of the housing. It stops the end from twisting and hides shrinkage gaps. It gives no withdrawal resistance by itself.
- **Proportions:** housing depth **10–15 mm (≈ 3–5 bu)** into posts and sills of 105–120 mm, and up to c. 20 mm into large beams. The full width and depth of the entering member are housed, or its depth minus a small bevel.
- **Orientation:** the housing is cut on the receiving member. The entering member is cut square (or bevelled on its bottom edge in katagi-ōire, §3.2).
- **Marking:** the outline of the entering member is transferred from its actual size (現寸, *genzun*), not from nominal dimensions. Fit is achieved by kigoroshi on the male piece.
- **Rules:**
  **R08-038** — (Should) Housings are **10–15 mm deep** in members of 105–120 mm width. **Deeper housings require a reason** (e.g. a heavy load on the seat) and a check of the receiving member's net section.
  **R08-039** — (Must) The housing is sized to the **actual** member, not the nominal one. Every housed member is matched to its own housing (記号合わせ) and marked.
- **Common errors:** housings cut to nominal size, leaving gaps. Housings on both sides of a post at the same height, which with the tenon mortises can remove half the post (see §10).

### 3.2 Slanted housing — 傾ぎ大入れ (katagi-ōire), and with short tenon (傾ぎ大入れ短ほぞ差し, *katagi-ōire tan-hozo-sashi*)
- **Reading:** 傾ぎ is read **かたぎ (*katagi*)**. It is also written 傾木. The name means "tilted housing".
- **Class:** shiguchi (housing with a sloped seat) + short tenon
- **Typical use:** **the end of a 2nd-floor girt (胴差, *dōsashi*) or a beam into a through-post (通し柱)**, where the girt comes from one or two sides at the floor level of a two-storey frame. Sashigamoi ends. It is one of the standard connections of the modern post-and-beam system, where it is normally supplemented by a 羽子板ボルト (*hagoita* bolt, → Ch. 09 §9) or strap to resist pull-out.
- **Load behaviour:** the **sloped bearing surface** carries the vertical shear of the girt into the post without deep housing: the girt "sits on" a sloping ledge. The short tenon resists lateral displacement. It gives **no pull-out resistance** without a pin, key or metal.
- **Geometry:** the post is housed shallowly (c. 10–15 mm). The *bottom* of the housing is not horizontal but **slopes downward toward the post face**, like the underside of a shoulder. The girt end is cut to match: its lower corner is bevelled, so the girt drops into the housing and wedges itself down and against the post. From the outside, the girt appears to be resting lightly on the post.
- **Proportions (representative, 105–120 post, girt depth *h* 180–300 mm):** housing depth at the top c. 15 mm. The sloped seat runs over the lower c. 1/3–1/2 of the girt depth, at an angle chosen so that the seat's depth into the post at its lowest point is c. 15 mm (practice varies). Short tenon 30 mm thick, c. 60 mm long, located in the upper part of the girt end.
- **Orientation:** the slope always descends into the post. A girt coming from both sides at the same height (二方差し) receives the two tenons from opposite faces, and they must not meet (§4.3, §10).
- **Rules:**
  **R08-040** — (Should) Katagi-ōire tan-hozo-sashi is a **shear connection only**. In any location where the girt can be pulled away from the post (all exterior walls, all through-post connections), add a pull-out restraint: a sao-shachi (§4.3), a hanasen, or a hagoita bolt.
- **Common errors:** the slope cut the wrong way (up into the post), so the girt slides off. The housing taken too deep "for safety", which weakens the through-post just where a second girt enters from the orthogonal direction.
- **Variants:** 傾ぎ大入れほぞ差し (with a longer tenon). 腰掛け (seat) instead of slope.

### 3.3 Housed dovetail — 大入れ蟻掛け (ōire-arikake)
- **Class:** shiguchi (housing + dovetail)
- **Typical use:** **T-junctions of sills (土台)**, of beams into beams (梁 into 胴差 or 桁), of floor beams (大引 into 土台), of joist-carrying beams (根太掛け), and in general **wherever one horizontal member meets another in a T at the same level**. It is the single most common horizontal shiguchi of the Japanese frame.
- **Load behaviour:** the housing carries vertical shear and prevents twisting. The dovetail resists **withdrawal** (the members being pulled apart) up to the shear strength of the dovetail neck and the socket cheeks. It cannot resist the entering member lifting out upward. That is resisted by the load above, by pins or by bolts.
- **Proportions (representative):**
  - housing depth 10–15 mm
  - dovetail length (蟻の長さ), measured from the housing face: **c. 0.4–0.6 × the receiving member's width**, i.e. c. 40–60 mm in 105–120 sills
  - dovetail neck width (首, *kubi*) ≈ **1/3 of the entering member's width**, i.e. 30–40 mm
  - flare (蟻勾配) c. 1:5 to 1:6 per side. The head is c. 1.5 × the neck width.
  - dovetail height: on sills, the full depth or the full depth less a short bottom chamfer. On beams into deeper beams, the dovetail occupies the upper part of the entering beam. **The lower part of a deep beam** is either seated in a housing (腰掛け) or bevelled.
- **Orientation:** the **male (dovetail) is on the entering member**, which is the lower-ranking member. The socket is on the continuous member. The dovetail is **dropped in from above**.
- **Marking:** mark the dovetail from the centreline of the entering member. Mark the socket from the centreline cross mark on the receiving member.
- **Cutting sequence:** (1) Saw the dovetail cheeks at the flare. (2) Saw the shoulders. (3) Cut the housing and the socket in the receiving member: saw the socket's flared walls, chop out, and pare the bottom flat. (4) Pare a slight clearance (c. 0.5 mm) at the bottom of the socket.
- **Assembly:** drop in and drive down with a mallet. Kigoroshi on the dovetail cheeks draws the joint tight as it seats.
- **Rules:**
  **R08-041** — (Must) The dovetail neck is **≥ 1/3 of the entering member's width** and free of knots, shakes and wane.
  *Why:* the neck is the weakest section in tension and the first to fail.
  **R08-042** — (Must) The socket must leave at least **c. 1/3 of the receiving member's width** as uncut wood behind the dovetail, i.e. the dovetail length ≤ c. 0.6 × *B*.
  **R08-043** — (Should) **A post must not stand over a dovetail socket** in a sill. Place the post on uncut wood beside the junction, or combine the post tenon and dovetail as in §5.6.
- **Common errors:** dovetail too long, so it cuts the receiving member nearly through. Dovetails cut with the flare on the wrong side (backwards) on one face. The socket bottom not flat, so the member rocks.
- **Variants:** 腰掛け蟻掛け (*koshikake-arikake*, dovetail with a seat below; the name is sometimes used for a beam-into-girder connection where a separate seat carries the beam). 片蟻掛け (*kata-arikake*, a dovetail flared on one side only, for use at corners and near ends). 蟻掛け without housing (older and rougher work).

   │            ┌──────┐                  │
   │           /        \   ← socket      │
   │          /          \                │

### 3.4 Helmet dovetail — 兜蟻掛け (kabuto-arikake)
- **Class:** shiguchi (lap over + dovetail)
- **Typical use:** the **roof beam (小屋梁) over the eaves plate (軒桁)** in **kyōro-gumi (京呂組)**, where the beam's top is to be **higher than the top of the keta**. For example, where the ceiling height must be increased, or where the beam is simply deeper than the keta seating allows. It is also used for other beams landing on a plate where the beam top is above the plate.
- **Load behaviour:** the beam end sits on the keta top (in compression). A downward-hanging dovetail on the underside of the beam end engages a socket in the keta top and resists the beam being pulled inward (withdrawal). The overhanging "helmet" (兜, *kabuto*), i.e. the part of the beam end lying *on* the keta, carries the rafters. The joint does **not** resist uplift of the beam. Traditional work relies on the roof load, and modern work adds a hagoita bolt through the keta.
- **Geometry:**
  - the beam end is cut so that its lower part forms a **dovetail tongue that drops into a dovetail socket in the top of the keta**
  - its upper part rests on the keta top and **stops at c. the keta's centreline**, or at its outer face in some versions
  - the top of the beam end is cut to the **roof pitch** and notched for rafters (垂木欠き or 垂木道 *taruki-michi*), so the rafters pass over the beam end and the keta smoothly
  - the end grain is concealed by the fascia or the eaves boarding
- **Proportions (representative):** dovetail depth into the keta c. 1/3–1/2 of the keta depth (e.g. 40–60 mm into a 120–150 keta). Neck ≈ 1/3 of beam width. The beam end bearing on the keta top ≥ 1/2 of the keta width.
- **Orientation:** the beam is male, the keta female. The beam is dropped in from above after the keta is set and spliced.
- **Rules:**
  **R08-044** — (Should) In kabuto-arikake **the beam end stops at about the centreline of the keta** and the rafters are notched over it. The beam must never project beyond the keta's outer face and interfere with the rafters or fascia.
  **R08-045** — (Should) Where the kabuto-arikake is used at an exterior wall with wind uplift, **add a pull-down restraint** (hagoita bolt, strap, or a traditional long pin through beam and keta into the post).
- **Common errors:** a dovetail socket in the keta placed over a keta splice. The helmet cut without allowing for the rafter pitch, so the roof line humps.
- **Variants:** 兜大入れ蟻掛け (with a housing). **Watari-ago** (§3.5) is used when the beam and keta are to be interlocked at the same level with the beam passing through.

### 3.5 Cogged joint — 渡り腮 (watari-ago)
- **Class:** shiguchi (cog / notch-and-tooth crossing)
- **Typical use:** **beams crossing over keta or girders** (梁 over 桁, 小屋梁 over 敷桁 in kyōro and orioki frames), **floor beams over girders**, and upper beams over lower beams in multi-level frames. In the traditional *machiya* and in the "watari-ago method" (渡り腮構法), popularised in recent decades by some Japanese architects as a way to build rigid floor frames without hardware, beams are laid over girders in both directions at two levels and interlock by watari-ago at every crossing.
- **Load behaviour:** the upper member sits on the lower member. The **cog** (the uncut centre of the lower member's top, called 腮 *ago*) engages a **groove** in the underside of the upper member. This resists the upper member **sliding along the lower member's length** and the lower member rotating (rolling). The notches at both sides of the cog lock the upper member against **sliding along its own length**. The watari-ago develops considerable **horizontal shear and in-plane (floor diaphragm) stiffness**. Vertical load is carried in bearing. Uplift is not resisted without a pin or bolt.
- **Geometry:**
  - **lower member:** its top is cut away on both sides of the upper member's footprint to a small depth, leaving the central **cog** standing (its width ≈ 1/3–1/2 of the lower member's width)
  - **upper member:** its underside receives a **transverse groove** matching the cog, with shallow side notches that sit on the lowered shoulders of the lower member
  - the upper member **passes over** and may continue (as in a beam running over a keta to support eaves) or end a short distance beyond it
- **Proportions (representative):** cut into the lower member (the notch at each side of the cog) c. **15–30 mm**. Groove in the upper member c. **20–30 mm deep**, or up to c. 1/5 of its depth. **The combined loss must be kept below c. 1/3 of either member's depth.**
- **Orientation:** the upper member is the one carried. The **cog is on the lower member**. In kyōro-gumi the beam is the upper member and the keta the lower. In the watari-ago floor method, beams at the upper level cross girders at the lower level.
- **Variants and names:** texts and regions distinguish several forms of watari-ago, including names such as 本渡り腮 (*hon-watari-ago*), 大渡り腮 (*ō-watari-ago*) and 小渡り腮. The distinctions concern how much of the lower member's width is housed, whether the upper member is also housed along its sides, and the depth of the cog. **The definitions are not uniform across sources, and this author could not verify a standard set.** When the terms are used on drawings, draw the section.
- **Rules:**
  **R08-046** — (Should) The **combined depth of cut** of a watari-ago (cog notch in the lower member plus groove in the upper) must not exceed c. **1/3 of the depth of either member**. Watari-ago crossings over a post should be **directly over the post** or near it, not at mid-span of the lower member.
  **R08-047** — (Should) In watari-ago floor frames the beams are **laid in a single consistent direction per level**, and every crossing is cogged. Mixing cogged and uncogged crossings defeats the diaphragm action.
- **Common errors:** cogs cut too narrow, so they split off along the grain. The upper member's groove cut too deep, causing bending cracks at mid-span.

```
   Elevation along the lower member (keta), upper member (beam) in section
                ┌───────────┐
                │  beam     │
                │   ┌───┐   │  ← groove in beam underside
   ─────────────┘   │cog│   └──────────────  ← lowered shoulders (15–30 mm)
   keta             │   │                     keta top
   ─────────────────────────────────────────
```

### 3.6 Halving at crossings — 相欠き (aikaki)
- **Class:** shiguchi (lap / halving)
- **Typical use:** **members crossing at the same level**: sills at corners and crossings (with pins or tenons), keta at corners (§5.4), ceiling grids (格縁 *gōbuchi*), kumiko of fittings, battens, fence rails, bracket arms (肘木 *hijiki*) crossing in bracket sets (§5.9).
- **Load behaviour:** locks the two members in plan. Vertical load passes across the joint by bearing. Each member is weakened to about half its depth at the crossing, so **halvings are placed only over supports** in structural members.
- **Proportions:** each member is cut to 1/2 of the depth where depths are equal. Where depths are unequal, cut so both members' tops or bottoms are flush as required, but **never remove more than 1/2 of either member**.
- **Orientation:**
  **R08-048** — (Must) At a halving in a load-carrying frame, the **member that is supported (the upper piece, 上木) has its notch facing down**, and the member that supports (下木) has its notch facing up. The halving lies **over a post or on a continuous support**.
  *Why:* a halving at mid-span leaves each beam with only half its depth where the moment is greatest.
- **Rules:**
  **R08-049** — (Should) Structural halvings are **locked with a pin, a tenon from the post below passing through both members, or a dovetail form (蟻形相欠き)**. A plain halving can lift apart.
- **Common errors:** halvings in beams at mid-span. The upper piece notched upward (inverted), which makes it hang on half its depth.
- **Variants:** 蟻形相欠き (dovetail-shaped halving). 箱相欠き (box halving, §5.4). 相欠き with 目違い. The 十字目違い (§6.3).

### 3.7 "Daimochi" — 台持ち (a splice, not a shiguchi)
**台持ち継ぎ (daimochi-tsugi)** is a **splice** (tsugite) used for roof beams over a post or other support: two members lap with a sloping seat and are held with dabo and bolts. **It is catalogued in → Ch. 07.** It is mentioned here because it is always placed *over a support* and so is always cut together with the post-top shiguchi. **R08-050** — (Should) When a daimochi-tsugi lies over a post, the **post-top tenon (or dabo) passes through both halves of the lap**, locking the splice to the post.

(落とし掛け *otoshigake*, the tokonoma head member, is a *component*, not a joint. Its fixing into the tokobashira is treated in → Ch. 14.)

### 3.8 Girt to post — 胴差し (dōsashi) connections
- **Class:** assembly of shiguchi
- **Typical use:** in two-storey frames the **girt (胴差, *dōsashi*)** runs at the second-floor level between the through-posts (通し柱) at the corners and important junctions, and sits on the tops of the ground-floor posts (管柱, *kudabashira*) in between.
- **Connections used:**
  1. **To through-post, one side only (corner or end):** katagi-ōire tan-hozo-sashi (§3.2) + pull-out restraint (hagoita bolt; traditionally a **long tenon through the post with a hanasen** or **wedged**, §2.8–2.9).
  2. **To through-post, two sides at the same height (二方差し):** one girt carries a **through tenon or yatoi-zao** that passes through the post into the opposite girt and is locked with **shachisen** (雇い竿車知 / 竿車知, §4.3), or both girts enter with kone-hozo wedged through from the opposite face (staggered, §10).
  3. **Over ground-floor posts (kudabashira):** the kudabashira top enters the underside of the girt with a tan-hozo (or long tenon with pin at wall ends). The upper storey post stands on the girt with a tan-hozo.
  4. **Beam into girt:** ōire-arikake (§3.3) or watari-ago (when the beam sits over).
- **Rules:**
  **R08-051** — (Must) A girt splice (→ Ch. 07, e.g. 追掛け大栓継ぎ or 腰掛け鎌継ぎ) is placed **near a ground-floor post, c. 150–300 mm off it, on the side where the male piece rests on the seat of the female**. It is never placed at mid-span between posts, and never in the through-post.
  **R08-052** — (Should) Where girts from **three or four directions** meet a through-post at one floor level, stagger them (§10) and use at most one pair of through (sao) connections in one direction at a given height.

### 3.9 Sashigamoi to post — 差鴨居 (sashigamoi) connections
- **Class:** assembly of shiguchi
- **Typical use:** the **sashigamoi** is a deep lintel (c. 6 sun to 1 shaku or more in depth, i.e. 180–300+ mm) that carries the sliding-door grooves and acts as a **structural beam and moment-resisting tie** between posts in minka and machiya. It is one of the main sources of racking resistance in traditional frames (→ Ch. 10).
- **Connections used (in rising order of strength):**
  1. **Stepped tenon, wedged through the post** (小根ほぞ差し割楔締め, §2.6 + §2.8)
  2. **Long tenon with komisen**, pinned through the post from its side (込栓打ち)
  3. **Through tenon with hanasen** (§2.9)
  4. **Sao-shachi / yatoi-zao-shachi** (§4.3) where sashigamoi meet from opposite sides of a post
- **Proportions (representative):** tenon thickness 1/3 *B* (e.g. 36–40 mm in a 4-sun post). Tenon height c. 0.5–0.7 of the sashigamoi depth, stepped to reduce the through part. The groove side (the underside, with 樋端 *hibata* and grooves for doors) is kept intact at the post face with a small shoulder.
- **Rules:**
  **R08-053** — (Must) A sashigamoi that is expected to act as a moment tie **must be locked against withdrawal at the post** (wedges, shachi, hanasen or komisen), and its shoulders must bear on the post face tightly over the full depth.
  *Why:* its racking resistance comes from the couple between the upper and lower parts of the shoulder bearing against the post, and from the lock that keeps the shoulder in contact.
  **R08-054** — (Should) Where sashigamoi enter a post from **two orthogonal directions**, set their tenons at different heights, or pass one tenon above or beside the other (§10).

## 4. Group C — Through-member connections

Traditional Japanese frames are held together, and resist racking, largely by **horizontal members that pass through or into posts and are locked there by wedges, keys and pins**. The two families are:

- the **thin through-ties** (貫, *nuki*), which pass through the posts
- the **deep inserted members** (差物, *sashimono*): sashigamoi, tie beams and floor ties, which enter the posts from the sides

The frame's behaviour is covered in → Ch. 10 and → Ch. 17. The fasteners themselves are in → Ch. 09.

### 4.1 Through-tie — 通し貫 (tōshi-nuki), wedged at the post (楔締め, *kusabi-jime*)
- **Class:** through-member + wedge
- **Typical use:** the horizontal ties that pass through posts at several levels: 地貫 (*jinuki*, ground tie), 腰貫 (*koshinuki*, waist tie), 胴貫 (*dōnuki*), 内法貫 (*uchinorinuki*, head-of-opening tie), 天井貫 (*tenjōnuki*), and in temples the 頭貫 (*kashiranuki*, head tie, often in wanagikomi slots, §2.12) and 飛貫 (*hinuki*). Nuki are also the lath framework for mud walls (→ Ch. 14). Heavier nuki-like floor ties (足固め, *ashigatame*) join the post feet of ishibadate frames.
- **Load behaviour:** each nuki acts as a **tie and a bending member between posts**. The **racking resistance** of a nuki frame comes from the nuki bearing on the top and bottom of its hole in the post as the post rotates: embedment (めり込み, *merikomi*) of the nuki into the post and of the post into the nuki, plus friction. This gives a **soft but very ductile** resistance that recovers well after large deformation (→ Ch. 17). The wedges keep the nuki tight in the hole so that bearing starts at small rotation.
- **Proportions (representative):**
  - traditional minka and machiya wall nuki: **thickness 4–6 bu (12–18 mm) × depth 3.5–4 sun (105–120 mm)**, often sawn from sugi or hinoki
  - heavier structural nuki in "traditional method" new builds and in temple frames: 24–30 mm and more × 105–150 mm. Temple kashiranuki and hinuki are much larger (e.g. 1/2–2/3 of the post diameter deep).
  - **hole in the post:** width = nuki thickness (tight, 0 to +0.5 mm). Height = nuki depth + wedge allowance, typically c. **10–20 mm** for wall nuki of 105–120 mm depth.
  - **wedges:** paired hardwood wedges (kashi, keyaki, or seasoned hinoki), as wide as the nuki thickness, c. 1/2 of the post width long each, taper c. 1:8 to 1:15 (→ Ch. 09 §4)
- **Orientation rules:**
  **R08-055** — (Must) The **nuki hole is cut with its long side along the post** (R08-002), and the nuki is **set to its level line (陸墨)**. The wedge gap is taken on one side only, so the other face of the nuki bears directly on the marked end of the hole.
  **R08-056** — (Must) Structural nuki are wedged with **a pair of wedges driven from opposite faces of the post** into the same gap, meeting near the post centre. A single wedge driven from one face is not acceptable for a structural nuki.
  *Why:* a single wedge tilts the nuki in the hole and bears only at one edge. Paired wedges fill the gap evenly and grip over the full post width.
  **R08-057** — (Should) **Which side of the nuki takes the wedge varies by carpenter and level.** A common rule is: the wedge **above** the nuki at most levels (the nuki bears on the bottom of its hole, which is the marked level), and **below** the lowest tie where the nuki must be pushed up against a reference. Whatever the choice, **all nuki at one level are wedged on the same side**, and the choice is written on the story pole.
  *Why:* consistency keeps the nuki level from post to post. The frame then does not creep toward one side as the wedges are driven.
- **Marking:** mark the hole on two opposite faces of the post from the post's centreline and the level line. Chop from both faces to meet in the middle.
- **Cutting sequence:** (1) Bore or chop the hole from both faces. (2) Pare the hole's long faces dead parallel, because they bear the nuki. (3) Pare the bearing end (the one on the level line) square. (4) The wedge side may be left slightly rough.
- **Assembly:** during tatemae (→ Ch. 15) the posts are stood and the nuki threaded through in sequence, lowest first. Where nuki are spliced in the post (§4.2) the spliced ends are engaged as they pass. After the frame is plumbed (建て入れ直し, *tateire-naoshi*), the wedges are driven in pairs, working symmetrically across the frame. They are **re-tightened** after the frame has dried and settled (増し締め, → Ch. 09 §7).
- **Common errors:** holes chopped from one face only, so they wander. Wedges of soft or cross-grained wood that crush or split. Wedges driven before plumbing, which locks the frame out of plumb. Wedges trimmed flush and glued, which prevents re-tightening.

```
   Post in elevation, nuki passing through (wedge above)
          │   post   │
          │ ┌──────┐ │ ← wedge gap (10–20 mm), wedges driven from
          │ │▓▓▓▓▓▓│ │   front and back faces, meeting at the centre
   ═══════╪═╪══════╪═╪═══════   nuki
   ═══════╪═╪══════╪═╪═══════
          │ └──────┘ │ ← hole bottom = level line (陸墨)
          │          │
```

### 4.2 Nuki splices inside the post
Nuki are rarely long enough to run a whole wall, so they are **spliced inside a post hole**. Common forms are a simplified hook splice (略鎌, *ryaku-kama*) or halving (相欠き) with the two ends overlapping inside the hole, locked by the wedges, with or without a pin (→ Ch. 07).

**R08-058** — (Must) **Nuki splices are made only inside a post** (or, in heavy work, directly beside it with a separate key). They are never made in open span.
*Why:* the nuki's function depends on continuity at the post. A splice between posts has no capacity at all.

**R08-059** — (Should) At a nuki splice in a post, the **hole length is the full post width**. The overlap is c. 2/3 or more of the post width, and **the wedges bear over the splice**, so that they lock the two halves together as well as to the post.

**R08-060** — (Must) Where nuki from **two orthogonal directions** pass through the same post, their holes are **vertically offset** so they do not intersect. The minimum offset is the height of one nuki hole. Where they must be at the same level, they are **halved across each other inside the post** and designed as one assembly. This is rare in walls and more common in temple frames.

### 4.3 Through-tongue with keys — 竿車知 (sao-shachi) and 雇い竿車知 (yatoi-zao-shachi)
- **Class:** through-member + flat tapered keys (車知栓, *shachisen*; → Ch. 09 §2)
- **Typical use:** **two horizontal members meeting from opposite sides of a post at the same height**: sashigamoi, girts (胴差) at through-posts, tie beams. It connects each member to the post and also connects the two members *to each other* through the post, so the post is clamped between two shoulders. Sao-shachi is also a **splice** (竿車知継ぎ, → Ch. 07) for sills and keta.
- **Load behaviour:** very strong in **withdrawal**. The keys draw the shoulders of both members tight against the two faces of the post. The joint is **demountable** by driving the keys out. It gives good **moment resistance** in deep sashigamoi, because the shoulders bear over the full depth.
- **Geometry:**
  - **sao-shachi:** member A carries a long **tongue (竿, *sao*)** of tenon section. It passes through a mortise in the post and into a slot (mortise) in the end of member B, which is itself shouldered and housed against the post's opposite face.
  - **the keys (shachisen)** are driven through member B across the sao. Each key's slot is cut half in B and half in the sao, with the sao half **offset toward the tip of the sao** (the draw). Driving the key pulls the sao, and thus member A, through the post, while pushing B toward the post.
  - **yatoi-zao-shachi:** a **separate loose tongue (雇い竿, *yatoi-zao*)** of hard wood passes through the post and into slots in *both* members, and each member is keyed to it. It is used where neither member can carry a long enough tongue, or where both members must be installed after the post.
- **Proportions (representative):**
  - sao thickness ≈ 1/3 of the post width (30–40 mm)
  - sao height c. 0.5–0.7 of the member depth, with top and bottom shoulders
  - sao length = post width + c. 1.5–2 × member depth into B
  - **shachisen** c. 12–18 mm thick, c. 30–45 mm wide (in the direction of the sao), long enough to pass through the member depth, taper c. 1:10 (representative)
  - **typically two keys per joint**, driven from opposite faces (one from above, one from below, or one from each side)
- **Orientation rules:**
  **R08-061** — (Must) Shachisen are **driven in pairs from opposite faces**, and their slots are offset (drawn) so that driving **closes** the joint. Test the direction of draw on the dry fit before cutting the final slots.
  **R08-062** — (Should) The key slots are located at least **c. 1.5 × the key width from the sao tip** and **from the post face**, so that neither the sao end nor B's end grain shears out.
- **Cutting sequence:** (1) Cut the sao on A. (2) Cut the through mortise in the post, and the sao mortise and shoulders in B. (3) Dry-fit A, post and B. (4) Mark the key slot positions through B onto the sao. (5) Withdraw and cut the slots, offsetting the sao half by the draw (c. 1–2 mm). (6) Make the keys from riven kashi.
- **Assembly:** set the post. Insert A's sao through the post, then bring B onto the sao against the far face. Drive both keys alternately until the shoulders close. Trim the keys but leave them long enough to re-drive.
- **Common errors:** keys in softwood. Keys driven from one face only, which cocks the sao. Slots drawn the wrong way. Key slots cut before dry fit.
- **Variants:** sao with hanasen instead of keys. **Sao-shachi across a 四方差し** (§5.7) with the orthogonal members kone-hozo wedged at a different height.

```
   Plan: sashigamoi A and B meeting at a post, sao-shachi
        A                  post               B
   ─────────────┐     ┌──────────┐     ┌──────────────
                │═════╪══════════╪═════╪═══╗ sao of A  │
                │     │          │     │  ▓║▓ ← shachisen (2, from
                │═════╪══════════╪═════╪═══╝   opposite faces)
   ─────────────┘     └──────────┘     └──────────────
```

### 4.4 Pinned sashimono — 込栓打ち (komisen-uchi) and nose-pinned (鼻栓) through-tenons
These are the horizontal equivalents of §2.2 and §2.9. A sashimono tenon enters the post and is pinned through the post's side with komisen (square or round). Alternatively, it passes through and is pinned outside with a hanasen. **R08-063** — (Should) Where a horizontal member is komisen-pinned into a post, **drive the pin horizontally through the post across the tenon**, i.e. through the post's cheeks, with the draw toward the member's shoulder. **Never drive it vertically down the post's grain** through the tenon.
*Why:* a vertical pin in a post runs along the post's grain and has almost no bearing on the post, and the post splits along the pin line.

### 4.5 Post-side pin — 胴栓 (dōsen)
The term 胴栓 (*dōsen*, "body pin") is used for a pin that passes **through the body of a member at a shiguchi**. Examples are a pin driven through a post and through the tenon of a horizontal member, or through a stacked assembly such as keta, beam and post top in an orioki corner. In function it is a komisen. Usage of the term varies, and some authors reserve it for pins driven sideways through a post into a tenon, as distinct from pins through a sill. The rules for komisen (R08-013 to R08-016, → Ch. 09) apply.

### 4.6 Summary of wedge orientation at posts

**R08-064** — (Must) **At a post, every wedge is oriented so that its pressure acts on the end-grain walls of the hole (above and below a horizontal member), never on the long-grain side walls.** In practice:
- kerf wedges in a through-tenon (割楔) are **horizontal** (R08-022)
- wedges beside a nuki are driven **horizontally across the post**, into the gap above or below the nuki (R08-056)
- keys (shachisen) pass through the *member*, not through the post

*Why:* the side walls of a post mortise are long grain and split under lateral pressure. The ends are end grain and resist crushing.

**R08-065** — (Should) Wedges and keys are **driven in opposing pairs** wherever possible, and **tightened progressively and symmetrically across the frame**, never one post at a time to full tightness.

## 5. Group D — Corner and multi-member joints

### 5.1 Mitres — 留め (tome) and their hidden forms
- **Class:** shiguchi (mitre family). These are mostly **finishing, fittings and furniture joints** (→ Ch. 14), but they also appear in exposed structural corners in sukiya and shrine work.
- **Forms:**
  - **大留め (ō-dome):** a full mitre across the whole section, visible on both faces. It needs a tongue, key or tenon inside, because a plain mitre has no strength.
  - **隠し留め (kakushi-dome):** a "hidden" mitre. It shows as a mitre on the face or faces, while the inside is a tenon, halving or shouldered joint that carries the load.
  - **箱留め (hako-dome):** a mitre for box-like corners (e.g. frames of tokonoma boards, boxes and shelving), in which the two members lap and mitre at the visible edges.
  - **留め形隠し蟻 (tomegata kakushi-ari):** a **secret mitred dovetail**. It shows as a plain mitre, but concealed dovetails inside lock the corner. It is used in high-grade boxes, cabinetry and tokowaki fittings.
  - **三方留め (sanpō-dome):** three members meeting in mitres at one corner (frames, legs and rails). It is often keyed with shachisen in fine work (車知留め).
- **Rules:**
  **R08-066** — (Must) **A plain mitre is never structural.** Every tome carrying load has a concealed structural element (tenon, tongue 雇い実, dovetail or shachi key).
  **R08-067** — (Should) In a mitre between members of **different moisture content or grain direction**, the mitre opens at the heel or toe as they move. Use seasoned stock and, in exposed exterior work, prefer a lapped or shouldered corner that hides movement.

### 5.2 Kyōro-gumi corners — 京呂組の隅 (keta over post, beam over keta)
In **kyōro-gumi** the keta (軒桁) sits on the post tops and the roof beams sit **on the keta** anywhere along it, independent of the post positions (→ Ch. 11). At the **building corner** the eaves keta and the gable beam or gable keta (妻梁 / 妻桁) meet over the corner post.

Typical assembly, from bottom to top:
1. **Corner post top:** a short or long tenon (§2.1–2.2), sometimes a kone-hozo, entering the lower of the two corner members.
2. **Keta and gable member at the corner:**
   - (a) **Halving** (相欠き, §3.6), or box halving or nejigumi (§5.4), with both ends projecting beyond the corner as horns (鼻, *hana*). The corner post's tenon passes through both halves, which is the most traditional form.
   - (b) **Housed dovetail** (大入れ蟻掛け) of the gable member into the keta, with the keta end projecting. This is common in modern work, with an L-strap or hagoita bolt.
3. **Beams on the keta:** kabuto-arikake (§3.4) or watari-ago (§3.5). Where a beam lands near the corner, it is kept off the corner post's tenon and off the halving.
4. **Horizontal corner brace (火打ち梁, §8.4)** between keta and gable member.

**R08-068** — (Should) At a kyōro corner the **keta ends and the gable member's ends project beyond the corner** by at least c. 1 × member depth (a "horn"). Alternatively, the corner is made with a joint designed for member ends (nejigumi, box halving).
*Why:* a dovetail or halving right at a member's end has no relish. The projection supplies it and also carries the verge and eaves.

### 5.3 Orioki-gumi corners — 折置組の隅
In **orioki-gumi** each roof beam sits **directly on a post**, and the keta (敷桁 or 軒桁) is laid on or around the beam ends (→ Ch. 11). The post top carries a **kasane-hozo** (§2.7) passing through the plate and into the beam, or the beam and plate are cogged together (watari-ago) and the post tenon passes into the lower member.

Typical orioki post head, from bottom to top:
1. Post, with a kasane-hozo or long tenon.
2. **敷桁 (shikigeta, the plate)** on the post, mortised through for the tenon.
3. **小屋梁 (the roof beam)**, cogged (渡り腮) over the plate, with the upper stage of the kasane-hozo in its soffit. The beam end projects beyond the plate and carries the eaves keta (軒桁) in some regional forms. The beam ends are often exposed.

**R08-069** — (Should) In orioki-gumi the **beam spacing equals the post spacing**, and the beam, plate and post are **locked on a single axis by one tenon**. Do not place a plate splice between the post and the beam at such an axis (R08-021).

**R08-070** — (Should) At an orioki **corner**, where the gable plate (妻側の桁/梁) meets the eaves plate over the corner post, use a halving or nejigumi with the kasane-hozo passing through both, and keep a horn.

### 5.4 L-corners of plates and keta — 相欠き, 箱相欠き (hako-aikaki), 捻組 (nejigumi)
- **Class:** corner shiguchi between two horizontal members at the same level
- **Forms:**
  - **Plain halving with horns** (相欠き, §3.6): the simplest form.
  - **Box halving** (箱相欠き, *hako-aikaki*): a halving in which one or both members are also **housed** into the other on their side faces. The lap is then boxed in, and neither member can slide relative to the other in any horizontal direction.
  - **Twisted interlock** (捻組, *nejigumi*): a corner joint in which the two halving surfaces are not flat but **stepped or crossed in opposite directions**. Each member hooks over and under the other, so the joint cannot be separated vertically without first rotating. It is used at the **corners of plates, daiwa and keta in temple and shrine work** and at exposed projecting corners. **Its exact geometry varies considerably by school.** The name may cover several forms, and a drawing should always accompany the term.
- **Load behaviour:** all forms lock the corner in plan. Box halving and nejigumi also resist separation in several directions and twisting.
- **Rules:**
  **R08-071** — (Should) At an L-corner of plates the **member on the long side (桁行, *ketayuki*) is usually the lower piece** (下木), and the gable-side member (梁間, *harima*) is laid over it. The **corner post's tenon passes through both**. This is customary, and local practice may reverse it where roof loads demand.
  **R08-072** — (Must) Any L-corner halving must be **held down** (by the post tenon through both halves, a dabo, a bolt, or the weight of rafters and hip), because a halving alone lifts apart.

### 5.5 Corner posts — 隅柱 (sumibashira)
The corner post receives **two members from orthogonal directions at each level**: sills at the foot, nuki and sashimono in the walls, keta or girt at the top.

**R08-073** — (Must) At a corner post, **no two mortises from orthogonal directions are placed at the same height unless they are designed together**: halved nuki, a combined tenon, or one member passing above the other. The standard rules for staggering are given in §10.

**R08-074** — (Should) The corner post's foot is **pinned or wedged** (long tenon with komisen, kone-hozo wedged, or fan tenon with pin) because corner posts are the first to lift under racking and wind. In modern frames, corner posts get the largest hold-down metal (→ Ch. 09 §9, → Ch. 17).

### 5.6 Sill corners and junctions — 土台の隅・T・十字
- **Class:** shiguchi on sills (土台, *dodai*)
- **Typical use:** the ground sills of a frame on a continuous foundation (→ Ch. 10).
- **Forms:**
  1. **L-corner, housed dovetail (大入れ蟻掛け):** one sill is dovetailed into the other, and the receiving sill projects c. 1 sun or more beyond the corner as a horn, or ends flush in modern work. The corner post stands on the receiving sill's solid part with a fan tenon or stepped tenon.
  2. **L-corner, halving (相欠き):** each sill is halved. The **corner post's long tenon passes through both halves** and is pinned. This is common in older work, and gives the post a strong anchorage to both sills.
  3. **L-corner, single-sided dovetail (片蟻掛け, *kata-arikake*):** a dovetail flared on the outside face only, used where the corner is flush and the inner face must remain square.
  4. **L-corner, stepped tenon with wedges (小根ほぞ割楔締め):** the post's dovetail-shaped kone-hozo passes through the sill junction and is wedged, which draws the post toward the inside.
  5. **T-junction:** ōire-arikake (§3.3).
  6. **Cross (十字) junction:** halving (相欠き), with a post over it whose tenon passes through both.
- **Rules:**
  **R08-075** — (Must) **At a sill corner or junction, the post tenon must not coincide with the dovetail socket** (R08-043). Where the post must stand over the junction, use a halving with the post tenon through both halves, not a dovetail.
  **R08-076** — (Should) In modern frames on concrete foundations, an **anchor bolt is placed within c. 200 mm of each sill corner and each splice** (on the upper piece, R08-035), but **never through a dovetail or halving**, and with the nut recessed (座彫り, *zabori*) so it does not interfere with the post.
- **Common errors:** the corner post mortise cut through the dovetail socket. Short horns that split. Anchor bolts passed through the joint.

### 5.7 Three-way and four-way post junctions — 三方差し (sanpō-sashi) and 四方差し (shihō-sashi)
- **Class:** multi-member shiguchi at a post
- **Typical use:** interior posts of minka and machiya where **sashigamoi, girts or tie beams arrive from three or four directions at nearly the same height**, e.g. at the intersection of room partitions under a large roof. The "daikoku-bashira" (大黒柱, the central post) and the other main posts of the dirt-floored area (土間, *doma*) and the living room typically receive four-way connections.
- **Principle:** four members cannot all enter a post at exactly the same height with full-size tenons. The post would be cut into a cross of mortises and lose most of its section. The carpenter therefore:
  1. **pairs the opposite members** (e.g. north–south) and connects them **through the post with one sao-shachi** (§4.3) at height *h₁*
  2. brings the **orthogonal pair** (east–west) in at a **different height** *h₂*, each with a **kone-hozo wedged or pinned**, or a second sao-shachi passing **above or below** the first
  3. uses the **deeper members' depth** to make the offset invisible. Two sashigamoi of the same depth, one with its tenon in its upper half and the other with its tenon in its lower half, have flush top and bottom faces while their tenons are at different heights inside the post.
- **Rules:**
  **R08-077** — (Must) In a shihō-sashi, **the tenons of orthogonal members are vertically offset** so that their mortises do not intersect. Alternatively, where one tenon passes the other inside the post, the smaller tenon is designed to pass through a dedicated slot in the larger one, which is uncommon and demands a mock-up.
  **R08-078** — (Should) **Only one pair of opposite members is connected through the post (sao, through-tenon) at any one height.** The other pair is connected with shorter tenons.
  **R08-079** — (Should) Posts that receive four-way connections are **one size larger** than the ordinary posts (e.g. 5 sun or more where ordinary posts are 4 sun), or are the daikokubashira itself (often 7 sun to 1 shaku and more).
- **See §10** for worked examples of staggering and cross-section checks.

### 5.8 The term 独鈷 (tokko / dokko)
独鈷 (*tokko*, also *dokko*) is the Buddhist ritual object (vajra) with a narrow grip and flared ends. In carpentry the name is applied by some carpenters to **double-ended keys and joints with a waisted (dumbbell-like) shape**, related to the chigiri butterfly key (→ Ch. 09 §6), and in some regions to specific corner or splice forms. **This author could not confirm a single standard meaning as a shiguchi name.** Use the term only with a drawing.

### 5.9 Head plate at corners — 台輪 (daiwa)
In **zenshūyō** (禅宗様) and some later temple styles, a thick flat plate (台輪, *daiwa*) is laid over the post heads and head ties to carry the bracket sets (→ Ch. 12). At corners the daiwa members **cross with a halving and project beyond the corner post** as decorated ends (木鼻, *kibana*, in the broad sense). The corner post's dabo or tenon passes through both members.

**R08-080** — (Should) Daiwa corners are **halved with the projecting ends** and pinned to the post by a dabo or tenon passing through both halves. The bracket set's large bearing block (大斗, *daito*) sits over the crossing, so that its weight holds the halving closed.

### 5.10 Bracket-set joints (組物, kumimono) — summary
The joints of bracket sets are described fully in → Ch. 12. In outline:
- **Bearing block on post or daiwa (大斗, *daito*):** located by a **dowel (太枘, *dabo*)** or a short tenon from the post top or daiwa. The dabo is c. 1/5–1/4 of the block width square and c. 1–1.5 sun long (representative).
- **Bracket arms in the bearing block (肘木, *hijiki*):** the block has a cross-shaped seat (斗の口, *masu no kuchi*) into which the arms drop. Where arms cross (in the transverse and longitudinal directions), they are **halved across each other (相欠き)** inside the seat.
- **Small bearing blocks on arms (巻斗 *makito*, 方斗 *hōto*):** each is located on its arm by a **dabo**, never by a nail.
- **Rules:**
  **R08-081** — (Must) **Every block in a bracket set is located by dabo** (or tenon) in both horizontal directions. Nothing in a bracket set is held by nails alone.
  **R08-082** — (Should) Dabo holes are bored **slightly deeper than the dabo** (as R08-005) so that the blocks bear on their full seats, not on the dabo.

## 6. Group E — Crossing joints (組手 kumite)

**組手 (*kumite*)** is the general name for joints in which members **cross each other** and interlock, as opposed to one member ending against another. In structural work they occur at beam crossings, sill crossings and bracket arms. In fittings they occur in kumiko lattices, grid ceilings and screens.

### 6.1 Cross-halving — 相欠き組手 (aikaki-kumite)
The crossing form of the halving (§3.6). **R08-083** — (Should) For structural crossings, the depth removed from each member is ≤ 1/2 of its depth, the crossing is **over a support**, and the joint is locked by a dabo, a post tenon or a nail/pin. For lattice and ceiling work (格縁 *gōbuchi* of 格天井 *gō-tenjō*, kumiko), exact half-depth halvings are the norm, cut to a fit that allows the lattice to be assembled without forcing (→ Ch. 14).

### 6.2 Cogged crossing — 渡り腮 as kumite
See §3.5. As a crossing joint the watari-ago is superior to the plain halving for beams because **each member loses far less depth** (c. 15–30 mm each rather than half). The pair also resists sliding in both directions.

### 6.3 Crossing with alignment tongues — 十字目違い (jūji-mechigai)
- **Class:** crossing joint with 目違い (mechigai)
- **Typical use:** members crossing at the **same level** where a simple halving would allow twisting or where the visible surface must stay continuous: sill crossings, ceiling grids of heavy section, and frame members of gates and fences.
- **Geometry:** a cross-halving in which the halving surfaces carry **small stub tongues and grooves (目違い)**. These are typically 5–10 mm high and c. 1/3 of the member width. They key the two members so that they cannot rotate or slide within the lap.
- **Rules:** **R08-084** — (Should) Mechigai are **small (≤ c. 1/4 of the remaining depth)**. Large mechigai reduce the bearing area of the halving without adding meaningful strength.

### 6.4 Three-member and three-way crossings — 三方組手 (sanpō-kumite)
Where **three members cross at one point**, the forms are:
- **In one plane at 60° intervals:** as in kumiko patterns such as 麻の葉 (*asa-no-ha*) and 三つ組手 (*mitsu-kumite*). Each member is cut to 1/3 of its depth at the crossing, or with specific shaped notches, so that the three lock into one another. This is a fittings technique (→ Ch. 14).
- **In three orthogonal directions (3D):** as in the **Hida puzzle toy** 千鳥格子 (*chidori-gōshi*, "plover lattice"). Three square sticks interlock at one point, each passing through notches in the others, and the last stick is inserted in a sequence that locks the assembly. It is **illustrative, not structural**. It shows the principle of interlocking by assembly order, which appears in real frames at corner posts where plates, beams and posts meet (§5.2–5.4) and in bracket sets (§5.10).
- **In grid ceilings (格天井 *gō-tenjō*):** the gōbuchi grid is two-way. Where a grid meets a perimeter frame (回縁 *mawaribuchi*) at a corner, three members meet, usually by mitres and housings (→ Ch. 14).

**R08-085** — (Should) In any three-way assembly, **decide and mark the assembly order before cutting**. The last member in must be the one whose notches are open toward the direction of insertion.

### 6.5 "Koshi-ire" — 腰入れ (term, uncertain)
The term 腰入れ appears in some joinery lists for a crossing or housing in which one member is let into the other **by a small seat or step (腰)** in addition to the main notch, so that it bears on a ledge and resists twisting. **This author could not confirm a standard definition.** Where the term appears in a local specification, draw the section. Functionally, the rules for housings (§3.1) and mechigai (§6.3) apply.

### 6.6 Dowels between stacked members — 太枘 (dabo)
Where one member **lies on another without being cut into it**, e.g. blocks on arms (§5.10), a sill beam on stone (→ Ch. 10), a stacked log (校倉 *azekura* members), or a daiwa on a post, it is located by **dabo**: short square or round hardwood dowels, half in each member. The full rules are in → Ch. 09 §5.

## 7. Group F — Joints for boards and fittings (brief)

These joints belong to **floors, walls, ceilings and fittings** and are treated in → Ch. 14. They are listed here because they are shiguchi in the broad sense and because the same rules of grain and movement apply.

| Joint | Japanese | Use | Key rule |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Tongue and groove** | 本実 (*honzane*) | floorboards, wall boards, ceiling boards, doors | Tongue ≈ 1/3 of board thickness. Leave the groove c. 1 mm deeper than the tongue. Lay the boards with the correct heart-side (木表/木裏) orientation (→ Ch. 03). |
| **Loose-tongue joint** | 雇い実 (*yatoi-zane*) | wide boards, tabletops, high-grade panelling | The tongue is a separate strip of harder wood with its grain *across* the joint (or plywood in modern work). It is not glued to both boards in wide panels. |
| **Rebated (shiplap) joint** | 相決り (*aijakuri*) | wall and ceiling boards, sheathing | Each board is rebated to half its thickness. The overlap allows shrinkage without an open gap. |
| **Sliding dovetail batten** | 蟻桟 (*arizan*) | tabletops, wide doors, tokonoma boards, ceiling panels | A tapered dovetail batten is slid across the back of the board in a tapered dovetail housing (送り蟻, *okuri-ari*). It keeps the board flat and **lets it shrink and swell** across its width. It is never glued along its length. |
| **Breadboard end** | 端喰 (*hashibami*) | the ends of tabletops, doors, board partitions | An end rail is tenoned or tongued across the end grain. It is **pinned or glued only at the centre**, and the other pins sit in elongated holes. |
| **Suction batten** | 吸付き桟 (*suitsuki-zan*) | high-grade tabletops and boards | A precisely fitted sliding-dovetail batten, tapered so it "sucks" onto the board. The fit is exact enough to hold without glue or screws. |

**R08-086** — (Must) **Any joint that crosses the grain of a wide board must allow the board to move across its width.** Battens and end rails are fixed at one point, usually the centre, or slide in dovetail housings. They are never glued or rigidly pinned over the full width.
*Why:* a board of 300 mm width moves several millimetres across the grain between the rainy season and winter, and a rigid cross member splits it.

## 8. Group G — Rafters, braces and members meeting at angles

### 8.1 Rafters on plates — 垂木と桁 (taruki to keta)
- **Class:** shiguchi (notch / seat) + nail
- **Typical use:** common rafters (垂木, *taruki*) on eaves plates (軒桁), purlins (母屋, *moya*) and ridge (棟木, *munagi*) (→ Ch. 11)
- **Forms:**
  - **rafter notch (垂木欠き, *taruki-kaki*):** a small seat cut in the underside of the rafter so it sits on the arris or the chamfered top of the plate
  - **plate notch (垂木彫り / 垂木道, *taruki-bori / taruki-michi*):** the plate's top is notched for each rafter, or planed to the roof pitch (a bevel called 峠 *tōge* on keta and moya)
  - **cogged over (渡り):** the rafter is cogged over the plate, which is rare in houses and more common with heavy rafters
- **Fixing:** traditionally **one or two nails per crossing** (wa-kugi, → Ch. 09 §8), driven skew through the rafter into the plate. In modern work, rafter ties (ひねり金物) are added in windy areas.
- **Rules:**
  **R08-087** — (Should) Notches in rafters are kept to **≤ c. 1/4 of the rafter depth**, and plates are **bevelled (峠を付ける)** to the roof pitch rather than individually notched where rafters are closely spaced.
  *Why:* the rafter is thin and its bending capacity lies at the notch. The plate is continuous and should not be weakened by a notch at every rafter.
  **R08-088** — (Should) Rafter spacing marks (垂木割, *taruki-wari*) are laid out from the **centre of the bay (柱間の芯)** so that the pattern is symmetric at every opening (→ Ch. 02, → Ch. 11).

### 8.2 Hip rafters on plates — 隅木と桁 (sumiki to keta)
The **hip rafter (隅木, *sumiki*)** is seated over the corner crossing of the plates, with a notch whose geometry follows from kikujutsu (→ Ch. 05). Its seat on each plate is a **compound-angle cog or notch** (隅木の渡り腮・仕掛け). The hip's weight holds the plate corner down. Details are in → Ch. 11. **R08-089** — (Must) The hip seat is laid out from the **plate's centreline crossing and the hip's centreline (隅木芯)**, never from arrises, and the plate corner joint (§5.4) is cut so that the hip seat falls on solid wood of both plates.

### 8.3 Struts into beams — 束 (tsuka) into 梁
- **Class:** hozo
- **Typical use:** roof struts (小屋束, *koyazuka*) on roof beams, carrying purlins and ridge. Floor struts (床束) under ōbiki. Ceiling hangers (吊束, §2.14).
- **Joint:** **short tenon (短ほぞ差し)** at both ends, with the width of the tenon along the receiving member (R08-002). At the upper end the purlin (母屋) sits on the strut top with a short tenon, and the purlin splice is placed near the strut but not over the tenon (→ Ch. 07). Fixing is by **kasugai (staples, → Ch. 09 §9)** on two faces, or by komisen at gable ends and wind-exposed positions.
- **Rules:**
  **R08-090** — (Should) Roof struts are **tied laterally** by bracing ties (小屋筋交い / 雲筋交い, *koya-sujikai* / *kumo-sujikai*) or through-ties (小屋貫, *koya-nuki*) passing through them. The short tenons alone do not keep the strut plumb.
  **R08-091** — (Should) Strut mortises in the top of a roof beam are **placed along the beam's centreline**. A beam with natural curvature (太鼓梁 / 丸太梁) is marked from its levelled top (太鼓落とし) and centreline (→ Ch. 11).

### 8.4 Horizontal corner braces — 火打ち (hiuchi): 火打ち梁 (hiuchi-bari) and 火打ち土台 (hiuchi-dodai)
- **Class:** diagonal brace in the horizontal plane
- **Typical use:** at the **corners of the roof or floor frame** (plate to gable beam, girt to beam) and of the sill frame, a short diagonal member at c. 45° **stiffens the horizontal plane** so that the frame does not rack in plan (floor/roof diaphragm action).
- **Traditional vs modern:** horizontal diagonal braces of timber are found in pre-modern Japanese frames, notably in storehouses and larger buildings. Traditional houses relied more on **deep beams interlocked by watari-ago and dovetails, board floors, and heavy roof mass** for plan stiffness. The **standard hiuchi-bari and hiuchi-dodai** at every corner, bolted, belong to **modern post-and-beam practice** under current codes (→ Ch. 17). Pre-made steel hiuchi now often replace timber ones.
- **Joint:** each end is **housed (大入れ) and cogged (渡り腮) or dovetailed into the side of the plate or beam**, typically c. 15–30 mm deep. It is fixed with a **bolt** (火打ちボルト) through the brace end and the member (modern), or with pins or nails (traditional). Typical timber hiuchi section is 90 × 90 mm, placed at c. 45° with a leg length along each member of c. 600–900 mm (representative).
- **Rules:**
  **R08-092** — (Should) Hiuchi are placed so that their housings **avoid the joints, splices and post tenons** of the members they brace. Each end lands on uncut wood at least c. 1 × member depth from the corner joint.
  **R08-093** — (Should) In a restoration of a traditional building, **do not add hiuchi or other modern stiffening without a structural assessment and conservation approval** (→ Ch. 16). Stiffening one plane can shift damage to posts or walls that were never designed for it.

### 8.5 Knee braces — 方杖 (hōzue)
- **Class:** diagonal brace in the vertical plane between post and beam
- **Typical use:** posts to beams in open structures (sheds, gates, verandas, bell towers, bridges), eaves brackets, and to shorten the span of a beam
- **Joint:** at each end a **housing with a short tenon** (大入れ短ほぞ差し), with the brace end cut to bear square (in compression) on the housing's seat. It is pinned or nailed to keep it in place. Its angle is commonly c. 45°, or 30–60°.
- **Load behaviour:** the brace works in **compression**. It pushes the beam up and the post sideways, which can **split the post** if the brace lands high in a slender post, or **shear the beam** near its end.
- **Rules:**
  **R08-094** — (Should) A knee brace is **seated in compression on its housing's seat**, with its tenon for location only, and it lands on the post at a height where the post is not weakened by other mortises.
  **R08-095** — (Should) Knee braces are used **in pairs** (on both sides of a post or at both ends of a beam) so that their thrusts balance. A single hōzue pushes the frame to one side.

### 8.6 Diagonal wall braces — 筋交い (sujikai), and why traditional frames prefer nuki
- **Class:** diagonal brace in the wall plane
- **Typical use:** the principal **racking resistance** of the modern post-and-beam house. A diagonal member between the post foot/sill corner and the post top/plate corner, of 30 × 90, 45 × 90 or 90 × 90 mm, is fixed with **brace plates (筋交いプレート)** (→ Ch. 17).
- **Joint:** each end is **housed (大入れ)** into the post and into the sill or plate, and fixed with nails and a brace plate. Traditionally the end of a heavy brace was housed and nailed, sometimes with a short tenon.
- **Traditional vs modern:** the historical Japanese frame used diagonal bracing mainly in **storehouses, castles, temples' roof spaces and roof frames** (雲筋交い / 小屋筋交い). The walls of houses and temples relied on **nuki, sashigamoi and mud walls (土壁)** (→ Ch. 10). The two systems conflict. A diagonal brace in a wall **cuts through or interrupts the nuki and the mud-wall lath**. It works stiffly at small drift and then fails abruptly (buckling, end failure), whereas the nuki frame is soft but ductile.
- **Rules:**
  **R08-096** — (Should) **Do not combine sujikai and structural nuki in the same wall panel** unless the combination is designed. If both are present, the brace takes the load first and fails first, and the nuki then carry an already damaged frame. Cutting the nuki to pass the brace removes the nuki's function entirely.
  **R08-097** — (Must) Where a sujikai is used, **its ends land on the post–sill and post–plate corners** (near the joint), are housed, and are fixed with appropriate plates. The brace must never be notched into the post by more than c. 1/3 of the post width, and a post must never be cut to let a brace pass through it.
  **R08-098** — (Should) In restoration of traditional frames, **strengthening by sujikai is a last resort**. Prefer additional nuki, thickened mud walls, board walls (板壁), or frames with sashigamoi, in consultation with the conservation authority (→ Ch. 16, → Ch. 17).

## 10. Cross-section loss at posts and staggering of mortises

### 10.1 Why it matters
A post that receives members from several directions is weakened by every mortise, housing and hole cut into it. The critical section is the **horizontal cross-section at the height where most wood has been removed**. Failure starts there: a crushing and splitting failure under vertical load, or a bending fracture when the post is loaded by racking (as in an earthquake). The goal is to **distribute mortises over height** so that no single horizontal section loses too much.

### 10.2 Heuristic limits (representative, not code values)
**R08-099** — (Should) At any horizontal section of a post, keep the **remaining net area at least c. 1/2 of the gross area, and preferably 2/3**. Keep at least **one full face-to-face strip of uncut wood** (c. 1/3 of the width) in each direction.
*Why:* the post must still carry its axial load and its bending in both directions. Code checks of modern through-posts treat the reduced section explicitly (→ Ch. 17). These limits are experience values and must be confirmed by calculation for heavily loaded or slender posts.

**R08-100** — (Must) **Orthogonal mortises never intersect inside the post** unless the joint has been designed as one assembly (R08-060, R08-077).

**R08-101** — (Should) The **vertical offset (段違い, *dan-chigai*)** between the mortises of orthogonal members is at least **the height of the smaller mortise**, and preferably with c. 15–30 mm of uncut wood between them.

### 10.3 Worked example — 4-sun (120 mm) post receiving four members (shihō-sashi)
Members: two sashigamoi (N and S) of depth 240 mm, and two sashigamoi (E and W) of depth 240 mm, with their **top faces flush** at the same level (the lintel height of the room openings).

**Poor arrangement (do not use):** all four members with centred tenons 36 × 150 mm at the same height. At the tenons' height the post is cut by two crossing slots 36 wide, leaving four corner pieces of about 42 × 42 mm. Net area ≈ 4 × 42² ≈ 7,060 mm² of the gross 14,400 mm², or c. 49%. Worse, there is **no continuous strip** of wood across the post in either direction, and the post acts as four slender sticks at that level.

**Good arrangement:**
- **N–S pair:** connected by a **sao-shachi** through the post. The sao is 36 mm thick and **placed in the upper part** of the members: sao height 110 mm, from 20 mm below the top face to 130 mm below it.
- **E–W pair:** each enters with a **stepped tenon (kone-hozo)** placed in the **lower part** of the members. The ōne is 36 × 80 mm, entering c. 40 mm, from 140 mm to 220 mm below the top face. The kone is 36 × 45 mm, passing through and wedged, or the two E–W tenons are pinned with komisen from the N and S faces.
- **Shoulders and housings:** all four members are housed (ōire) only 10 mm, or not at all, with shoulders bearing on the post faces.

Result: at any horizontal section the post is cut **by one slot direction only**. At the sao's level, a 36 mm slot is cut N–S through the post, and the net area is (120 − 36) × 120 ≈ 10,080 mm², c. 70%. At the kone-hozo level the E–W slot is similar. There is a **10 mm band of uncut wood** between the two levels. The faces of all four sashigamoi are still flush.

   top of all four members  ───────────────────────────
           N–S sao (through, 36 thick)  ┃██████████┃  20–130 mm
                                        ┃          ┃
           uncut band                   ┃          ┃  130–140 mm
           E–W kone-hozo (from E and W) ┃░░░░░░░░░░┃  140–220 mm

### 10.4 Worked example — corner post with sill, nuki and girt
A 4-sun corner through-post in a two-storey minka:
- **Foot:** fan tenon or stepped tenon into the halved sill corner. Long tenon with komisen driven from the inside face.
- **Nuki at each level:** the two orthogonal nuki holes are offset by at least one nuki-hole height (R08-060). For example, the long-side nuki is set 30 mm above the gable-side nuki at every level, and the level lines on the story pole show both.
- **Second-floor girts (two directions):** katagi-ōire short tenon from each direction, with the tenons **offset vertically** by c. 1/2 of the girt depth (one high, one low). Each is locked by a hanasen or a hagoita bolt that passes the other's tenon. The two housings together must leave ≥ 1/2 of the net area (R08-099).
- **Top:** tenon into the plate corner (halving or nejigumi), passing through both plate members.

**R08-102** — (Should) **Before kizami begins, draw every face of each multi-connection post (a "post development", 柱の展開図) at full or 1:5 scale**, with every mortise, housing, hole and its height. Check the net section at every level where cuts overlap.

### 11.2 Checklist before cutting a shiguchi
1. Which member is continuous (higher rank)? (R08-001)
2. Does every mortise follow the grain of the member it is in? (R08-002)
3. Tenon ≈ 1/3 width? Mortise 2–5 mm deeper? (R08-003, R08-005)
4. Is there enough relish to the end grain and beyond the pin? (R08-007, R08-013)
5. Is withdrawal resisted where it must be? (R08-011, R08-040, R08-053)
6. Do wedges push on end grain only? (R08-022, R08-064)
7. Do orthogonal mortises clear each other, and is the net section adequate? (R08-099 to R08-101)
8. Is the joint off splices, dovetail sockets and anchor bolts? (R08-035, R08-043, R08-075)
9. Has the assembly order been decided, and is the dry fit done? (R08-008, R08-085)
10. Are all marks referenced to the centre and level lines, and matched with grid symbols? (R08-004, R08-039)
