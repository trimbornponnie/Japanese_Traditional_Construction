# Chapter 01 — Construction Manuals & Sources (建築書・文献 / *kenchiku-sho, bunken*)

## Scope

This chapter is the annotated bibliography of the rulebook. It lists and explains the sources a practitioner needs to understand, check and extend the rules in the other chapters. These are: the historical carpentry manuals of Japan, meaning the proportion books (木割書, *kiwari-sho*), the carpentry-geometry books (規矩術書, *kikujutsu-sho*), the printed pattern books (雛形本, *hinagata-bon*) and the secret family scrolls (秘伝書, *hidensho*); the official and institutional standards that govern timber building in Japan today; the modern Japanese practitioner and scholarly literature; the Western-language literature; online databases; and the museums, schools and bodies that keep the craft alive. It closes with rules for reading historical proportion texts, a recommended reading path, and a summary table.

The rules in this chapter (R01-xxx) are not about cutting wood. They are about **using sources properly**: which source governs which question, how far a historical proportion may be trusted, and how to avoid the most common mistakes made by readers of this literature.

**Verification markers.** Each bibliographic entry carries one of three markers:

- **[V]** Verified. Title, date and author were confirmed against library catalogues, publisher data or standard reference works while this chapter was being written.
- **[P]** Probable. This is the widely repeated standard attribution, but some detail (exact year, edition, author reading) could not be independently confirmed. Treat it as a strong but checkable claim.
- **[U]** Unverified. The item was named in the brief or appears in secondary literature, but its existence or details could not be confirmed. It is kept only so that readers know it was considered. Do not cite it without checking.

## 1. The written tradition: an overview

Japanese carpentry knowledge was passed on in four overlapping ways. Each produced a different kind of source.

1. **Oral transmission** (口伝, *kuden*), from master to apprentice on the job. Most everyday technique (how to sharpen, how to cut a particular splice, how tight a fit should be) was never written down. The written record is therefore **incomplete by design**.
2. **Secret family manuscripts** (秘伝書 *hidensho*, 伝書 *densho*, 家伝書 *kadensho*). These are hand-copied scrolls or bound books belonging to a carpenter family or school (流 *ryū*). They record the proportion rules (*kiwari*) and sometimes the geometry (*kikujutsu*) of prestige buildings: shrines, temples, gates, pagodas and palaces. From the late 15th to the 17th century they were the dominant written form. They were often deliberately terse or incomplete, because the full meaning was supposed to be supplied by the teacher.
3. **Printed pattern books** (雛形本 *hinagata-bon*). From the mid-17th century commercial woodblock publishing in Kyoto, Osaka and Edo made kiwari and kikujutsu knowledge available to any carpenter who could buy a book. The Architectural Institute of Japan library alone holds more than a hundred hinagata-bon, dating from 1700 (Genroku 13) to 1914 (Taishō 3). Printing broke the monopoly of the great families. It also spread fairly standardised proportions across the country.
4. **Modern textbooks, standards and scholarship** (from the Meiji era, 1868, onward). Carpenters' knowledge was re-organised into engineering-school textbooks, dictionaries and, later, building codes. Architectural historians (from the 1930s, and above all after 1945) went back to the old manuscripts and published critical editions.

A reader must always ask which of these four layers a statement comes from. A proportion in a 1608 family manuscript, a figure in an 1850 printed pattern book and a dimension in a 2020 building-code commentary are three different kinds of claim.

**R01-001** — Before using any rule, dimension or proportion, **identify which layer of the tradition it comes from**: oral practice, family manuscript, printed pattern book, or modern textbook/standard. Record that layer next to the figure in your notes. *(Must)*
*Why:* Figures from different layers have different authority. A manuscript proportion describes the ideal of one school at one time. A printed pattern book is a commercial simplification. A code figure is a legal minimum. Mixing them without labels is the most common source of error in secondary literature.

## 2. Historical Japanese manuals (chronological)

Each entry gives: title (kanji, rōmaji, English), date, author or lineage, content, significance, holdings and modern editions, and verification status.

### 2.1 Medieval manuscripts (15th–16th century)

#### 『三代巻』 *Sandai-kan* — "Scroll of the Three Generations" — 1489 [V/P]

- **Date:** colophon (奥書 *okugaki*) dated Entoku 1 (延徳元年), 1489. This is the oldest dated colophon of any surviving Japanese architectural text.
- **Authors:** the colophon names three people, usually given as 春巌昌椿 (Shungan Shōchin), 藤原吉定 (Fujiwara no Yoshisada) and 藤原縄吉 (Fujiwara no Nawayoshi). Readings of these names vary; treat them as approximate. The title presumably refers to three generations of transmission.
- **Content:** kiwari for religious buildings, written in literary Chinese (漢文 *kanbun*).
- **Transmission:** it does not survive as an independent 15th-century original. It is known through the copy incorporated in 『愚子見記』 *Gushikenki* (1680s, see below). Its 1489 date therefore rests on a later copy of the colophon.
- **Significance:** the earliest securely datable evidence that proportion rules were being written down. It proves that a codified kiwari system existed in the late Muromachi period, before the great Edo-period manuals.

#### 『木砕之注文』 *Kokudaki no chūmon* (also read *Kikudaki no chūmon*) — "Specifications for Dividing the Timber" — 1562 [P]

- **Date:** commonly given as Eiroku 5 (永禄五年), 1562.
- **Alternative title:** also known as 『寿彭覚書』 (*Juhō oboegaki*; reading uncertain).
- **Content:** a kiwari text. Among other items it specifies the dimensions of torii members (笠木・島木・貫 etc.) and of shrine and temple components. 木砕 *kikudaki / kokudaki* ("breaking down the timber") is an older synonym of 木割 *kiwari*.
- **Modern edition:** a critical study and edition has been published by 中央公論美術出版 (Chūō Kōron Bijutsu Shuppan), associated with research by 中川武 (Nakagawa Takeshi, Waseda University). Exact editors and year were not confirmed.
- **Significance:** together with *Sandai-kan*, it is the main evidence for pre-Edo kiwari. It is usually counted as the oldest kiwari book that survives as an independent text. It is often linked with the Shitennō-ji (四天王寺) carpenter tradition of Osaka, but that attribution could not be confirmed and should be treated as unverified.

**R01-002** — Treat medieval kiwari texts (*Sandai-kan*, *Kokudaki no chūmon*) as **evidence of how proportions were thought about, not as recipes**. Never reproduce a building from them without a measured survey of a comparable surviving building. *(Must)*
*Why:* They are fragmentary. They survive in later copies, use a specialised vocabulary whose readings are partly conjectural, and were written for readers who already knew the unwritten rules.

### 2.2 The shogunal master-carpenter families (early Edo, 17th century)

After 1600 the Tokugawa shogunate created a works office (作事方 *sakuji-kata*) headed by hereditary master carpenters (大棟梁 *daitōryō*). Two families dominated it, the **Heinouchi** (平内) and the **Kōra** (甲良). A third, the **Nakai** (中井) of Kyoto, served as 京都大工頭 (*Kyōto daiku-gashira*, chief carpenters of Kyoto) and controlled the carpenters of the Kinai region. Each family kept its own proportion manuscripts, and the differences between them are the origin of the "schools" (*ryū*) of kiwari.

#### 『匠明』 *Shōmei* — "Clarity of the Craftsman" (often rendered "The Master Craftsman's Illumination") — 1608 [V]

- **Date:** colophon by 平内政信 **Heinouchi Masanobu** dated Keichō 13 (慶長十三年), 1608. An earlier colophon by his father 平内吉政 **Heinouchi Yoshimasa** is dated Keichō 10 (1605).
- **Lineage:** the Heinouchi family came from Kii province (Wakayama). They became *sakuji-kata daitōryō* of the Edo shogunate.
- **Structure:** five books (五巻):
  1. 殿屋集 *Den'ya-shū*: residences and palaces (shoin-style mansions, 主殿 *shuden*, guest halls). This is the key source for early shoin-zukuri proportions (→ Ch. 13).
  2. 門記集 *Monki-shū*: gates of all types (→ Ch. 12, Ch. 13).
  3. 社記集 *Shaki-shū*: Shinto shrines (→ Ch. 12).
  4. 塔記集 *Tōki-shū*: pagodas (→ Ch. 12).
  5. 堂記集 *Dōki-shū*: Buddhist halls (→ Ch. 12).
- **Content:** kiwari tables and prose rules. For each building type the text gives the bay spans, and then derives member sizes (columns, beams, tie-beams, bracket parts, rafters, eaves projection) as fractions or multiples of a base dimension. It also contains a famous preface on the carpenter's moral and intellectual qualifications. That preface is often quoted for its list of the skills a master must have: reading and writing, arithmetic, drawing, kikujutsu, and carving.
- **Holdings:** the principal manuscript is held by the University of Tokyo.
- **Modern edition:** 伊藤要太郎 校訂 (ed. Itō Yōtarō), 『匠明』 with the companion commentary 『匠明五巻考』 (*Shōmei gokan-kō*), 鹿島研究所出版会 / 鹿島出版会 (Kajima), 1971; reprinted several times.
- **Significance:** the single most important kiwari manuscript. It is complete, well dated, covers every major building type, and comes from the carpenters who actually built for the shogunate. Most modern discussions of kiwari start from *Shōmei*.

#### 『建仁寺派家伝書』 *Kenninji-ha kadensho* — "Family Transmission Books of the Kennin-ji School" — 17th–19th c. [V]

- **Lineage:** the **Kōra (甲良) family**, hereditary *daitōryō* of the Edo shogunate and rivals of the Heinouchi. Their kiwari system is called **Kennin-ji school** (建仁寺流 *Kenninji-ryū*). The name is traditionally explained by the family's association with Kennin-ji in Kyoto; the exact reason is debated.
- **Content:** a set of 14 volumes covering, among others, shrines, halls, gates, pagodas, Zen-style (禅宗様) buildings, temple compounds (伽藍), sukiya, and detailed drawings of bracket parts. The titles include 『神社』, 『諸堂』, 『門集』, 『禅家』, 『伽藍』, 『層塔』, 『宝塔類』 and 『数寄屋』. Together they make a systematic design theory, and are notably strong on Zen-style architecture.
- **Holdings:** Tokyo Metropolitan Library (東京都立中央図書館, Special Collections), as part of the 646-item set "Materials relating to the construction of Edo Castle (transmitted in the Kōra family)" (江戸城造営関係資料（甲良家伝来）). The set is designated an Important Cultural Property. The library has published images online in its digital showcase.
- **Significance:** it represents the **Edo Kennin-ji school**, the second great Edo tradition of kiwari after the Heinouchi texts, and was widely copied.
- **Modern edition:** Kennin-ji school texts have been edited in the 『日本建築古典叢書』 series (Dairyūdō Shoten, 1980s), with contributions by 河田克博 (Kawata Katsuhiro) on the Kennin-ji school. The exact volume numbers and years are [P].

#### 『愚子見記』 *Gushikenki* — "Record of What a Foolish Son Has Seen" — 1682–1683 [P]

- **Date:** compiled in the Tenna era (天和), usually given as 1682 or 1683.
- **Author:** 平政隆 (Taira no Masataka / Hira Masataka), a carpenter associated with the Hōryū-ji (法隆寺) carpenter tradition of Nara. His relationship to the Heinouchi family could not be confirmed.
- **Content:** an encyclopaedic compilation in several books. It covers kiwari for many building types, carpentry lore, tools, ceremonies and technical notes. It **incorporates older texts, including the *Sandai-kan***, which is why it is the key witness to 15th-century kiwari.
- **Modern edition and study:** 内藤昌 (Naitō Akira), 『愚子見記の研究』, 井上書院 (Inoue Shoin), 1988 [V]. This is the standard study, with a transcription.
- **Significance:** it shows the Nara/Hōryū-ji line of practice, which is distinct from the Edo shogunal families. It is an anthology of earlier material, not a single-author manual.

#### Nakai family materials (中井家文書 / 中井家伝来資料) [P]

The Nakai family were the chief carpenters of Kyoto (京都大工頭) under the early Tokugawa. They controlled carpenter guilds across six provinces of the Kinai region and directed the construction of Nijō Castle, parts of Edo Castle, and major temples. They left a large archive of drawings (指図 *sashizu*), specifications and administrative documents, not a single named manual. These materials are held in several collections and have been studied by 谷直樹 (Tani Naoki) and others. They are the main source for **Kyoto practice and for the administration of large projects**, rather than for kiwari as such.

### 2.3 Printed pattern books (雛形本 *hinagata-bon*), mid-17th to 19th c.

"Hinagata" means "model" or "pattern". A hinagata-bon is a woodblock-printed book of standard designs with their proportions, usually drawn as elevations, with dimension notes and kiwari tables. They were sold commercially. Hundreds of titles and editions exist, and many were reprinted with changed titles. The entries below are representative; they are not a complete list.

#### 『新編雛形』 *Shinpen hinagata* — "Newly Compiled Patterns" — 1655 (Meireki 1) [P]

Often cited as the **earliest printed carpentry pattern book**. Its existence and date are repeated in the literature, but the edition could not be checked directly. It marks the start of commercial publication of kiwari.

#### 『大工雛形』 *Daiku hinagata* — "Carpenters' Patterns" (generic title) [V as a genre]

"Daiku hinagata" is not one book. It is a **generic title element** used by many publishers from the late 17th to the 19th century. Examples include series of pattern books for shrines (宮雛形 *miya-hinagata*), temples (堂雛形 *dō-hinagata*), gates (門雛形), sukiya and tea rooms (数寄屋雛形), and eaves and rafters (軒廻り雛形). Always cite the full title, publisher and date of the specific edition.

#### 『新撰大匠雛形大全』 *Shinsen daishō hinagata taizen* — "New Selection: Complete Patterns of the Master Craftsman" [V]

- A multi-volume printed pattern book. The National Diet Library holds a copy and has digitised it (NDL Digital Collections). An edition of 1851 is recorded; earlier editions under related titles (『大匠雛形』 *Daishō hinagata*) are reported from the early 18th century, but their dates are [P].
- **Content:** standard elevations and kiwari of shrines, temples and gates for general carpenters.
- **Use:** a good example of how the elite kiwari of the shogunal families was simplified and spread.

#### 『匠家雛形』 *Shōka hinagata* — "Patterns for Craftsman Households" [V]

- **Author:** 本林常将 (Motobayashi Tsunemasa; reading [P]), in three parts (3編).
- **Holdings:** Waseda University Library (古典籍総合データベース, with online images).
- **Date:** 18th century (Kyōhō period, 1716–1736, is the usual attribution) [P].

#### 『匠家必用記』 *Shōka hitsuyōki* — "Essential Records for Craftsman Households" — 1756 [P]

- **Date:** Hōreki 6 (宝暦六年), 1756 (standard attribution).
- **Author:** 立川小兵衛富房 **Tachikawa Kohei(ji) Tomifusa**, an Edo carpenter and founder of the Edo line of the **Tachikawa school** (立川流). His name also appears on 『軒廻棰雛形』 *Nokimawari taruki hinagata* ("Patterns for rafters around the eaves") [V], a kikujutsu-flavoured pattern book on eaves and fan rafters (扇垂木 *ōgi-daruki*), and on 『大工雛形 倭絵様集』 (*Yamato eyō-shū*, a collection of carving patterns, 1760s) [P].
- **Content (three books, 上中下):** less a proportion table than a **carpenter's handbook of knowledge and conduct**: the origins and legends of the craft, the meaning of ceremonies (jichinsai, jōtōshiki; → Ch. 15), the names and use of tools, units and measures, and general rules. Transcriptions of parts of the middle book circulate among modern craftsmen.
- **Significance:** it gives the self-understanding of an 18th-century urban master carpenter. The Tachikawa name was later carried to Suwa (Nagano) by 立川和四郎富棟 (Tachikawa Washirō Tomimune), who trained in Edo. The Suwa Tachikawa school became famous for shrine carving and competed with the **Ōsumi school** (大隅流), for example at Suwa Taisha.

#### 『番匠作事往来』 *Banshō sakuji ōrai* — "Primer on Carpenters' Work" — Edo period [V]

- **Genre:** an *ōraimono* (往来物), a school primer written in letter form and used to teach reading and vocabulary. It is not a technical manual.
- **Content:** it takes the pupil through building a samurai residence, from the ridge-raising ceremony onward. It names the parts of gates, foundations, roofs, main house, corridors, kitchen, stables, sheds and storehouses, with their materials. Illustrated editions (〈絵入〉番匠作事往来) and variant titles exist, such as 『大工番匠往来』 and 『普請番匠往来』.
- **Holdings:** Kyoto University (Rare Materials Digital Archive), the NIJL Union Catalogue (国書データベース), and others. It is available online.
- **Use:** very useful as a **period vocabulary list**, showing what a term meant in the Edo period.

### 2.4 Kikujutsu (carpentry geometry) literature, 18th–19th c.

Kiwari fixes the sizes of members. Kikujutsu (規矩術) fixes their **three-dimensional geometry**: slopes, hips and valleys, fan rafters, curved eaves, splayed members. It uses the carpenter's square (sashigane; → Ch. 02 §4, Ch. 05). Printed kikujutsu books appear from the 18th century. The genre peaks in the 1830s–1850s, when some master carpenters were also trained in Japanese mathematics (和算 *wasan*).

#### 『匠家矩術要解』 *Shōka kujutsu yōkai* — "Essentials of Square-Geometry for Craftsman Households" — 1833 [V]

- **Date:** Tenpō 4 (天保四年), 1833. The publisher was 須原屋茂兵衛 (Suharaya Mohei) of Edo and others.
- **Author:** compiled under the name 平内安房 (Heinouchi, lord of Awa). This was the title of **平内廷臣 Heinouchi Masaomi (also read Nobuomi)**, d. 1856, *daitōryō* of the shogunate. Sources give his birth year as 1791 or 1799.
- **Format:** an accordion-folded book (折本), about 17 × 7 cm, meaning a pocket reference.
- **Holdings:** Waseda University (online), National Diet Library.
- **Content:** condensed methods for hips, valleys and roof geometry using the sashigane.

#### 『矩術新書』 *Kujutsu shinsho* (also catalogued as 『匠家矩術新書』 and 『隅矩雛形 矩術新書』) — "New Book of Square-Geometry" — 1848 [V]

- **Date:** Kaei 1 (嘉永元年), 1848.
- **Author:** Heinouchi Masaomi.
- **Holdings:** Tohoku University (digital archive), CiNii-registered libraries.
- **Content:** a systematic treatise on kikujutsu. Heinouchi Masaomi was a mathematician as well as a master carpenter. The book is regarded as the point where Edo kikujutsu became a **theorised, mathematically argued discipline**, rather than a set of rule-of-thumb recipes. Later Meiji kikujutsu textbooks descend from this line.
- **Significance for this rulebook:** it is the historical basis of the methods in → Ch. 05.

#### 『規矩真術 軒廻図解』 *Kiku shinjutsu: nokimawari zukai* — "True Art of Kiku: Illustrated Eaves Framing" — Meiji era [P]

- **Author and date:** a second-hand catalogue lists it under 鈴木多橘 (Suzuki Takitsu; reading [U]) with the date Meiji 9 (1876). That may be the date of a reprint, not the first edition.
- **Content:** an illustrated treatment of eaves framing: corner rafters, fan rafters, hip geometry.
- **Status:** it exists, but the author and first-edition date need confirmation.

#### 『規矩元法』 *Kiku genpō* — **not a carpentry manual** [V: re-classified]

This title appears in some carpentry reading lists. According to the National Diet Library's "Mathematics of the Edo Period" (江戸の数学) exhibition, it is a **surveying manual of the Shimizu school** (清水流). It shows how to measure angles of elevation and depression with an instrument called the 規矩元器. Here 規矩 has its general meaning of "measure and square", not carpentry. It is included here only to warn against mis-citing it as a carpentry text.

#### 『紙上蜃気』 *Shijō shinki* [U]

Named in the brief as a kikujutsu or hinagata book. No catalogue record, author or date could be confirmed. Do not cite it without checking.

#### 大工棟梁秘伝書 (*daiku tōryō hidensho*, "secret books of master carpenters") — a genre, not a title [V as genre]

Many manuscripts with titles such as 『大工秘伝書』, 『番匠秘伝』 and 『規矩秘伝』 survive in local archives, private families and university collections. Most are unpublished. They typically mix kiwari tables, kikujutsu diagrams, ritual prescriptions (auspicious dimensions, ridge-raising rites, protective charms) and family genealogy. Regional archives and the NIJL Union Catalogue (国書データベース) are the finding aids. Any individual *hidensho* must be dated and placed in a lineage before its figures are used.

**R01-003** — **Cite pattern books by full title, edition, publisher and date**, never by a generic name such as "the Daiku Hinagata" or "a hinagata". *(Must)*
*Why:* The same title was reused by different publishers, and editions were silently altered. Two books with the same title can give different proportions.

**R01-004** — **Treat hinagata as design patterns, not structural codes.** A hinagata gives the appearance and proportion of a well-formed building of a given type and rank. It does **not** show that the members are adequate for a given span, snow load, site or earthquake. Check structure separately (→ Ch. 10, Ch. 17). *(Must)*
*Why:* Pattern books were written to reproduce accepted forms. Their authors assumed standard spans and standard timber quality. Scaling a hinagata to a bigger span or a weaker species while keeping its ratios can produce undersized members.

### 2.5 Meiji to early Shōwa: textbooks and dictionaries

#### 斎藤兵次郎『日本家屋構造』 Saitō Hyōjirō, *Nihon kaoku kōzō* — "The Construction of Japanese Houses" — 1904 (Meiji 37) [V]

- **Publisher:** 信友堂 (Shin'yūdō), 1904 onward.
- **Structure:** in several volumes: 上巻 構造篇 (construction) with plates; 中巻 製図篇 (drafting) with plates; 下巻 参考篇 (reference).
- **Content:** the first comprehensive **engineering-school textbook of traditional Japanese house carpentry**. It covers framing, joints (tsugite and shiguchi drawn systematically), roofs, fittings and drafting conventions, as practised around 1900.
- **Significance:** it documents late-Edo and Meiji urban house carpentry just before Western framing and metric codes changed it. It is the source of many of the joint drawings that still circulate. Reprints exist.

#### 中村達太郎『日本建築辞彙』 Nakamura Tatsutarō, *Nihon kenchiku jii* — "Dictionary of Japanese Architectural Terms" — 1906 [V/P]

- **First published:** 1906 (Meiji 39), by 丸善 (Maruzen) [P].
- **Modern edition:** 『新訂 日本建築辞彙』, annotated by 太田博太郎 and 稲葉信子, 中央公論美術出版, 2011 [P].
- **Content:** definitions of several thousand carpentry and architectural terms, many with small drawings. It records usage by carpenters at the end of the Edo tradition.
- **Use:** the **first place to check the historical meaning of a term**. Use it with JAANUS (§6) for English equivalents.

**R01-005** — When a term's meaning is disputed, **prefer the earliest reliable lexicographic source** (e.g. *Nihon kenchiku jii*, period primers such as *Banshō sakuji ōrai*) over modern popular usage, and record both meanings. *(Should)*
*Why:* Carpentry terms have shifted meaning. The same word (e.g. 内法 *uchinori*, 峠 *tōge*, 京間 *kyōma*) can mean different things in different regions or periods (→ Ch. 02 §2, §6).

| MOC Notification 2000 No. 1460 | 平成12年建設省告示第1460号 | Required metal fastening of joints and connections (N-value method etc.). This is the notification that most constrains traditional unfastened joinery. | [V] |
| MOC Notification 2000 No. 1352 | 平成12年建設省告示第1352号 | Balanced arrangement of walls (four-division method, 四分割法). | [V] |
| 2025 revision (effective April 2025) | 4号特例の縮小 等 | Reduction of the special exemption for small timber houses. Revised wall-quantity and column-size requirements. Structural documents now required for more houses. | [P: details → Ch. 17] |

**R01-006** — For any building that will be used, sold, or rented, **the current Building Standard Law, Enforcement Order and notifications override every historical manual in this chapter.** Where a traditional method does not comply with the prescriptive route, it must be justified by an accepted calculation route (e.g. limit strength calculation), not by appeal to tradition. *(Must)*
*Why:* This is the legal position in Japan. Designated cultural properties are handled under the Cultural Properties Protection Law and are exempted from the BSL (BSL Art. 3). Most traditional-style new buildings are not.

**R01-007** — **Check the date of the edition** of any code, notification or commentary before relying on a figure. *(Must)*
*Why:* Timber provisions were significantly revised in 1981, 2000, 2003 and 2025, among other years. Commentaries and textbooks lag behind the law.

### 3.2 Standard specifications and design guides

- **JASS 11 木工事** — 日本建築学会 (Architectural Institute of Japan, AIJ), 『建築工事標準仕様書・同解説 JASS 11 木工事』 [V]. The AIJ's standard specification for carpentry: timber grades and moisture content, joint and fastener workmanship, tolerances, protection. It is revised periodically, so cite the edition year.
- **公共建築木造工事標準仕様書** — 国土交通省大臣官房官庁営繕部 (MLIT Government Buildings Department), *Standard specification for public timber buildings* [V]. Used for government-funded timber buildings. The general 公共建築工事標準仕様書（建築工事編） also contains a carpentry chapter.
- **『木造軸組工法住宅の許容応力度設計』** — 公益財団法人 日本住宅・木材技術センター (Japan Housing and Wood Technology Center). Popularly called the **"grey book"** (グレー本) from its cover. The 2008 and 2017 editions are standard [V]. It gives the allowable-stress design method for post-and-beam timber houses and is the reference for structural calculation of conventional frames.
- **『[フラット35]対応 木造住宅工事仕様書』** — 住宅金融支援機構 (Japan Housing Finance Agency, formerly 住宅金融公庫), published annually by 井上書院 [V]. It is the de-facto workmanship specification for ordinary timber houses (foundations, sills, framing, fastening, insulation), with diagrams of standard joints. It is conservative, and useful for seeing what "normal" modern practice is.
- **『伝統構法を生かす木造耐震設計マニュアル — 限界耐力計算による耐震設計・耐震補強設計法』** — 木造軸組構法建物の耐震設計マニュアル編集委員会 (ed.), 学芸出版社, **2004** [V]. It applies limit strength calculation to traditional frames: minka, machiya, temples. This is the standard reference for seismic design and retrofit of traditional buildings.
- **『伝統的構法のための木造耐震設計法 — 石場建てを含む木造建築物の耐震設計・耐震補強マニュアル』** — 伝統的構法木造建築物設計マニュアル編集委員会 (ed.), 学芸出版社, **2019** [V]. It is the product of the MLIT-funded research project on design methods for traditional construction. It covers stone-foundation (ishibadate, 石場建て) buildings in which the columns are not fixed to the foundation. *(The brief's "2019" manual is this book. The similarly titled "伝統構法を生かす…" manual is the 2004 predecessor.)*
- **AIJ design standards for timber structures** — 『木質構造設計規準・同解説』 (AIJ) [V]. The engineering design standard for timber, including connection design.

**R01-008** — Use the specifications (JASS 11, the public specification, the Flat 35 specification) as a **minimum workmanship floor** and a vocabulary check. Do not use them as a source for traditional joinery proportions. *(Should)*
*Why:* They are written for modern metal-fastened frames. Their joint drawings are simplified, and they assume hardware that traditional practice avoids (→ Ch. 09).

### 3.3 Cultural-property conservation literature

- **Repair reports (修理工事報告書 *shūri kōji hōkokusho*)** [V as a genre]. For every repair of a National Treasure or Important Cultural Property building, the Agency for Cultural Affairs (文化庁) requires a detailed report. It contains measured drawings, a history of earlier repairs, analysis of original techniques, joint details found on dismantling, inscriptions (墨書 *bokusho*) found on members, and a record of replaced parts. Many are compiled by the **Japanese Association for Conservation of Architectural Monuments** (公益財団法人 文化財建造物保存技術協会, 文建協 *Bunkenkyō*, founded 1971) or by prefectural boards of education. These are **the most reliable source for how historic buildings were actually built**, as opposed to how manuals said they should be built. They are held in the National Diet Library, university libraries and the Nara/Tokyo national research institutes for cultural properties. Many are now digitised in the "全国遺跡報告総覧" or institutional repositories [P].
- **『文化財建造物伝統技法集成』** — 文化財建造物保存技術協会 [P: exact year/volumes]. A compilation of traditional techniques documented during conservation work.
- **Selected Conservation Techniques (選定保存技術 *sentei hozon gijutsu*)** [V]. A system created by the 1975 amendment of the Law for the Protection of Cultural Properties. It designates techniques essential for conserving cultural properties, and recognises **holders** (保持者, individuals) and **preservation bodies** (保存団体). Architecture-related techniques include, among others:
  - 建造物修理 (building repair);
  - 建造物木工 (architectural carpentry; holders have included 西岡常一 Nishioka Tsunekazu and 松浦昭次 Matsuura Shōji);
  - **規矩術（古式規矩）** (*kikujutsu, ancient style*) and **規矩術（近世規矩）** (*kikujutsu, early-modern style*). These are two separately designated geometries, which shows that "kikujutsu" is not one uniform system;
  - roofing techniques: 檜皮葺・杮葺 (cypress-bark and shingle roofing), 茅葺 (thatch), 本瓦葺 (tile);
  - 建造物彩色 (architectural polychromy), 左官（日本壁） (plastering), 建具製作 (fittings), 畳製作 (tatami).
- **UNESCO inscription (2020).** "Traditional skills, techniques and knowledge for the conservation and transmission of wooden architecture in Japan" (伝統建築工匠の技：木造建造物を受け継ぐための伝統技術) was inscribed on the Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in December 2020. It covers 17 selected conservation techniques [V].

**R01-009** — **Prefer measured survey and repair-report evidence over manual proportions** whenever you are reproducing, repairing or dating a specific historic building. Use the manual only to explain the pattern the survey reveals. *(Must)*
*Why:* Real buildings depart from the manuals: bays adjusted to site, members sized to available timber, later repairs. Repair reports record what is really there, including joints that are hidden until dismantling.

**R01-010** — When a repair report gives a joint or dimension, **note which campaign** (original construction, a named earlier repair, or the modern repair) the detail belongs to. *(Must)*
*Why:* Buildings of Nara or Kamakura date have usually been repaired several times. A joint "found in" a 7th-century building may be a 17th-century replacement (→ Ch. 16).

### 3.4 Architectural Institute of Japan (AIJ) and reference compilations

- **『日本建築史図集』** — 日本建築学会 (ed.), 彰国社 (Shōkokusha) [V]. A compact atlas of plans, sections and photographs of key historic buildings, organised by period. First published in the mid-20th century; the 新訂第三版 (3rd revised edition, 2011) is current [P: edition year]. It is the standard teaching atlas.
- **『建築大辞典』** — 彰国社, 2nd ed. 1993 [V]. A comprehensive architectural dictionary, including traditional terms.
- **『日本建築史基礎資料集成』** — 太田博太郎 et al. (eds.), 中央公論美術出版 [V as series]. A multi-volume series of measured drawings and descriptions of historic buildings arranged by building type (shrines, Buddhist halls, pagodas, shoin, tea houses, etc.). It is the best published survey data after the repair reports.
- **AIJ Library hinagata-bon collection** [V]. More than 100 donated pattern books (1700–1914), partly available through the AIJ digital archive.

## 4. Modern Japanese practitioner and scholarly books

This is a selective list. Japanese publishers (彰国社, 井上書院, オーム社, 学芸出版社, 誠文堂新光社, 鹿島出版会, 中央公論美術出版, 建築知識/エクスナレッジ) have published hundreds of titles on traditional carpentry. Titles marked [V] were confirmed.

### 4.1 History and overview

- **太田博太郎 (Ōta Hirotarō), 『日本建築史序説』**, 彰国社 (first ed. 1947; enlarged editions to the 2000s) [V]. The classic short history of Japanese architecture. It is the conceptual map for placing any source in its period.
- **大河直躬 (Ōkawa Naomi), 『番匠』**, 法政大学出版局 (series ものと人間の文化史, No. 5), 1971 [V/P]. A history of the carpenter's profession: organisation, guilds, the *tōryō* system, the rise of the shogunal families. Essential background for reading the manuals.
- **村松貞次郎 (Muramatsu Teijirō), 『大工道具の歴史』**, 岩波新書, 1973 [V]. A history of carpentry tools and their effect on building.
- **吉川金次 (Yoshikawa Kinji), 『鋸』** (1976) and **『斧・鑿・鉋』** (1984), 法政大学出版局 (ものと人間の文化史) [V/P]. Detailed tool histories based on surviving tools and pictorial evidence.
- **土田一郎 (Tsuchida Ichirō), 『日本の伝統工具』**, 鹿島出版会 (SD選書), 1989 [V]. Photographic study of Japanese tools and their makers.
- **伊藤要太郎 (Itō Yōtarō), 『匠明五巻考』**, 鹿島研究所出版会, 1971 [V]. The commentary volume of the *Shōmei* edition. It explains the kiwari rules of each book.
- **内藤昌 (Naitō Akira), 『愚子見記の研究』**, 井上書院, 1988 [V].

### 4.2 Joints (継手・仕口)

- **住吉寅七・松井源吾 (Sumiyoshi Torashichi & Matsui Gengo), 『木造の継手と仕口』**, 鹿島出版会, 1989 [V]. Sumiyoshi was a master carpenter. Matsui (Waseda University) was a structural engineer who, with others, also studied the strength of joints experimentally. The book is an illustrated catalogue of classical joints, with notes on use. It is the Japanese original of *Wood Joints in Classical Japanese Architecture* (§5).
- **斎藤兵次郎『日本家屋構造』** (1904; §2.5). The historic baseline for house joints.
- **Kenchiku Chishiki (建築知識) special issues and Ekusu Nalejji (エクスナレッジ) books** on 継手・仕口 and 木組み [V as a genre]. These are good modern drawings, of varied depth; check the author's credentials.
- **『木造の詳細』** series, 彰国社 [P]. A multi-volume detail handbook for timber building (structure, house design). Useful for modern hybrid details. Its value for traditional joinery depends on the volume.

### 4.3 Kikujutsu textbooks

- **富樫新三 (Togashi Shinzō), 『図でわかる規矩術』**, オーム社 (2nd ed. 2018) [V]. A standard modern kikujutsu textbook, figure-led.
- **『すぐに役立つ 建築の規矩術』**, オーム社 (5th ed.) [V as title]. Practical kikujutsu for builders.
- **『実用図解 大工さしがね術』**, オーム社 (first published c. 1969; 5th ed. current) [V as title]. A sashigane methods book. It is reportedly the Japanese original behind Nakahara's English *Japanese Joinery* (see §5); that link is [P].
- **大工道具研究会 (ed.), 『図解 規矩術の基礎と実践 — 曲尺の使い方の基礎から応用までを折り紙でわかりやすく解説』**, 誠文堂新光社, 2015 [V]. Teaches kikujutsu with folded-paper models, which is a good bridge for beginners.
- **Vocational training textbooks (職業訓練教材)** on 大工 and 規矩術, published for public vocational schools [V as a genre]. Systematic, conservative and well illustrated. They are the best source for **standard modern Japanese marking conventions and symbols** (→ Ch. 02 §7).

### 4.4 Master carpenters' own voices

- **西岡常一 (Nishioka Tsunekazu), 『木に学べ — 法隆寺・薬師寺の美』**, 小学館, 1988 (paperback later) [V]. Reflections of the last hereditary Hōryū-ji master carpenter (1908–1995): timber selection ("buy the mountain, not the tree"), the life of wood, Asuka- and Hakuhō-period techniques, the yariganna.
- **西岡常一・小川三夫・塩野米松, 『木のいのち木のこころ』** (天・地・人, 3 vols.), 草思社, 1993–1994; 新潮文庫 edition 2005 [V]. Interviews with Nishioka and his only live-in apprentice Ogawa Mitsuo. Ogawa's volume describes apprenticeship at Ikaruga Kōsha.
- **西岡常一, 『宮大工棟梁・西岡常一「口伝」の重み』**, 日本経済新聞出版社 (日経ビジネス人文庫), 2008 [V]. Nishioka's account of the orally transmitted precepts of the Hōryū-ji carpenters, the **口伝 kuden**, such as "assemble the timbers according to their growth habit" (木は生育の方位のままに使え). → Ch. 03.
- **小川三夫 (Ogawa Mitsuo)**, e.g. 『不揃いの木を組む』 (草思社, c. 2001) [P] and, with 塩野米松, 『棟梁』 (文藝春秋, c. 2008) [P]. On training carpenters and organising temple builds.

**R01-011** — Read master carpenters' memoirs and *kuden* collections as **statements of principle and judgement**, and cross-check any numerical claim in them against drawings or surveys. *(Should)*
*Why:* These books transmit the reasoning behind practice, which manuals rarely do. They are oral testimony, however, often recorded by interviewers, and they compress numbers.

### 4.5 Takenaka Carpentry Tools Museum publications

The **竹中大工道具館** (Takenaka Carpentry Tools Museum, Kobe; §7) publishes exhibition catalogues, a research bulletin (研究紀要) and books on tools, tool-smiths and carpentry techniques [V as publisher]. The title 「大工道具の世界」 ("The World of Carpentry Tools") given in the brief could not be pinned to a specific publication and year [U]. The museum's catalogues are the most authoritative illustrated sources on tools (→ Ch. 04).

## 5. Western-language literature

### 5.1 Joinery-specific

- **Kiyosi Seike, *The Art of Japanese Joinery*.** Translated and adapted by Yuriko Yobuko and Rebecca M. Davis. Weatherhill / Tankosha, 1977 [V]. A short, photographic introduction to about 40 joints, with photographs of disassembled joints and axonometrics. It gives the best first impression of the spatial logic of the joints. It has almost no dimensions.
- **Torashichi Sumiyoshi & Gengo Matsui, *Wood Joints in Classical Japanese Architecture*.** Kajima Institute Publishing, 1991 [V]. English version of 『木造の継手と仕口』 (1989). A catalogue of classical tsugite and shiguchi with isometric drawings and notes on use. It is **the most useful English catalogue for naming and identifying joints** (→ Ch. 07, Ch. 08).
- **Yasuo Nakahara, *Japanese Joinery: A Handbook for Joiners and Carpenters*.** Translated by Koichi Paul Nii. Hartley & Marks, 1983 [V]. A practical handbook with layout by sashigane, joints, roof framing and eaves. It is dense, and translated from a Japanese sashigane/carpentry manual. It is the best English source for **sashigane-based layout**, but the English terminology is sometimes awkward.
- **Hideo Sato & Yasuo (printed "Yasua") Nakahara, *The Complete Japanese Joinery*.** Translated by Koichi Paul Nii. Hartley & Marks, 1995 [V]. Two parts: Sato's "Japanese Woodworking" (tools, techniques) and Nakahara's "Japanese Joinery" (a reissue of the 1983 book). About 400 pages.
- **Wolfram Graubner, *Holzverbindungen: Gegenüberstellungen japanischer und europäischer Lösungen*.** Deutsche Verlags-Anstalt, 1986. English: ***Encyclopedia of Wood Joints***, Taunton Press, 1992 [V]. A side-by-side comparison of Japanese and European joints, organised by joint function. Excellent drawings. The Japanese joints are drawn from secondary sources.
- **Klaus Zwerger, *Das Holz und seine Verbindungen*** / ***Wood and Wood Joints: Building Traditions of Europe and Japan*** (later editions add China). Birkhäuser, 1997; revised and expanded editions 2011 and later [V/P: later edition years]. A comparative, scholarly treatment of timber-building traditions and joints, with good analysis of why joints take their forms.
- **Jay van Arsdale, *Shoji: How to Design, Build, and Install Japanese Screens*.** Kodansha International, 1988 [V]. The standard English workshop manual for shōji and kumiko (→ Ch. 14). A separate Van Arsdale book titled *Japanese Joinery* could not be confirmed [U].
- **Toshio Odate, *Making Shoji*.** Linden Publishing, 2000 [P]. A companion to his tools book.

### 5.2 Tools and the craft

- **Toshio Odate, *Japanese Woodworking Tools: Their Tradition, Spirit and Use*.** Taunton Press, 1984 [V]. By a carpenter trained in Japan who then worked in the U.S. It covers the use, setting-up and care of each tool, with the cultural attitudes of the trade. Essential for → Ch. 04.
- **William H. Coaldrake, *The Way of the Carpenter: Tools and Japanese Architecture*.** Weatherhill / Heibonsha, 1990 [V]. A scholarly account of the carpenter's tools, organisation and ideology, with a good discussion of kiwari and *Shōmei*. It is the best single English account of **how the manuals fit into the history of the craft**.
- **Azby Brown, *The Genius of Japanese Carpentry: An Account of a Temple's Construction*.** Kodansha International, 1989. Revised edition: *The Genius of Japanese Carpentry: Secrets of an Ancient Woodworking Craft*, Tuttle, 2013 [V]. It documents the reconstruction at Yakushi-ji (Nara) under Nishioka Tsunekazu: timber procurement, layout, joints, raising, with Brown's own drawings. The best English **case study of a modern miyadaiku project**.

### 5.3 Architecture, houses and roof structure

- **Heino Engel, *The Japanese House: A Tradition for Contemporary Architecture*.** Tuttle, 1964 [V]. An analytic study of the house: the ken module, tatami, kiwari, structure, fittings. Its analysis of **shinshin-sei vs. tatami-wari planning** is still standard (→ Ch. 02 §2).
- **Heino Engel, *Measure and Construction of the Japanese House*.** Tuttle, 1985 [V]. A condensed, drawing-focused volume on module and construction.
- **Mary Neighbour Parent, *The Roof in Japanese Buddhist Architecture*.** Weatherhill / Kajima, 1983 [V]. A detailed study of roof and eaves structure (hanegi, noyane, bracket sets) in temple architecture, with extensive terminology. Essential for → Ch. 11 and Ch. 12. Parent is also the compiler of JAANUS (§6).
- **William H. Coaldrake, *Architecture and Authority in Japan*.** Routledge, 1996 [V]. On architecture as political expression, including Ise, Tōdai-ji, castles, Tokugawa mausolea and the role of the shogunal carpenters.
- **Kazuo Nishi & Kazuo Hozumi, *What Is Japanese Architecture? A Survey of Traditional Japanese Architecture*.** Translated by H. Mack Horton. Kodansha International, 1985 [V]. A short, drawing-rich survey by building type. The best **one-volume visual overview** of temple, shrine, residence and minka forms.
- **Teiji Itoh, *Traditional Domestic Architecture of Japan*.** Weatherhill / Heibonsha, 1972 (Heibonsha Survey of Japanese Art, vol. 21) [V]. On minka, machiya and residential forms.
- **Chūji Kawashima, *Minka: Traditional Houses of Rural Japan*.** Kodansha International, 1986 [V]. Measured drawings and descriptions of farmhouses by region (→ Ch. 13).
- **Norman F. Carver Jr., *Japanese Folkhouses*.** Documan Press, 1984 [V]. A photographic survey of minka.
- **Edward S. Morse, *Japanese Homes and Their Surroundings*.** Ticknor, 1886 [V]. An eyewitness record of Meiji-era houses and carpentry, with many sketches of details, tools and fittings. Valuable as a **dated observation** of 1870s–1880s practice.
- **Tetsuro Yoshida, *Das japanische Wohnhaus*.** Wasmuth, 1935 [V]. The classic German-language account of the Japanese house, written by a Japanese architect for European readers. It was translated into English (*The Japanese House and Garden*, 1955) [P].
- **Kenzō Tange & Noboru Kawazoe, *Ise: Prototype of Japanese Architecture*.** MIT Press, 1965 [V]. On the Ise shrines and periodic rebuilding (→ Ch. 16).
- **Mira Locher, *Traditional Japanese Architecture: An Exploration of Elements and Forms*.** Tuttle, 2010 [V]. A clear modern introduction organised by building element.
- **Arata Isozaki, *Japan-ness in Architecture*.** MIT Press, 2006 [V]. Theoretical essays (Ise, Katsura, Tōdai-ji). Useful for ideas, not for technique.

### 5.4 Practitioner web resources

- **Chris Hall, *The Carpentry Way*** (blog, since c. 2008) [V]. A North-American carpenter trained in Japanese methods. Long, detailed essays on kikujutsu, joint design, sashigane layout, and translations and analysis of Japanese texts. The best free English source on **kikujutsu in practice**. It is a personal blog, so check claims against the primary Japanese texts.

**R01-012** — **Do not take joint dimensions from Western picture books** (e.g. Seike, Graubner) as working dimensions. Use them for identification and understanding. Take proportions from Japanese technical sources or from measured examples, and scale them by the rules in → Ch. 06. *(Must)*
*Why:* Many Western books draw joints at display proportions, simplify hidden faces, or translate a regional variant as "the" joint.

**R01-013** — When a Western book gives a Japanese term, **verify the term in Japanese script** in a Japanese dictionary or JAANUS before adopting it. *(Should)*
*Why:* Romanisations in older English books are inconsistent (missing macrons, *rendaku* errors, invented compounds), and some "Japanese" names in English literature are translators' coinages.

## 7. Museums, schools and living-tradition bodies

### 7.1 Museums

- **竹中大工道具館 Takenaka Carpentry Tools Museum** (Kobe) [V]. Founded 1984 in Nakayamate, Kobe, by the Takenaka Corporation, and moved in 2014 to a new building near Shin-Kobe station. It is Japan's only museum dedicated to carpentry tools. The collection covers tools, tool-smiths' work, full-size joint and structure models (including a full-scale temple bracket set), and tea-house construction. It publishes catalogues and research bulletins and runs workshops. **The primary institutional reference for tools (→ Ch. 04) and for joint mock-ups (→ Ch. 07, Ch. 08).**
- **Open-air architecture museums** with dismantled and re-erected buildings. Examples are the Japan Open-Air Folk House Museum (日本民家園, Kawasaki), Edo-Tokyo Open Air Architectural Museum (江戸東京たてもの園), Meiji-mura (博物館明治村, Inuyama) and Shikoku-mura (四国村). They let you see minka framing at close range (→ Ch. 13) [V].
- **Nara National Research Institute for Cultural Properties** (奈良文化財研究所) and **Tokyo National Research Institute for Cultural Properties** (東京文化財研究所) [V]. Research and publications on ancient architecture and conservation.

### 7.2 Conservation and preservation bodies

- **公益財団法人 文化財建造物保存技術協会 (Bunkenkyō)**, Japanese Association for Conservation of Architectural Monuments [V]. Founded 1971. It supervises and designs repairs of designated buildings, trains conservation architects and craftsmen, and writes many repair reports.
- **一般社団法人 全国社寺等屋根工事技術保存会** (National Association for the Preservation of Roofing Techniques for Temples and Shrines) [V as body; founding year P]. The recognised preservation body for cypress-bark, shingle and related roofing techniques.
- **一般社団法人 日本伝統建築技術保存会** (Japan Association for the Preservation of Traditional Architectural Techniques) [V as body; P for details]. Based in Kyoto. A recognised preservation body for traditional building techniques, running training for carpenters and other trades in conservation work.
- Other selected-technique preservation bodies exist for thatch, tiles, plaster, tatami, fittings and polychromy. The Agency for Cultural Affairs publishes the current list.

### 7.3 Firms and apprenticeship traditions

- **金剛組 Kongō Gumi** (Osaka) [V]. A temple-building firm traditionally dated to **578 CE**, associated with the founding of Shitennō-ji. It has been called the world's oldest continuously operating company. Since 2006 it has been part of the Takamatsu Construction Group, and it continues as a specialist miyadaiku company.
- **Takenaka Corporation (竹中工務店)** [V]. Descends from a line of shrine and temple carpenters (traditionally dated to 1610 in Nagoya). It founded the tools museum.
- **鵤工舎 Ikaruga Kōsha** [V]. Founded in 1977 by **小川三夫 Ogawa Mitsuo**, Nishioka Tsunekazu's apprentice, in Ikaruga (Nara). Incorporated in 1990 with headquarters in Shioya, Tochigi Prefecture. It trains miyadaiku through live-in apprenticeship: sharpening first, then assisting, with no manuals. It has produced many of today's temple carpenters.
- **Miyadaiku (宮大工) firms and tōryō lineages** attached to particular temples and regions. Their practice is mostly unpublished. Access is by apprenticeship or through repair-report documentation.
- **Vocational schools and colleges** [P]. Traditional carpentry is taught at public vocational training schools (職業能力開発校) and at some private colleges, for example 職藝学院 (Shokugei Gakuin, Toyama) and 岐阜県立森林文化アカデミー (Gifu Academy of Forest Science and Culture). Programmes change, so check before relying on this list.
- **Craft-competition and hand-plane societies** such as 削ろう会 (*Kezuroukai*, a hand-plane shavings competition held in Japan since the late 1990s, with overseas offshoots) [P]. They show the living standard of tool practice.

## 8. How to read historical kiwari texts

This section gives the working method for extracting usable information from *Shōmei*, the Kennin-ji books, hinagata-bon and similar texts. Chapter 02 §3 explains the kiwari system itself, and Chapter 12 applies it to bracket sets.

### 8.1 What a kiwari text is (and is not)

A kiwari text is a **chain of proportional derivations**. It starts from one or two governing dimensions and derives every other member from them. Typically:

- **Governing dimension A: the bay (柱間 *hashira-ma*).** Usually the central bay (中の間 *naka-no-ma*) of a hall or gate. In later texts it is expressed as an integer number of rafter spaces (枝 *shi*; → Ch. 02 §3.4).
- **Governing dimension B: the column diameter or width (柱径 / 柱太さ).** Often itself given as a fraction of the central bay (e.g. "column = one-tenth of the central bay" is a typical form of statement).
- **Derived members.** Tie-beams (貫, 頭貫), bracket parts (大斗, 肘木, 巻斗), purlins (桁), rafters (垂木), eaves projection (軒の出), ridge, and so on. Each is given as a fraction or multiple of B, or of another derived member. A chain such as "rafter width = x of column; gap = y of rafter; eaves projection = n rafter spaces" is typical.

A kiwari text is **not**:
- a structural calculation;
- a set of absolute dimensions, unless the text explicitly says so for a particular example building;
- a record of any one real building (though some examples may be modelled on real buildings);
- complete. Joints, fastening and layout geometry are mostly absent, and were left to the carpenter and to kikujutsu.

### 8.2 Units inside kiwari texts

The most common mistake is to misread the proportional vocabulary as absolute units.

| Expression | Typical meaning inside a kiwari text | Trap |
|---|---|---|
| 〜の一割 (*ichi-wari*) | one-tenth of the stated reference | Not "10 %" of an unstated quantity. Identify the reference. |
| 〜の八分 (*hachi-bu*) | 0.8 of the reference (eight-tenths) | **分 here is a tenth of the reference**, not the absolute unit 1 bu ≈ 3.03 mm. |
| 〜の三分五厘 | 0.35 of the reference | 厘 = hundredths of the reference. |
| 本 / 枚 (*hon / mai*) | a count of members or rafter widths | Check whether it counts rafters or spaces. |
| 枝 (*shi*) | one rafter spacing (rafter + gap) | A module, not a length. Its length depends on the rafter size chosen. |
| 〜寸 / 〜尺 (*sun / shaku*) | absolute dimensions, usually in a worked example | Usually in the kanejaku of the period (→ Ch. 02 §1). Check for regional or period variants before converting to mm. |

**R01-015** — When reading a kiwari statement, **first identify the reference member** ("of what?") and write the rule as an explicit ratio, e.g. *nageshi height = 0.9 × column width*. Only then convert to absolute units. *(Must)*
*Why:* 分 and 厘 in kiwari are usually decimal fractions of the reference, not absolute *bu* and *rin*. Reading "柱の八分" as "8 bu" (≈24 mm) instead of 0.8 × column gives nonsense.

**R01-016** — **Build the whole derivation chain** (base → first-order members → second-order members) in a table before using any single figure from it. *(Should)*
*Why:* Figures are often given relative to an intermediate member. An error at one link propagates down the chain, and the table exposes missing links.

### 8.3 The base module

- In **ancient and medieval** practice the governing quantity was usually the **bay span**. Column size and other members followed from it, and the rafter spacing was adjusted to fit the bay.
- From the **Kamakura–Muromachi** period onward, the **rafter-spacing module (枝割 *shiwari*)** increasingly governed. Bays were set as whole numbers of rafter spaces, so that bracket sets and rafters line up (the six-rafter bracket rule, 六枝掛 *rokushi-gake*, originated in the Kamakura period; → Ch. 02 §3.4, Ch. 12).
- In **residential** (shoin, sukiya) kiwari the governing quantity is usually the **column width**, with the bay fixed by the ken module or the tatami (→ Ch. 02 §2). The *Den'ya-shū* of *Shōmei* is the classic source.
- **Early-modern kiwari tends to thinner members** than those found in surviving ancient buildings. This reflects changed aesthetics and timber supply. Scaling an Edo kiwari to reconstruct an ancient building therefore gives members that are too slender.

**R01-017** — Do not **apply a kiwari from one period or building type to another** (e.g. Edo temple kiwari to a Nara-period reconstruction, or temple kiwari to a shoin residence). *(Must)*
*Why:* The base module and the expected slenderness differ between periods and types, so the ratios do not transfer.

### 8.4 What hinagata show

A hinagata is a **model drawing with dimensions**, usually an elevation, sometimes a plan or section. It shows one instance of a pattern at a stated size. Carpenters used it:

- as a **reference for form and proportion**, scaling it up or down;
- as a **catalogue** of accepted types, for discussing a commission with a client;
- as **training** material.

Hinagata do **not** normally show joints, hidden structure or layout geometry. "Scaling" a hinagata was not a simple photographic enlargement. The carpenter re-derived the members from the new base module with the kiwari rules, and some members (e.g. rafter sizes, bracket parts) were rounded to standard sizes.

**R01-018** — When scaling a hinagata, **re-derive the members from the kiwari chain at the new base size, then round to available timber and check structurally**. Never scale all dimensions linearly. *(Must)*
*Why:* Some members scale with the bay (lengths). Others scale with the column (sections). Others are fixed by the rafter module or by timber availability. Linear scaling breaks these relations, and with them the structural logic.

### 8.5 Secrecy and oral tradition (口伝 *kuden*)

- Family manuscripts were **deliberately incomplete**. Key numbers or methods were often marked "口伝" ("transmitted orally") and left out. A text saying 「口伝アリ」 ("there is an oral teaching") means the written rule is not the whole rule.
- Some manuscripts contain **deliberate simplifications** for pupils at a certain level. Others include ritual dimensions (auspicious lengths, prohibited dimensions) mixed with technical ones.
- Transmission was by **copying**. Copy errors in numbers are common, especially between similar characters (e.g. 二 and 三, 六 and 八 in cursive).

**R01-019** — Where a manuscript defers to *kuden* or where a figure is implausible, **mark the rule as incomplete and look for the missing information in surveyed buildings of the same school**, not by guesswork. *(Must)*

**R01-020** — Where two copies of the same manuscript differ in a number, **prefer the reading that agrees with surveyed buildings**, and record the variant. *(Should)*

### 8.6 Schools (流派 *ryūha*) and their variation

| School / tradition | Base | Character | Main sources |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Heinouchi (平内) school** | Edo shogunal *daitōryō* family, from Kii | Comprehensive, all building types; shoin residences documented in detail | *Shōmei* (1608); Heinouchi Masaomi's kikujutsu books (1833, 1848) |
| **Kennin-ji school (建仁寺流)** | Kōra (甲良) family, Edo shogunal *daitōryō* | Systematic; strong on Zen-style (禅宗様) architecture; widely copied in eastern Japan | *Kenninji-ha kadensho* (Kōra family) |
| **Kyoto / Nakai** | 京都大工頭 Nakai family | Administration of large Kyoto projects; many drawings | Nakai family archives |
| **Nara / Hōryū-ji carpenters** | Nara | Old lineage; anthology of older kiwari | *Gushikenki* (incl. *Sandai-kan*) |
| **Shitennō-ji school (四天王寺流)** | Osaka; traditionally linked to Kongō Gumi | Old Kinai tradition | *Kokudaki no chūmon* (attribution unverified) |
| **Tachikawa school (立川流)** | Edo, then Suwa (Nagano) | Shrine building and elaborate carving | *Shōka hitsuyōki*; Tachikawa pattern books |
| **Ōsumi school (大隅流)** | Suwa and elsewhere | Shrine carpentry, rival of the Suwa Tachikawa | Local manuscripts [P] |

Differences between schools show up in:
- the **choice of base module** (bay vs. column vs. rafter spacing);
- **specific ratios**, e.g. column-to-bay ratio, eaves projection, depth of tie-beams, bracket proportions;
- **naming** of members, since the same part may have different names in different schools;
- the **kikujutsu method**. The existence of two separately designated selected conservation techniques for 古式規矩 and 近世規矩 shows that even the geometry was not unified.

**R01-021** — When restoring or extending a building, **identify the school or regional tradition of the original builders** (from inscriptions, repair reports, regional practice) and use the kiwari and conventions of that tradition, not a generic one. *(Must, for conservation; Should, for new work in a regional idiom)*

**R01-022** — **Never mix the kiwari of two schools within one building.** *(Should)*
*Why:* A kiwari is an internally consistent system. Mixing produces mismatched members (e.g. brackets from one system under rafters spaced by another), which experienced eyes read at once as wrong.

### 8.7 Cross-checking against measured surveys

The standard method for using a kiwari source responsibly:

1. **Select** a kiwari source of the right period, school and building type.
2. **Tabulate** its derivation chain (R01-016).
3. **Find** two or more surveyed buildings of the same type and period (repair reports, 『日本建築史基礎資料集成』).
4. **Compute** the ratios actually found in those buildings (e.g. column/central bay, rafter/column).
5. **Compare.** Where the survey and the manual agree, the rule is confirmed. Where they differ, note the range.
6. **Design** from the range, choosing the value appropriate to rank, scale and timber.
7. **Check** structure independently (→ Ch. 17).

**R01-023** — **Cross-check every kiwari rule against at least two measured surveys** of comparable buildings before relying on it for design or restoration. Report the result as a range, not a single number. *(Must)*
*Why:* Manuals present ideals. Surveys show what builders actually did. The overlap is the defensible practice.

**R01-024** — **Distinguish proportion (appearance) from strength (structure).** A kiwari that looks right does not prove structural adequacy, and a structural calculation does not make a building look right. Both checks are required. *(Must)*

## 9. Recommended reading path

### Stage 1 — Orientation (beginner)

1. **Nishi & Hozumi, *What Is Japanese Architecture?*** Building types and vocabulary, visually.
2. **Seike, *The Art of Japanese Joinery*.** The spatial idea of joints.
3. **Engel, *The Japanese House*** (the sections on module, measure and structure). The ken/tatami module (→ Ch. 02).
4. **Odate, *Japanese Woodworking Tools*.** Tools and attitude (→ Ch. 04).
5. **Brown, *The Genius of Japanese Carpentry*.** A whole project from forest to raising.
6. **JAANUS**, used continually as a dictionary.

### Stage 2 — Working knowledge (intermediate)

7. **Sumiyoshi & Matsui, *Wood Joints in Classical Japanese Architecture*** (or the Japanese original). Joint identification and use.
8. **Nakahara, *Japanese Joinery*** / **Sato & Nakahara, *The Complete Japanese Joinery*.** Sashigane layout and practical joinery.
9. **Coaldrake, *The Way of the Carpenter*.** History of tools, organisation and the manuals.
10. **Parent, *The Roof in Japanese Buddhist Architecture*.** Roof and eaves structure.
11. A Japanese **kikujutsu textbook** (e.g. 『図解 規矩術の基礎と実践』 then 『図でわかる規矩術』). Start the geometry (→ Ch. 05).
12. **Chris Hall, *The Carpentry Way*.** Worked kikujutsu problems in English.
13. **Western comparative**: Zwerger, *Wood and Wood Joints*; Graubner, *Encyclopedia of Wood Joints*.

### Stage 3 — Advanced (Japanese-reading practitioner or researcher)

14. **斎藤兵次郎『日本家屋構造』** (1904). The historical baseline for house carpentry.
15. **『匠明』 with 『匠明五巻考』.** Kiwari at its source.
16. **『愚子見記の研究』** and the **Kennin-ji school** texts. Other schools.
17. **Heinouchi Masaomi's kikujutsu books** (『匠家矩術要解』, 『矩術新書』) via digital collections. Edo geometry at its most developed.
18. **Repair reports** (修理工事報告書) for buildings of the type you are working on. Real practice.
19. **Current standards**: grey book, JASS 11, the 2004 and 2019 traditional-construction seismic manuals, and the current BSL and notifications (→ Ch. 17).
20. **Nishioka's and Ogawa's books.** The judgement behind the rules.

**R01-025** — **Pair every "reading" source with a "doing" exercise.** For example, after the Seike/Sumiyoshi stage, cut the joints at small scale; after the kikujutsu textbook, lay out a hip rafter at full size. *(Should)*
*Why:* Japanese carpentry literature assumes the reader has hands-on experience. Without it, the texts are routinely misunderstood.
