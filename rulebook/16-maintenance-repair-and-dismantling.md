# Chapter 16 — Maintenance, Repair and Dismantling (維持・修理・解体 / iji, shūri, kaitai)

**Scope.** This chapter treats the traditional Japanese timber building as a system designed to be maintained, taken apart, repaired and re-erected over centuries. It covers the philosophy and the grades of repair (from re-roofing to full dismantling), the Ise Jingū rebuilding cycle as the extreme case of planned renewal, condition assessment and documentation, the principal repair joints and operations (post-base replacement, inlays, splicing, lifting, straightening, moving), dismantling order and member recording, a preventive maintenance calendar, and the salvage and reuse of old timber. Joint geometry is in → Ch. 07 and → Ch. 08; the assembly order that dismantling reverses is in → Ch. 15; member numbering (番付 *bantsuke*) is in → Ch. 02; timber properties and decay resistance in → Ch. 03; modern code and structural issues of retrofitting in → Ch. 17.

## 1. Philosophy: buildings made to be repaired

The survival of Japanese timber buildings for more than 1,300 years (the Hōryū-ji Western Precinct's Kondō and five-storey pagoda being the canonical examples) is not due to imperishable materials. It is due to a building system in which:
1. **Every member can be removed.** Joints are interlocking, pinned and wedged (→ Ch. 06, Ch. 09) rather than glued or nailed; a frame can be taken apart member by member without destroying the joints.
2. **Sacrificial parts protect permanent parts.** Roof coverings, bargeboard ends, rafter ends, board cladding, nure-en, post bases and sills are expected to decay and be replaced, while the core frame — posts, beams, bracket sets — survives under the roof.
3. **Repair is expected and cyclical.** Roofs are re-covered on a cycle of decades; frames are dismantled and re-erected on a cycle of centuries. The craft knowledge is maintained by doing these repairs (→ §3 on Ise).
4. **New wood is joined to old wood with the same joinery** used for new construction, so that a repair is not a foreign intervention but a continuation of the building's own logic.

**R16-001** — Repair **should** be done with the joinery system of the original building (interlocking timber joints, pins, wedges), and new work **must not** make the frame less dismantlable than it was. *Why:* the building's long-term survival depends on the next generation of carpenters being able to take it apart again.

**R16-002** — Minimum intervention: an original member **should** be kept if it can be repaired to carry its load; it **should** be replaced only when repair is impossible or would leave less original material than replacement. *Why:* each original member carries historical information (tool marks, inscriptions, joinery evidence) and material authenticity; this principle underlies modern Japanese conservation practice.

## 2. Grades of repair and their cycles

Japanese conservation practice (as organised under the Law for the Protection of Cultural Properties, 文化財保護法 *Bunkazai Hogo Hō*, 1950, and carried out by the prefectural boards and specialised bodies such as the Japanese Association for Conservation of Architectural Monuments, 文化財建造物保存技術協会 *Bunkenkyō*, founded 1971) distinguishes the following grades of repair, from least to most invasive:

| Grade | Japanese | Scope | Typical interval (representative) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Routine maintenance | 維持修理 / 日常管理 *iji-shūri* | Cleaning gutters, replacing broken tiles, re-plastering patches, tightening wedges | Annual to 5 years |
| Partial repair | 部分修理 *bubun-shūri* | Localised replacement (a post base, a sill, rafter ends, an eave corner) | As needed |
| Roof re-covering | 屋根葺替 *yane fukikae* | Removing and renewing the roof covering, repairing substrate and rafter ends | See table below |
| Paint/coating renewal | 塗装修理 *tosō-shūri* | Re-coating lacquer, bengara, vermilion (丹塗り) on shrines and temples | c. 20–50 years |
| Half-dismantling repair | 半解体修理 *han-kaitai-shūri* | Roof and roof frame removed; upper part of the structure dismantled; the lower frame (posts, lower beams) straightened and repaired in place | c. 100–200 years |
| Full dismantling repair | 解体修理 *kaitai-shūri* | Building taken down to the foundations, every member numbered, repaired, re-erected; foundation reset | c. 200–400 years for major temple buildings (highly variable) |

The intervals for half and full dismantling are broad generalisations; they depend on climate, maintenance history, roofing type and fortune (fire, earthquake, typhoon).

### 2.1 Roof re-covering cycles (representative)
| Roofing | Japanese | Re-covering interval | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Thatch (susuki, reed) | 茅葺 *kayabuki* | c. 20–40 years (full); partial "insertion" repair every few years | Longer where the roof dries well (steep, smoke-cured in houses with an irori); shorter in shade/humidity. See 差し茅 §5.9. |
| Hinoki bark | 檜皮葺 *hiwadabuki* | c. 30–40 years | Bark harvested from living hinoki by specialists (原皮師 *motokawashi*) at c. 8–10+ year intervals from the same tree. |
| Wood shingle | 杮葺 *kokerabuki* | c. 20–30 years | Thin split sawara/sugi shingles, bamboo-nailed. |
| Thick shingle | 栩葺 *tochibuki* / 木賊葺 *tokusabuki* | c. 25–40 years | Thicker shingles; longer lived. |
| Clay tile | 本瓦葺 *hongawarabuki* | Re-laying (葺き直し *fukinaoshi*) c. 60–100 years, with partial tile replacement in between | Tiles reused where sound; mud bedding (葺き土) and substrate renewed. |
| Copper sheet | 銅板葺 *dōbanbuki* | c. 60+ years | Modern replacement roofing for many shrines. |

**R16-003** — The roof covering **must** be maintained on its cycle and **must not** be allowed to fail; any leak **must** be stopped within the season it is found. *Why:* the roof is the sacrificial skin protecting the frame; a single season of leakage into a joint begins rot that can require a half-dismantling repair.

**R16-004** — Re-covering **should** be used as the occasion to inspect and repair the roof frame, rafter ends, eave corners (隅 *sumi*) and hanegi (→ Ch. 11), since these are only accessible when the covering is off. *Why:* economy of access; hidden decay concentrates in these zones.

### 2.2 Case examples (for orientation)
- **Hōryū-ji, Shōwa Great Repair** (昭和大修理 *Shōwa daishūri*): begun 1934, continuing through the war, completed with a ceremony in 1985; dozens of buildings in the precinct were repaired, many by full dismantling. During the dismantling repair of the Kondō, a fire in January 1949 damaged the lower storey's posts and wall paintings; the loss was a direct impetus for the 1950 Law for the Protection of Cultural Properties. Master carpenter Nishioka Tsunekazu (西岡常一, 1908–1995) worked on these repairs.
- **Tōshōdai-ji Kondō** (Nara): full dismantling repair c. 2000–2009 (the "Heisei Great Repair").
- **Yakushi-ji East Pagoda** (Nara): first full dismantling repair in its history, begun 2009 and completed c. 2020–21.
- **Himeji Castle**: a full dismantling repair of the main keep in the Shōwa period (c. 1956–1964), and a roof and wall repair in the Heisei period (c. 2009–2015).

Each major repair is published as a repair report (修理工事報告書 *shūri kōji hōkokusho*), which is the primary source for members replaced, traces found and decisions taken; later carpenters rely on these reports.

**R16-005** — Every repair beyond routine maintenance **should** be documented in a written report with drawings, photographs and a list of replaced and repaired members, deposited with the owner and (for designated properties) the authorities. *Why:* the next repair, perhaps a century later, depends on knowing what was done and why.

## 3. Ise Jingū shikinen sengū (式年遷宮)

### 3.1 What it is
At Ise Jingū (伊勢神宮), the principal sanctuaries of the Inner Shrine (内宮 *Naikū*) and Outer Shrine (外宮 *Gekū*), together with the fourteen subsidiary sanctuaries (別宮 *betsugū*), are rebuilt on an adjacent, alternate site every twenty years, and the deity is ceremonially transferred to the new sanctuary. The custom is traditionally dated to the reign of Emperor Tenmu, with the first sengū recorded in 690 (Empress Jitō) for the Naikū. It was interrupted for over a century during the Sengoku period and resumed in the late 16th century. The 62nd sengū took place in 2013; the 63rd is scheduled for 2033, and its preparatory rituals had begun by 2025.

### 3.2 What is rebuilt
- The main sanctuaries (正殿 *shōden*) and their treasure halls (宝殿) and fences and gates, in the 神明造 (*shinmei-zukuri*) style (→ Ch. 12): unpainted hinoki, earth-fast posts (掘立柱 *horitate-bashira*), thatched (萱葺) gabled roofs with free-standing ridge-supporting posts (棟持柱 *munamochi-bashira*).
- The Uji Bridge (宇治橋) over the Isuzu River, rebuilt a few years ahead of the sengū.
- Some 1,600 items of sacred vestments and treasures (御装束神宝 *goshōzoku shinpō*), also remade by specialist craftsmen.
- The timber requirement is commonly cited as of the order of 10,000 hinoki trees (c. figure; **flagged**), traditionally from the Kiso forests (木曽) and increasingly from the shrine's own forest (宮域林), managed since the 1920s with a view to supplying future sengū.

### 3.3 The alternate sites
Each sanctuary has two adjacent plots of the same size. While the shrine stands on one, the other lies empty, covered in white stones, with only a small hut (覆屋 *ōiya*) protecting the location of the sacred "heart pillar" (心御柱 *shin-no-mihashira*). The rebuilt shrine is built on the empty plot, so that at the moment of transfer two identical shrines stand side by side; the old one is then dismantled.

### 3.4 Reuse of the old timbers
The dismantled timbers are not discarded. The best-known cycle:
- The **munamochi-bashira** of the old Naikū and Gekū main sanctuaries become the two **torii at either end of the Uji Bridge** (the inner torii from the Naikū, the outer from the Gekū), serving there for about twenty years.
- After that, these torii timbers are reused again as the torii at the **Seki-juku Higashi-oiwake** (関宿 東追分, Mie) and at the **Shichiri-no-watashi** landing at Kuwana (桑名 七里の渡し), respectively.
- Other members are distributed to shrines throughout Japan for rebuilding or repair (in 2013 and after, timbers were sent notably to shrines damaged in the 2011 Tōhoku earthquake and tsunami).

### 3.5 Significance for this rulebook
The sengū is the extreme case of the principle that a building's identity lies in its form and its transmission rather than in the physical material. Its two practical lessons for carpenters are:
1. **Skill transmission:** a twenty-year cycle is approximately one working generation; each carpenter participates in about two sengū in a career, first as apprentice, then as master, so the full knowledge of the building — including its unwritten parts — is transmitted by practice.
2. **Design for replacement:** earth-fast posts and thatch are not durable; rather than making them durable, the system renews them on schedule.

This is not the norm for Japanese buildings in general: temples and most shrines are *repaired*, not rebuilt, and their authenticity is judged by the survival of original material. The two approaches are complementary expressions of the same culture, a point emphasised in the **Nara Document on Authenticity** (1994), drafted at a conference in Nara, which recognised that authenticity must be judged within the cultural context of each heritage, and that in some traditions the continuity of form, technique and function carries authenticity alongside original material.

**R16-006** — In any building type, the craft processes used in repair **should** themselves be preserved and documented as part of the heritage. *Why:* the sengū example — and the 2020 inscription of Japan's "traditional skills, techniques and knowledge for the conservation and transmission of wooden architecture" on UNESCO's Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage — recognises that the skills are as much the heritage as the object.

## 4. Condition assessment and documentation

### 4.1 What to look for
| Defect | Japanese | Where / signs | Typical cause |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rot at post bases | 根腐れ *negusare* | Soft wood when probed with an awl (千枚通し/錐), darkening, fungal fruiting bodies, hollow sound when tapped; worst at the bottom 1–2 shaku of posts, at dodai, and at the ends of sills | Splash-back, ground moisture, blocked underfloor ventilation, plumbing leaks |
| Termite damage | 蟻害 *gigai* (白蟻 *shiroari*) | Mud tubes (蟻道 *gidō*) on foundation stones and posts; galleried wood with an intact surface; swarming in spring (Yamato termite c. April–May; Formosan termite c. June–July) | Moisture, wood-soil contact |
| Wood-boring beetles | ヒラタキクイムシ etc. | Fine powder (frass) and small exit holes in sapwood | Sapwood of hardwoods/bamboo |
| Roof leakage | 雨漏り *amamori* | Water stains on ceilings and rafters, rot at valleys, ridge and eave corners, deformed tiles, moss; rafter ends dark | Failed covering, blocked gutters, slipped tiles |
| Differential settlement | 不同沈下 *fudō chinka* | Out-of-level floors and kamoi (→ Ch. 14 §6), sticking tategu, diagonal cracks in plaster, gaps at the top or bottom of doors | Stones sinking, soil consolidation, earthquake |
| Tilt | 傾き *katamuki* | Posts out of plumb, measured with plumb bob (下げ振り *sagefuri*) or digital level; shōji gaps tapering | Earthquake, wind, settlement, loosened nuki wedges |
| Loose wedges | 楔の緩み | Nuki wedges (貫楔) loose or fallen out; gaps at nuki through-mortises | Shrinkage, cyclic racking |
| Crushed seats (embedment) | めり込み *merikomi* | Crushing of wood perpendicular to grain at beam seats, under post tenons, at nuki bearing faces; permanent set | Overload, earthquake, rot softening |
| Cracked tenons / joints | 仕口の割れ, 枘折れ | Split beam ends at housings, sheared tenons (often found at dismantling) | Earthquake, over-cutting (断面欠損), shrinkage |
| Joint pull-out | 抜け *nuke* | Tenons partly withdrawn from mortises; opened splices | Racking, lateral displacement |
| Sagging eaves | 軒垂れ *nokidare* | Eave line drooping, especially at corners (隅垂れ *sumidare*); hanegi failure | Rot at hanegi fulcrum or tail, overloading, broken counterweight connection (→ Ch. 11) |
| Plaster failure | 壁の剥落, 散り切れ | Delamination, bulging, gap at chiri | Movement, water, age |

### 4.2 Measuring tilt and level
- **Tilt** is measured at each post on two faces with a plumb bob over a known height (e.g. 1 m or the uchinori height), recorded as a ratio (e.g. 10 mm over 1,000 mm = 1/100).
- **Level** is surveyed at the top of the dodai/ashigatame, at the shikii and at the kamoi of each bay, using a water level (水盛り *mizumori* in the traditional method, now a laser level), relative to a fixed datum.
- Results are plotted on a plan (tilt arrows at each post; contour of floor levels) to diagnose whether the building is tilting as a whole (settlement of one side) or racking (frame deformation).

Representative thresholds: many Japanese post-earthquake damage-assessment schemes for timber houses treat residual tilt of around 1/60 as significant and around 1/20 as severe; in conservation, any tilt that makes tategu inoperable or visibly distorts the frame is usually corrected at the next repair. (**Flagged:** thresholds depend on the scheme and edition; → Ch. 17.)

**R16-007** — Tilt and level surveys **must** precede any decision on lifting (揚げ前) or straightening (建て起こし), and **should** be repeated after the work. *Why:* the operations are chosen according to whether the defect is settlement or racking; the after-survey verifies the result.

### 4.3 Documentation
- **Measured survey** (実測 *jissoku*): plans, sections and elevations drawn from measurement, with the member grid identified by the bantsuke (→ Ch. 02) and every post and beam labelled.
- **Field notebooks** (野帳 *yachō*): the surveyor's working sketches with dimensions, taken on site; retained as primary records.
- **Photographs**: every elevation and interior, every joint before and during dismantling, every defect, with scale bars and member labels.
- **Numbering of members**: before any member is removed, it is labelled with its bantsuke position and with a tag (札 *fuda*) — see §7.
- **Trace investigation** (痕跡調査 *konseki chōsa*): the systematic reading of disused mortises, nail holes, weathering lines, paint ghosts, groove remnants and cut-off tenons to reconstruct earlier states of the building (original plan, removed walls, changed openings, earlier roof forms). This is how many Japanese buildings have been restored to their original form (復原 *fukugen*) during dismantling repair.
- **Inscriptions**: 墨書 (*bokusho*, ink inscriptions) on members — carpenters' names, dates, construction records, often on hidden faces of ridge beams, on 棟札 (*munafuda*, ridge tablets) — and **carpenters' marking traces** (墨付け, 番付, 合印 *aijirushi*) reveal the original construction sequence and the identity of builders.

**R16-008** — No member **may** be removed, cut or planed before it has been recorded (position, photographs, inscriptions, traces). *Why:* removal destroys context; planing destroys tool marks and inscriptions. Planing of old surfaces **should** in general be avoided.

**R16-009** — Disused mortises, holes and marks found on members **must** be recorded and **should not** be filled or cut away without recording. *Why:* they are the evidence for the building's history (痕跡) and may justify restoration of an earlier form.

**R16-010** — Munafuda and inscribed members **must** be preserved and returned to their positions (or preserved in the building archive), and a new munafuda recording the repair **should** be added. *Why:* the building's own record; custom.

## 5. Repair techniques

### 5.0 Choosing between repair methods
The general hierarchy, from least to most intervention:
1. Clean, dry, and remove the cause (stop the leak, restore ventilation, treat termites).
2. Consolidate small defects: fill cracks with wood slivers (埋木), re-wedge, re-pin.
3. Replace only the decayed portion of a member with new wood (根継ぎ, 継木, 矧ぎ木).
4. Reinforce a weakened member alongside (添え木, hidden steel, resin — §8.3).
5. Replace the whole member (新材取替) only when 1–4 are impossible.

**R16-011** — Decayed wood **must** be cut back to sound wood (probe, drill test, or visual) before any repair; new wood **must not** be joined to decayed or termite-galleried wood. *Why:* decay and termites continue behind the repair, and the joint has nothing sound to bear on.

**R16-012** — The cause of decay **must** be removed before the repair is closed up. *Why:* a repair in unchanged conditions fails again within years.

**R16-013** — New wood for repairs **should** be the same species as the original, of similar grain and density, and air-dried to a moisture content close to that of the old member. *Why:* differential shrinkage and stiffness between old and new wood opens the joint and concentrates stress.

### 5.1 Netsugi — post-base replacement (根継ぎ)
- **Class:** tsugite (vertical splice) used as a repair.
- **Typical use:** replacing the rotted foot of a post (柱 *hashira*) — the most common structural repair in Japanese timber buildings. Also used on the feet of tsuka, the ends of dodai and sills.
- **Load behaviour:** the splice carries axial compression (the post load) through end-grain bearing; it must also transmit the shear and bending that the post base receives from nuki, ashigatame and dodai, and must not rotate or slip under lateral load. Tension (uplift) resistance depends on the joint type (kanawa and okkake daisen resist tension; simple stub tenons do not).
- **Joint choice (by exposure and loads):**

| Joint | Japanese | Visibility | Strength | When chosen |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Rabbeted oblique scarf with key | 金輪継ぎ *kanawa-tsugi* | Visible on two faces as stepped oblique line | High in compression, tension and bending; resists twist | The standard netsugi for structural posts; exterior and interior. |
| Oblique scarf with draw pins | 追掛け大栓継ぎ *okkake-daisen-tsugi* | Visible on two faces | High | Heavy posts; can be assembled by sliding sideways, so useful where vertical movement is limited. |
| Four-way gooseneck | 四方鎌継ぎ *shihō-kama-tsugi* | Shows a kama outline on all four faces; assembled diagonally | Moderate; good alignment | Show posts where a decorative/precise appearance is wanted; limited tension. |
| Four-way dovetail | 四方蟻継ぎ *shihō-ari-tsugi* | Shows dovetail outline on all four faces; assembled diagonally | Moderate | As above. |
| Blind stub tenon (sao) with pins | 竿継ぎ / 短枘 + 込み栓 | Only a horizontal butt line visible | Compression only; low bending | Light interior posts, tsuka, where the joint is largely hidden (e.g. behind plaster). |
| Half-lap / platform | 台持ち継ぎ *daimochi-tsugi* | Visible | Compression, with bolts | Rarely for netsugi; primarily a beam splice (→ Ch. 07); **flagged** as uncommon here. |

- **Proportions (representative, kanawa):** splice length c. 2.5–3.5 × post width (for a 4-sun post, c. 1 shaku–1 shaku 3 sun), key (栓 *sen*) section c. 1/4–1/3 of post width, cut from hard wood (kashi); the splice starts above the highest decayed point by at least one post width. Joint details → Ch. 07.
- **Orientation rules:** the new foot is the lower piece; its moto (butt) points down, matching the original post (never reversed — sakabashira taboo, → Ch. 03). The splice's visible oblique line is placed on the least conspicuous faces (toward the wall or interior), and the joint is set so that splash-water runs off rather than into the splice (the top surfaces of the lower piece's step slope outward or the step faces are arranged to shed water).
- **Marking:** the post's centre-lines (心墨) and the level line (陸墨) are transferred onto the old post above the cut and re-established on the new foot, so that the new foot is exactly coaxial; the foundation stone's surface is re-scribed (光付け) onto the new foot's base.
- **Cutting sequence:**
  1. Shore the building and relieve the post of load (§6.1).
  2. Record, then remove plaster, sill fittings, nuki wedges around the post base as needed.
  3. Cut off the rotted base at the planned splice height.
  4. Cut the female/male half of the joint on the old post *in situ* (it cannot be taken to the bench) using templates.
  5. Cut the matching half on the new foot at the bench, test against a template taken from the in-situ cut.
  6. Scribe the new foot's bottom to the stone (hikari-tsuke).
  7. Insert the new foot sideways (kanawa: assembled by sliding laterally and then the key driven), align, drive the key.
  8. Re-cut nuki and dodai joints through the new foot as required.
  9. Lower the load onto the post; check plumb and level.
- **Assembly:** new foot slid in from the side (the kanawa and okkake daisen are assembled laterally, which is why they are suitable for netsugi of standing posts: the post above does not need to be lifted far).
- **Rules:** R16-014 to R16-016.
- **Common errors:** splice too short; key of soft wood; new foot green (shrinks, key loosens); splice cut at a height where nuki or dodai mortises also weaken the section; ignoring the cause of rot.
- **Variants:** partial netsugi of only two faces (片側根継ぎ, 腰継ぎ), where rot is on one side only; stone-plinth raising (a higher stone set under a shortened post, reducing splash exposure).

**R16-014** — A netsugi **must** be placed with its entire length in sound wood and **must not** coincide with other large section losses (nuki mortises, dodai tenons) — relocate or shorten the other joint, or lengthen the splice. *Why:* coincident section losses create a hinge.

**R16-015** — The kanawa (or okkake-daisen) splice **should** be the default netsugi for structural posts; simple stub-tenon netsugi **may** be used only where the post carries little bending and is braced by walls. *Why:* the post base receives bending from nuki and lateral load; stub tenons cannot resist it.

**R16-016** — The new foot's centre-lines **must** coincide with the original post's, established from the building's 心墨, and its base **must** be re-scribed to the stone. *Why:* an offset foot puts the post load eccentric on the splice; an unscribed base rocks.

### 5.2 Umeki (埋木) — inlays / dutchmen
- **Class:** surface repair.
- **Typical use:** filling old mortises and nail holes (when authorised), replacing local surface rot, knot holes, insect damage, splits, and damaged arrises on show members.
- **Method:** cut the defect back to a clean geometric recess (rectangle, lozenge, or boat-shape aligned to the grain); fit a patch of matching species, grain direction and colour, cut slightly tapered (蟻形 in section, or wedge-sided) so that it tightens as driven; glue (traditionally with rice-starch paste or hide glue; modern conservation with reversible adhesives) and plane flush.
- **Rules:** the patch grain **must** run with the member's grain; the patch **should** be slightly proud and planed down; for load-bearing zones, umeki is decorative only and does not restore strength.

**R16-017** — An umeki **must** follow the grain direction of the member and **should** be cut with sides tapered or undercut so it cannot fall out even if the adhesive fails. *Why:* cross-grain patches shrink differently and pop; tapered patches self-lock.

### 5.3 Hagiki (矧ぎ木) and splicing new to old (継木)
- **Hagiki** (also 剥ぎ木, 矧木): adding a strip of new wood along the length of a member (an edge or face) to replace a decayed side while keeping the core — e.g. replacing the weathered top surface of a sill (敷居), the outer face of a beam end exposed to rain, or the lower edge of a rafter. Joined by glue, loose tongues (雇い実), pins, or small dovetail keys (契り *chigiri*, bow-tie keys).
- **Tsugiki / splicing** (継木): replacing a decayed end of a horizontal member (beam end at a wall, keta end at an eave corner, rafter end) with a new piece joined by a proper tsugite (e.g. 追掛け大栓, 金輪, 尻挟み継ぎ, → Ch. 07) located as close to a support as possible.

**R16-018** — A splice replacing a decayed beam or keta end **must** be located over or immediately beside a support, never at mid-span, and **should** use a splice that resists bending (okkake-daisen, kanawa). *Why:* splices at mid-span cannot carry the bending moment (→ Ch. 07).

**R16-019** — Where a structural member's section has been reduced by hagiki, the combined section **should** be checked for strength; if inadequate, a soegi (§5.4) or hidden reinforcement **should** be added. *Why:* a glued or tongued strip does not reliably act compositely.

### 5.4 Soegi (添え木) — sistering
A new member placed alongside a weakened member (beam, rafter, hanegi, post) and joined to it with bolts, pins, keys (太枘 *dabo*), or straps so that the two share the load. In conservation, soegi are placed on hidden faces (above ceilings, inside walls) and are removable.

**R16-020** — Soegi **should** be placed on concealed faces, fixed with removable fasteners, and **should** bear on the same supports as the member they reinforce. *Why:* reversibility; a sister that does not reach the supports only adds dead weight.

### 5.5 Re-wedging nuki and re-pinning joints
Nuki pass through posts and are fixed by wedges (貫楔 *nuki-kusabi*) driven beside them in the mortise (→ Ch. 09). As timber dries and cyclic loads work the frame, wedges loosen and the frame loses stiffness.

**R16-021** — Loose nuki wedges **should** be re-driven, or replaced with new, slightly larger wedges of dry hard wood (kashi) or matching species, as part of routine maintenance (and always after an earthquake); wedges **must** be driven in pairs from opposite sides where the original detail had pairs. *Why:* the nuki frame's lateral resistance depends on the tightness of these wedges; paired driving keeps the nuki centred.

**R16-022** — Broken komisen, shachisen and hanasen (→ Ch. 09) **must** be replaced with pins of equal or harder wood cut from straight, riven stock; pins **must not** be replaced by steel bolts without justification. *Why:* steel is much stiffer and does not crush like wood; it concentrates stress and splits the member in an earthquake.

### 5.6 Dodai replacement (土台の取替)
The dodai (sill on the foundation, → Ch. 10) is the member most exposed to ground moisture and termites. It is often replaced in sections: the building is lifted slightly (揚げ前, §6.1), the decayed length cut out between posts, and a new length spliced in with koshikake-kama or koshikake-ari splices located just beyond a post (→ Ch. 07), and posts re-tenoned into it. In Edo-period minka where posts stand directly on stones (石場建て *ishibadate*) without dodai, the equivalent repair is netsugi of each post.

**R16-023** — Dodai sections **should** be replaced in durable heartwood (hinoki, hiba, kuri, or preservative-treated where permitted) and the cause (ground contact, blocked ventilation, splash) corrected; splices **must** fall at the post side per → Ch. 07 placement rules. *Why:* dodai decay recurs if the moisture regime is unchanged.

### 5.7 Tategu and zōsaku repair
- **Shōji:** re-papering by soaking the old paper off (water and a brush), cleaning the kumiko, re-pasting; broken kumiko replaced individually (possible because they are unglued, → R14-067).
- **Fusuma:** the fuchi and hikite removed (oreai-kugi, → R14-069), the finish paper renewed over the existing underlayers (or underlayers renewed if damaged); the old underlayers often contain earlier papers and documents of historical interest — record them.
- **Board doors and tokoita:** suitsuki-zan re-driven as the board shrinks (→ R14-082).
- **Shikii:** worn grooves re-cut, umegashi renewed, or the running surface built up with a hagiki strip.

**R16-024** — When dismantling fusuma or other layered paper fittings, the underlayers **should** be examined and any historical documents recorded (and, for designated properties, preserved) before disposal. *Why:* fusuma underlayers were commonly made from recycled paper including letters and accounts (反故紙 *hogogami*), which can date the fitting and the house.

### 5.8 Plaster repair
- Small cracks: cleaned and filled with matching clay or lime.
- Delaminated or bulging areas: cut back to sound arakabe, re-keyed (moisten, score) and built up in the original layer sequence (→ Ch. 14 §3).
- Large failures or rotten lath: lath repaired with new bamboo and rope, then the full sequence (arakabe, uragaeshi, nakanuri, uwanuri) with appropriate drying times.
- Clay from demolished walls is traditionally reused: old arakabe clay is soaked, re-mixed with fresh straw and aged — valued as it is "matured".

**R16-025** — Plaster repair **should** use materials compatible with the original (clay with clay, lime with lime) and **must not** use cement-based mortars on earthen walls. *Why:* cement is stiffer, less permeable and incompatible; it traps moisture against posts and detaches from the clay.

**R16-026** — Old earthen-wall clay **should** be salvaged and reused. *Why:* aged clay performs well and reuse is traditional and economical.

### 5.9 Thatch repair — sashigaya (差し茅)
Partial repair of a thatched roof without full re-thatching: new bundles of thatch (茅 *kaya*: susuki, reed) are pushed into worn or eroded areas with a thatching paddle (ガギ, 叩き), after pulling out rotten material, and dressed to the roof surface. Full re-thatching (葺き替え) removes the thatch to the substrate (or to a sound under-layer) and re-lays it, tied to the roof battens with ropes/withies. In communal villages (e.g. Shirakawa-gō) full re-thatching of large gasshō roofs has traditionally been done by the community mutual-aid system (結 *yui*).

**R16-027** — Worn areas of thatch (especially valleys, eaves, and the ridge) **should** be repaired by sashigaya as soon as erosion reaches the tying layer. *Why:* once the binding ropes are exposed, they rot, and the thatch slides, requiring full re-thatching.

### 5.10 Re-roofing sequence (tile or bark)
1. Survey and photograph the roof; record ridge ornaments, tile types and inscriptions on tiles (瓦銘 *kawara-mei* — dated tiles are frequent).
2. Remove ridge (棟 *mune*) and ornaments; number and store.
3. Remove tiles (or bark/shingle) by courses; sort reusable tiles.
4. Remove bedding clay (葺き土) and underlayment (土居葺 *doibuki*, 野地 *noji*).
5. Inspect and repair noji boards, rafters (垂木), hanegi, eave corners; correct sags (軒垂れ) by re-setting hanegi and packing.
6. Re-lay underlayment (traditionally thin wood shingles 土居葺; now sometimes sheet membranes — a policy decision).
7. Re-lay the covering, mixing reused and new tiles (new tiles on the less visible faces or evenly distributed as policy decides).
8. Re-set the ridge and ornaments.

**R16-028** — During re-roofing, eave sags **should** be corrected at the structural level (hanegi, keta, rafter support) rather than by thickening the bedding or covering. *Why:* packing adds weight and masks the cause; the sag continues.

## 6. Structural operations: lifting, straightening, moving

### 6.1 Agemae (揚げ前) — lifting the building
**Agemae** (揚げ前, also written 上げ前) is the operation of raising a building or part of it on jacks, to relieve members for repair (netsugi, dodai replacement, stone resetting) or to correct settlement (re-levelling). (The spelling 楊前 is occasionally met but appears to be a variant/miswriting; 揚げ前 is standard. **Flagged.**)

Method (representative):
1. Survey levels (§4.2); decide the target levels.
2. Install temporary lifting beams (受け梁) under the dodai, ashigatame or ōbiki, or clamp lifting cleats (抱き) to posts with bolts through temporary blocks (without drilling show faces where avoidable).
3. Set jacks (traditionally screw jacks and levers; now hydraulic jacks) on solid cribbing (枕木 *makuragi* stacks) on firm ground.
4. Lift in small increments, simultaneously or in a planned sequence around the building, monitoring plaster cracks and joints; typical increments of a few mm per step.
5. Pack under the lifted members as they rise (safety packing); never leave the building resting on jacks alone.
6. Perform the repair; reset stones (with new footings if needed), shim to the target levels.
7. Lower onto the repaired supports in increments.

**R16-029** — A building **must** be lifted only in small, monitored increments, with safety packing following each increment, and **must not** be left standing on jacks alone. *Why:* uneven lifting racks the frame and cracks plaster; jacks can fail or sink.

**R16-030** — Lifting points **should** be at members that can take the load (dodai, ōbiki, ashigatame, post bases via clamped cleats), never under floor boards, nuki alone, or finish members. *Why:* point loads crush or pull out joints.

### 6.2 Tateokoshi (建て起こし) — straightening a leaning frame
Restoring a racked or leaning frame to plumb. Method (representative):
1. Survey tilt (§4.2).
2. Loosen the frame: remove or release wall infill where necessary, withdraw nuki wedges, loosen pins, so that the frame can rotate at its joints (walls and wedges resist straightening).
3. Pull the frame toward plumb with come-alongs/turnbuckles and ropes (traditionally 神楽桟 *kagurasan* capstans and ropes) anchored to ground anchors or to a sound part of the building, in small increments, over-correcting slightly to allow for spring-back.
4. Check plumb continuously with sagefuri.
5. Re-drive wedges (new, tight), re-pin, add or repair walls and bracing; release the pulling tackle only when the frame is locked.

**R16-031** — Before straightening, all elements that lock the frame in its deformed shape (tight wedges, walls, bracing) **must** be released, and after straightening the frame **must** be re-locked (new wedges, repaired walls) before the pulling tackle is removed. *Why:* pulling against locked joints breaks tenons; releasing the tackle before locking allows the frame to return.

**R16-032** — Straightening **should** proceed in stages (over days if necessary), and pull forces **should** be applied at beam or keta level near the posts, not at mid-span of members. *Why:* old joints need time to creep back; forces at mid-span bend members.

### 6.3 Hikiya (曳家) — moving a building
**Hikiya** is the moving of a whole building, intact, on rollers or rails to a new location (for road widening, site redevelopment, or to relocate onto a new foundation). The building is lifted (agemae), a timber or steel grillage is inserted beneath the sill level, the building is placed on rollers (コロ *koro*) or wheeled dollies running on rails or timber tracks, and it is pulled with winches (traditionally capstans, 神楽桟) at walking pace or slower. Buildings of several hundred tonnes (including stone castle keeps' timber superstructures and large temple halls) have been moved in Japan by this means; a well-known recent example is the moving of the Hirosaki Castle keep (弘前城天守) in 2015 to allow repair of the stone base beneath it.

**R16-033** — Before hikiya, the frame **must** be braced internally (temporary diagonal braces, tightened nuki wedges) and fragile finishes (plaster, tategu) removed or protected; the grillage **must** carry every post line. *Why:* rolling induces racking and dynamic loads that the frame was not designed for; unsupported post lines drop.

### 6.4 Ichiku (移築) — relocation by dismantling
Where hikiya is impractical (long distances, restricted routes), the building is dismantled (§7), transported, and re-erected on a new site: **ichiku** (移築). This is common for minka moved to open-air museums (e.g. Nihon Minka-en in Kawasaki, the Hida Folk Village in Takayama) and for historic tea houses and gates; historically, temple halls and castle buildings were also frequently relocated. See §10.

## 7. Dismantling order, recording and storage (解体)

### 7.1 Order
Dismantling is, in principle, the **reverse of tatemae** (→ Ch. 15): the last member placed is the first removed. For a typical temple hall or house:
1. **Tategu, tatami, movable fittings** — numbered and removed first; stored separately.
2. **Zōsaku** — ceilings, nageshi, tokonoma members, floors — each numbered.
3. **Roof covering** — ridge, tiles/bark/thatch, bedding, underlayment.
4. **Roof substrate and eaves** — noji boards, rafters (垂木, numbered by bay and side), hanegi, 茅負 *kayaoi* and 木負 *kioi*.
5. **Roof frame** — purlins (母屋 *moya*), ridge (棟木), struts (束), beams (梁) of the koya-gumi.
6. **Walls** — plaster and lath removed (clay salvaged); board walls removed.
7. **Bracket sets and upper frame** (for temples, → Ch. 12): tokyō elements removed piece by piece, from the top down.
8. **Horizontal frame members** — keta, beams, sashimono, nuki (wedges knocked out first, then nuki withdrawn).
9. **Posts** — lowered and laid down.
10. **Sills and foundation stones** — surveyed and numbered if reset.

**R16-034** — Dismantling **must** proceed in the reverse order of assembly, and no member **may** be forced out of a joint; if a member will not come free, the reason (hidden pin, wedge, nail, later repair) **must** be found. *Why:* forcing breaks tenons and conceals the joint's construction; hidden pins and later nails are common.

**R16-035** — Pins and wedges **should** be driven out, not cut, where possible, and **should** be kept with the member they came from. *Why:* pins are part of the record and can often be reused; cutting them damages the holes.

### 7.2 Numbering and tagging
- Every member receives a **tag** (札 *fuda*: wooden, metal, or plastic label, attached with nails or ties to a hidden face) giving: building, member type, bantsuke grid position (→ Ch. 02, e.g. い三, ろ五), direction (north/south face etc.), and a serial number cross-referenced to the survey drawings.
- The original **bantsuke** written by the builders is recorded but not relied on alone (it may be incomplete, reused from another building, or altered in earlier repairs).
- Small parts (pins, wedges, bracket blocks, kumiko) are bagged per joint and tagged.
- Joints are photographed and, for important joints, drawn at 1:1 or 1:5 (**joint recording**: shape, dimensions, tool marks, any shims).

**R16-036** — Every dismantled member **must** carry a durable tag keyed to the survey drawings, attached so as not to damage show faces, and **should** be photographed with its tag. *Why:* re-erection depends on the correct position of each member; members of similar size are easily confused.

### 7.3 Storage
- Members are stored under cover, off the ground on bearers (枕木), with spacers, in dry, ventilated conditions (a temporary storage shed, 格納庫, is usually built on site for major repairs).
- Long members are supported at close intervals to avoid permanent sagging.
- Members are protected from sun (checking) and from rain.
- Members are grouped in reverse dismantling order so they come out in assembly order for re-erection.

**R16-037** — Dismantled members **must** be stored flat, supported along their length, under cover and off the ground, and **should** be arranged for retrieval in re-erection order. *Why:* old members crack and distort quickly with wetting and uneven support; re-erection efficiency.

## 8. Old and new timber: reuse, marking, materials policy

### 8.1 Old timber (旧材 kyūzai) vs new timber (新材 shinzai)
After dismantling, each member is assessed:
- **Reuse as is** — sound, joints intact.
- **Repair and reuse** — netsugi, umeki, hagiki, splicing (§5).
- **Replace** — only when the member cannot safely carry its load after repair, or when it is a later, inappropriate replacement being removed in a restoration.
- Removed original members of historical value are often kept in storage or displayed rather than discarded.

**R16-038** — The decision to replace an original member **should** be made member by member by the responsible master carpenter with the conservation architect, and recorded with the reason. *Why:* replacement is irreversible; accountability and consistency.

### 8.2 Marking new members (刻印, 焼印)
New members inserted in a repair are marked, usually on a hidden face, with a **brand or stamp** (刻印 *kokuin*, 焼印 *yakiin*) giving the repair year and often the name of the repair (e.g. "平成○○年修理", "令和○年補"). This allows future investigators to distinguish original from repair material without doubt. In conservation practice the marks are small and placed on non-show faces.

**R16-039** — Every new member and every significant new part (netsugi foot, spliced end, large umeki) inserted in a repair **must** be marked on a hidden face with the repair date (brand, stamp or ink). *Why:* distinguishes original from repair material for future repairs and research.

### 8.3 Resin and metal reinforcement — policy
Modern conservation sometimes uses **epoxy resins** (樹脂) — to consolidate rotted wood, to fill voids, or to bond reinforcing rods (glass-fibre, stainless steel) into beam ends — and **metal reinforcement** (steel plates, bolts, hidden steel frames, carbon-fibre). Japanese conservation practice has used steel reinforcement extensively in 20th-century repairs (e.g. hidden steel members in roof frames of large halls), and resin consolidation in specific cases.

The debate:
- **For:** preserves more original material (a resin-consolidated beam end avoids replacement), adds earthquake resistance required by modern standards (→ Ch. 17), and can be hidden.
- **Against:** **reversibility** — epoxy cannot be removed without destroying the wood; **compatibility** — stiffness and moisture behaviour differ from wood, leading to decay at the interface and stress concentration; **authenticity** — the structural behaviour changes; **long-term uncertainty** — resin durability over centuries is unknown, whereas timber joinery has a proven record.
- The Venice Charter (1964) and the Nara Document on Authenticity (1994) are the international reference texts in these debates; Japanese practice generally prefers traditional timber repair and restricts modern materials to cases where traditional methods cannot achieve the required safety, requiring that modern interventions be minimal, documented and, as far as possible, removable.

**R16-040** — Irreversible materials (epoxy, glued-in rods, cement) **should** be used only where traditional timber repair cannot achieve the necessary safety, and then **must** be documented and confined to the smallest possible volume. *Why:* reversibility and authenticity; the next repair must not be prevented by this one.

**R16-041** — Metal reinforcement **should** be removable (bolted, not glued), placed on concealed faces, protected from condensation (which rots the adjacent wood), and **must not** replace the timber joint's function where the joint can be repaired. *Why:* condensation on cold steel embedded in wood is a known cause of hidden decay; stiff steel changes the frame's ductile behaviour.

### 8.4 Recognising reused timber (転用材 ten'yōzai)
Traditional buildings frequently contain members reused from earlier buildings (転用材): the practice was economical and sometimes ritual. Signs:
- Mortises, housings or notches that serve no function in the present position (空き枘穴).
- Bantsuke or marks that do not match the present grid.
- Weathering or paint on faces now concealed, or smoke-blackening on members in a room without a hearth.
- Different tool marks (e.g. yariganna finishes on one member among plane-finished ones).
- Members shortened or re-cut, with sawn ends differing from surrounding work.

**R16-042** — Evidence of reuse (転用) **must** be recorded during dismantling and **should** be investigated for its original context. *Why:* reused members can date earlier buildings on the site or elsewhere and explain anomalies in the present building.

| | Check for leaks in ceilings and at chiri lines after heavy rain | Water stains, damp plaster. |

| Interval | Task |
|---|---|
| Annually | Roof and gutter inspection; bird and animal damage (nesting in bracket sets); termite inspection; underfloor/under-veranda ventilation; fire-protection systems check (Japan observes a national Cultural Property Fire Prevention Day on 26 January — the anniversary of the 1949 Hōryū-ji Kondō fire — with fire drills at temples and shrines). |

**R16-043** — Every traditional building **should** have a maintenance log recording inspections, repairs and their dates. *Why:* repair intervals are learned from the building's own history; defects that recur locally reveal their cause.

**R16-044** — Ground-level conditions **must** be maintained so that no timber is in contact with soil, splash zones are kept clear (gravel strips, 雨落ち溝 *amaochi-mizo*), and underfloor ventilation is never blocked. *Why:* most decay and termite damage starts at the ground line.

**R16-045** — Tatami **should** be turned (uragaeshi) and re-covered (omotegae) rather than replaced while the core is sound, and **must** be returned to their marked positions (→ R14-013). *Why:* economy; tatami are made for their position.

**R16-046** — After any significant earthquake, the building **should** be inspected for tilt, loose wedges, pulled-out joints and roof damage, and wedges re-driven before the next event. *Why:* nuki frames lose much stiffness when wedges loosen; aftershocks act on a weakened frame.

## 10. Salvage, relocation and the old-timber market

### 10.1 Ichiku (移築)
Relocating a building by dismantling and re-erection (§6.4, §7) has a long history in Japan: buildings were given, sold, and moved as gifts and as spoils (many temple halls and gates were moved from castles or palaces; tea houses were moved with their owners). In the modern period, minka and machiya threatened by redevelopment are moved to open-air museums, private owners and new sites.

Key points for ichiku:
- The new site's foundation (stones or concrete) must reproduce the original post layout exactly (survey from the original site).
- Earthen walls are usually not moved; their clay may be transported for reuse. New lath and plaster are applied on the new site.
- Replacement of decayed members is done at the time of dismantling, so the building is re-erected sound.
- Under modern regulations, a relocated building is treated as new construction and must satisfy the Building Standard Law (→ Ch. 17), which may require additional structural measures; designated cultural properties and buildings covered by local ordinances have different routes.

**R16-047** — For ichiku, the original post grid and levels **must** be surveyed before dismantling, and the new foundation **must** reproduce them; the building **should** be re-erected with the same orientation to sun and prevailing weather where possible. *Why:* the frame's joints fit only the original geometry; orientation affects the weathering pattern the building has adapted to (weathered faces on the weather side).

### 10.2 The old-timber (古材 kozai) market
Old timbers (古材) from demolished minka — especially large smoke-blackened keyaki, matsu and kuri beams (梁), daikokubashira, boards and tategu — are salvaged and traded, both for reuse in new traditional buildings and for interior use. Salvage organisations and dealers (e.g. old-timber banks, 古材バンク, run by non-profits and firms) stock such members.

### 10.3 Rules for reusing old members
Reused members differ from new timber in known ways:
- **Section losses:** old mortises, housings and holes reduce the effective section, often at exactly the points that matter (beam ends, post mid-heights).
- **Hidden decay and insect damage:** especially at former bearing points and at the ends.
- **Embedded metal:** old nails, cramps (鎹 *kasugai*) and bolts, which damage tools and indicate possible corrosion staining/decay.
- **Moisture history:** old timber is fully seasoned and very stable — a major advantage — but may have checks (割れ).
- **Strength:** well-preserved old timber has broadly comparable (sometimes slightly lower, sometimes higher) strength to new timber of the same species and grade; strength reduction is dominated by defects and section losses rather than by age itself. Grading of old members for structural reuse is by visual inspection and, where required, by non-destructive testing.

**R16-048** — Before reuse, an old member **must** be inspected for decay, insect damage and embedded metal along its full length, and all existing holes and joints **must** be mapped; the member **must** be placed so that no existing hole coincides with a new joint or a zone of high stress, or the hole **must** be filled with a tight-fitting umeki of matching species (which restores appearance, not strength). *Why:* old joint holes reduce section; umeki do not carry tension.

**R16-049** — Old members **should** be used in a position and orientation similar to their original (post as post, beam as beam, same moto/sue direction, same up face). *Why:* timber has adapted to its original loading (creep, checks); reversing a beam can open checks under tension; a reversed post breaks the sakabashira taboo (→ Ch. 03).

**R16-050** — Salvaged members **must** be metal-detected and all embedded metal removed before any cutting. *Why:* safety and tool protection; hidden metal also marks likely decay pockets.

**R16-051** — The provenance of salvaged members (source building, location, approximate date) **should** be recorded and passed on with the member. *Why:* provenance affects value, historical significance and future repairs; unrecorded kozai loses its history.
