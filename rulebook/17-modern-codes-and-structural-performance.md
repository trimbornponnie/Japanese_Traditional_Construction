# Chapter 17 — Traditional Construction under Modern Codes (現行法規と構造性能 / *genkō hōki to kōzō seinō*)

## Scope

This chapter explains how Japanese law treats buildings made with traditional joinery, and what research has found about how such frames behave. It covers:
- the Building Standard Law and its Enforcement Order provisions for timber;
- exemptions for heritage buildings;
- the calculation routes open to traditional frames;
- the research programme of 2008–2013 and the design manuals that came out of it;
- representative data on *nuki* joints, earth walls, *sashigamoi* frames and *ishibadate* sliding;
- fire, durability and energy regulations as they affect traditional houses;
- CNC precutting versus hand-cut joinery.

It ends with rules for designers who want to build traditionally today.

> **Important — this is not legal advice.** Building law in Japan changes often: major amendments took effect in 2000, 2007, 2016, 2019 and April 2025, and notifications (告示 *kokuji*) are amended more often still. The article contents summarised here were checked as far as possible against published summaries at the time of writing (2026), but **the reader must check the current text of the law, the Enforcement Order and the relevant notifications, and must engage a licensed architect (建築士 *kenchikushi*) and, where required, a structural engineer.** Items that could not be verified are marked "(verify)". Figures are representative.

The structural principles referred to here are set out in → Ch. 10. The historical and technical sources are in → Ch. 01.

The following summarises the Order's "wooden construction" section (第3章第3節 木造). The wording is paraphrased; consult the current text.

|---|---|---|---|

| 41 | Timber quality | Structural timber free of harmful knots, rot, grain deviation | Compatible with traditional grading (→ Ch. 03) |
| 42 | Sills and foundations | Posts at the lowest storey must have a sill below, except where (1) the post is fixed to the foundation, (2) in a one-storey building with *ashigatame*, or (3) since 2016, the post is joined to the foundation by a *dabo* or other method specified by the Minister, and it is verified that no harmful tension occurs in the post. - **Art. 22** — raised floor height ≥ 45 cm and underfloor ventilation, unless the ground is sealed (→ Ch. 10 §8).
- **Art. 36 onward** — general structural provisions.
- **Art. 82 onward** — structural calculation methods, including limit strength calculation under Art. 82-5.

### 2.1 Art. 42 and *ishibadate*

The 2016 amendment added a third exception to the sill requirement.
- The post may be connected to the foundation with a dowel under **Notification No. 690 of 2016** (平成28年国土交通省告示第690号).
- According to published summaries, the dowel is fixed into the foundation and embedded **at least 90 mm** into a post of at least **105 mm** width (or an equivalent dowel connection).
- It must also be confirmed, by a method specified in the notification, that **no harmful tensile force** (uplift) occurs in the post that would unseat the dowel.

In effect this allows posts on stone or concrete pads with dowels, with a calculation check of uplift. It does not by itself make a free-sliding *ishibadate* a specification-route building. Free sliding still requires the calculation routes (§4).

**R17-001** — **Must.** For an *ishibadate* building, establish at the outset which exception of Art. 42 (or which calculation route) the design will rely on. The choice fixes the base detail (free, dowelled or anchored; → R10-020) and the whole design procedure.

### 2.2 Art. 43 post sizes

The Order sets a minimum ratio of post dimension to the vertical distance between horizontal members (土台, 足固め, 胴差, 梁, 桁…). The long-standing table, before the 2025 amendment, read approximately as follows (verify against the current text):

| Building type | Posts in buildings with column spacing ≥ 10 m, or in schools/assembly buildings: top storey or single storey | …other storeys | Other posts: top storey or single storey | …other storeys |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| (1) Buildings with especially heavy walls (e.g. 土蔵造 *dozō-zukuri*, earth-walled storehouse construction) | 1/22 | 1/20 | 1/25 | 1/22 |
| (2) Buildings with light roofs (metal, slate, wood shingles etc.) | 1/30 | 1/25 | 1/33 | 1/30 |
| (3) Other buildings (e.g. tiled roofs) | 1/25 | 1/22 | 1/30 | 1/28 |

*Example:* a tiled-roof house with 2,730 mm between sill and girder at the ground floor of a two-storey building: 2,730/28 ≈ 98 mm, so a 105 mm post satisfied the old table.

**2025 change.** Because modern houses have become heavier (insulation, solar panels, heavier glazing), the April 2025 amendment changed the method. For ordinary buildings, the required post size is now determined from the building's actual loads, using a formula or tables published by MLIT, instead of the simple light/heavy-roof classification. The heavy-wall (*dozō*) row is understood to remain as a ratio (verify). A heavy earth-walled, tiled traditional house will typically need **larger posts** than the old table suggested. The traditional 4–5 sun (120–150 mm) posts generally meet this, but it must be checked.

### 2.3 Art. 46 required wall quantity and wall factors

The **wall-quantity method** (壁量計算 *kaberyō keisan*) compares:
- the **effective wall length** in each direction and storey — the length of each wall multiplied by its wall factor (壁倍率 *kabe-bairitsu*);
- the **required wall length** — floor area × a coefficient for earthquakes, and the exposed elevation area × a coefficient for wind.

A wall factor of **1.0** corresponds to an allowable short-term shear of **1.96 kN per metre** of wall (200 kgf/m).

**Wall factors in Order Art. 46 Table 1** (long-standing values; verify current table):

| Wall / frame | Factor |
|---|---|
| Earth-plastered wall (土塗壁) or wood lath on one side (木ずり片面) | 0.5 |
| Wood lath on both sides | 1.0 |
| Brace of ≥ 15 × 90 mm timber or ≥ 9 mm steel bar | 1.0 |
| Brace ≥ 30 × 90 mm | 1.5 |
| Brace ≥ 45 × 90 mm | 2.0 |
| Brace ≥ 90 × 90 mm | 3.0 |
| Crossed braces (たすき掛け) of the above | 2 × single (90 mm crossed: 5.0) |
| Combinations | Sum, up to an upper limit (long 5.0; reported as raised in 2025 for combinations — verify) |

**Notification 1100 of 1981** (昭和56年建設省告示第1100号) specifies further walls and their factors, including panel walls. After its **2003 amendment**, **earth walls on bamboo lath** meeting specified conditions receive factors graded by thickness:
- the thickest specified earth walls (commonly cited as about **70 mm** total thickness) receive **1.5**, comparable to a single 30 × 90 mm brace;
- intermediate thicknesses receive **1.0**;
- others keep **0.5**.

The conditions concern:
- the *nuki* (number, section, spacing);
- the bamboo lath (*mawatashi-dake* diameter, *komai* spacing);
- the earth coats (*arakabe* and *nakanuri*);
- the thickness.

The same period also saw the recognition of other traditional walls in the notification, with their own factors and conditions:
- lattice walls (面格子壁 *mengōshi-kabe*);
- dropped-in thick board walls (落とし込み板壁 *otoshikomi-itakabe*);
- earth-plastered hanging walls and waist walls (垂れ壁・腰壁) under specified conditions.

Exact specifications and values must be taken from the current notification.

**Required quantities before 2025** (representative; cm of wall per m² of floor area, earthquake):

| Building | Light roof | Heavy roof |
|---|---|---|
| One storey | 11 | 15 |
| Two storeys: ground floor | 29 | 33 |
| Two storeys: upper floor | 15 | 21 |

For wind, the exposed elevation area × 50 cm/m² (general), with higher values in designated windy areas.

**2025 change.** Required wall quantities for earthquakes are now calculated from the building's actual weight (roof, walls, insulation, solar panels etc.) by formula or MLIT tables, not by the light/heavy roof table. In addition, **quasi-bearing walls** (準耐力壁 *jun-tairyoku-heki*) and **hanging and waist walls** (垂れ壁・腰壁) meeting conditions may be counted in the existing wall quantity, as set out in the amended Notification 1100. There are also reduction factors for tall storeys (e.g. storey height > 3.2 m) (verify details).

**Balance and joints.**
- Notification 1352 of 2000 requires balanced placement, checked by the **quarter-division method** (四分割法 *yon-bunkatsu-hō*).
- Notification 1460 of 2000 specifies brace-end connections and the **post-top/post-base connections**, either from tables by position or by the **N-value calculation** (N値計算).
- The tables include traditional options such as the long tenon with draw-pin (長ほぞ差し込み栓打ち) for the lowest demand levels. Higher demands need steel hardware (plates, hold-downs).

**Why this route is difficult for traditional frames.**
- *Nuki* frames are not credited at all in the Order table and have only limited recognition otherwise.
- Earth walls reach at most 1.5.
- A traditional house with few walls on the street side and around the garden (open plans with *engawa* and *shōji*) rarely satisfies the required wall quantity and the quarter-division balance.
- The design philosophy of the wall-quantity method — stiffness and strength at small drift — conflicts with the deformation-capacity philosophy of the traditional frame (→ Ch. 10 §11).

**R17-002** — **Should.** If the specification (wall-quantity) route is used for a traditional-style house, use recognised traditional walls (thick earth walls, lattice walls, board walls) to meet the wall quantity. Avoid adding stiff braces or panels to a *nuki* frame merely to reach the numbers, unless the whole lateral system is redesigned (→ R10-067).

## 3. Exemptions and special regimes

### 3.1 Cultural properties — BSL Art. 3

Article 3(1) of the Building Standard Law excludes from the Act's application:
- (i) buildings designated as **National Treasures, Important Cultural Properties** and similar under the Cultural Properties Protection Act;
- (ii) buildings with certain earlier designations;
- **(iii) buildings whose preservation is regulated under a local ordinance (条例) for cultural or historic buildings and which the designated administrative agency (特定行政庁) designates with the consent of the building review council (建築審査会)**;
- (iv) reconstructions of such buildings, under conditions.

Repairs to designated buildings are governed by heritage law and practice instead (→ Ch. 16). In practice, structural safety measures are still sought, e.g. seismic assessment guidelines for cultural properties issued by the Agency for Cultural Affairs.

### 3.2 Municipal ordinances for historic buildings

Using Art. 3(1)(iii), several municipalities have ordinances that let historic buildings that are not national cultural properties be exempted from the Act, on condition of an agreed safety plan (fire, evacuation, structural reinforcement):
- **Kyoto City:** the **Kyoto City Ordinance on the Preservation and Utilisation of Historic Buildings** (京都市歴史的建築物の保存及び活用に関する条例, 2012) is used especially for *kyō-machiya*. Kyoto also has the **Kyoto City Ordinance on the Preservation and Succession of Kyō-machiya** (京都市京町家の保全及び継承に関する条例, 2017). The latter is a conservation ordinance (e.g. prior notification of demolition), not a building-code exemption. The city publishes guidance on what can be done with *machiya* under and outside the Act.
- Other cities have adopted similar Art. 3(1)(iii) ordinances. Check the current list with MLIT or the municipality.

### 3.3 Preservation districts — BSL Art. 85-3

In **Important Preservation Districts for Groups of Traditional Buildings** (重要伝統的建造物群保存地区, *jūdenken*), BSL Art. 85-3 allows the municipality, by ordinance and with the Minister's approval, to relax certain provisions of the Act. Typical examples are setbacks, height limits, and some fire provisions for roofs and external walls. This makes it possible to repair or rebuild traditional facades in the historic townscape.

### 3.4 Small buildings and the 2025 amendment

Until March 2025, small timber buildings (two storeys or fewer, ≤ 500 m², ≤ 13 m high and ≤ 9 m to the eaves: "Category 4 buildings", 4号建築物) designed by a licensed architect were exempt from **structural review** at building confirmation (the 4号特例). The designer remained responsible for compliance, but the documents were not checked.

From **April 2025**:
- The category was abolished and split.
  - **"New Category 2"** (新2号): two-storey timber buildings and single-storey buildings over 200 m². These now require submission and review of structural (and energy) documents at confirmation.
  - **"New Category 3"** (新3号): single-storey buildings ≤ 200 m². These keep a reduced review.
- The threshold at which timber buildings need structural calculation was lowered from 500 m² to **300 m²** of total floor area.
- The height limit for simpler routes was raised to **16 m**.

(Summarised from published explanations; verify details.)

For traditional houses this means:
- The specification-route checks (wall quantity, post sizes, joints) of a two-storey house are now **actually reviewed**.
- The practical scope for informally "traditional" two-storey houses without calculation has disappeared.

**R17-003** — **Must.** Treat every new two-storey traditional house (and every single-storey house over 200 m²) as requiring full structural documentation for building confirmation under the post-2025 rules, whichever route is chosen.

## 4. Design routes for traditional frames

| Route | Legal basis | Suits traditional frames? | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A. Specification route** (仕様規定: wall quantity, balance, joints) | Order Art. 40–49 with notifications | Partly. Frames on a sill with thick earth walls, lattice walls and hardware or pinned joints | Cannot credit *nuki* deformation capacity; open plans fail; free *ishibadate* excluded (Art. 42) |
| **B. Allowable stress calculation** (許容応力度計算) | Order Art. 82 onwards | Poorly | Elastic, small-drift method; traditional joints are semi-rigid and non-linear |
| **C. Limit strength calculation** (限界耐力計算) | Order Art. 82-5 (introduced 2000) | **Yes** — the main legal route for *ishibadate* and *nuki* frames since 2000 | Capacity-spectrum-type method using the building's non-linear load–drift curve; checks at damage limit and safety limit; can include base sliding. Since 2007 requires the *tekihan* conformity review, even for small houses, which costs time and money |
| **D. Time-history response analysis + Ministerial approval** (時刻歴応答解析, 大臣認定) | BSL Art. 20 | Yes, for large or special buildings | Expensive; used for large temple reconstructions and research buildings |
| **E. Heritage exemption** | BSL Art. 3 (§3) | Only for designated buildings | Not a route for new buildings (except reconstructions under Art. 3(1)(iv)) |

### 4.1 Limit strength calculation in outline

1. Establish the **load–drift relationship** (復元力特性) of each storey by summing the contributions of each element at each drift angle:
   - *nuki* joints, *sashigamoi* joints, *ashigatame*;
   - earth walls, board walls, lattice walls;
   - column rocking and bending.

   Values come from tests or from recognised element data in the manuals (§5).
2. Convert to an equivalent single-degree-of-freedom system and derive the **capacity curve**.
3. Compare with the **demand spectrum**, reduced for damping, at two limit states:
   - **Damage limit** (損傷限界): for rare moderate earthquakes. A storey drift limit of the order of 1/120 rad is commonly used for timber under this route (verify).
   - **Safety limit** (安全限界): for very rare strong earthquakes. Traditional frames are typically designed to safety-limit drifts of the order of 1/30–1/15 rad; the manuals set the limits and the conditions for using them.
4. For *ishibadate*, the manuals give methods for checking whether the base slides and for estimating its effect.
5. Check members and joints at those drifts (post bending at *tarekabe*, joint pull-out, *nuki* splitting).

**R17-004** — **Should.** Where the limit strength route is used, commission the structural design early (before the *itazu* is fixed). Budget time for the conformity review (*tekihan*). Use element data from the recognised manuals, or from tests on the actual joint details, rather than from generic values.
*Why:* The performance of a *nuki* or *sashigamoi* joint depends on its species, section, fit and pins. Data transferred from a different detail may be unconservative.

## 5. Research programmes and design manuals

### 5.1 Before 2008: the limit-strength manual

After the 2000 amendment, researchers and practitioners centred on Kyoto (including Suzuki Yoshiyuki 鈴木祥之 of Kyoto University and later Ritsumeikan University) developed a practical method for applying limit strength calculation to traditional frames. It was published as:
- 『**伝統構法を生かす木造耐震設計マニュアル — 限界耐力計算による耐震設計・耐震補強設計法**』 (木造軸組構法建物の耐震設計マニュアル編集委員会 ed., Gakugei Shuppansha, Kyoto, **2004**).

The manual gives element data (*nuki*, earth walls, *sashigamoi*, board walls) and procedures for new design and seismic retrofit. It was widely used for *machiya* retrofits and for new traditional houses. (Note: the title is sometimes quoted with a 2019 date. The 2019 publication is a different book; see §5.3.)

### 5.2 The 2008–2013 research programme

- **Name:** 伝統的構法の設計法作成及び性能検証実験 検討委員会 ("Committee for the Creation of Design Methods and Performance Verification Experiments for Traditional Construction").
- **Period:** established in fiscal 2008, with work continuing to about 2013.
- **Funding and secretariat:** subsidised by MLIT; secretariat the **Japan Housing and Wood Technology Center** (日本住宅・木材技術センター).
- **Scope:** development of design methods; full-scale shake-table tests; element tests of joints and walls; surveys of traditional construction practice and materials.
- **From fiscal 2010:** focused on design methods including ***ishibadate***.
- **Outcome:** around 2013 it proposed three levels of design method for traditional frames, as reported:
  1. a **standard design method** (標準設計法) using simplified calculation;
  2. a **detailed design method** (詳細設計法) based on limit strength calculation;
  3. a **general design method** (汎用設計法) using time-history response analysis.

These proposals were not incorporated into the Order as a specification route. The long-standing request from practitioners — a simple route for *ishibadate* houses that does not require the *tekihan* conformity review — was only partly met (e.g. by the 2016 dowel provision, §2.1). (Status: verify current MLIT position.)

**Shake-table tests at E-Defense** (the full-scale three-dimensional shake table of NIED, 防災科学技術研究所, at Miki, Hyōgo):
- **November–December 2008.** Two full-size two-storey traditional houses were tested without metal connectors, using the JMA Kobe record (1995) at 100%.
  - A "**Kantō type**" stood on a sill (posts with long tenons into the sill).
  - A "**Kansai type**" stood on stones with dowels (*ishibadate*), with *ashigatame* and *nuki*.
  - Both survived with large deformations and returned close to their original position.
  - The Kantō type suffered bending fractures of some posts and some earth wall spalling. The Kansai type had less damage (as reported in published summaries).
- **FY2010 (tests publicised in January 2011).** *Ishibadate* specimens were tested under the same programme. Media reports noted that they did not collapse (verify the specimens and the measured sliding and drifts in the official reports).
- Earlier E-Defense tests (from 2005) examined existing and relocated timber houses, including old conventional houses.

### 5.3 The 2019 manual

- 『**伝統的構法のための木造耐震設計法 — 石場建てを含む木造建築物の耐震設計・耐震補強マニュアル**』 (伝統的構法木造建築物設計マニュアル編集委員会 ed., Gakugei Shuppansha, **2019**).

This book brings the results of the 2008–2013 programme into a practical manual. It covers *ishibadate*, the standard and detailed design methods, element data, and seismic retrofit. It is currently the main reference for designers of traditional frames.

### 5.4 Other references

- **"Grey book":** the Japan Housing and Wood Technology Center's manual for allowable stress design of post-and-beam houses (『木造軸組工法住宅の許容応力度設計』; 2008 and 2017 editions). The standard reference for route B (conventional frames).
- **AIJ publications:** the Architectural Institute of Japan publishes the standard for timber structures (木質構造設計規準) and a design manual for timber joints (木質構造接合部設計マニュアル), which include embedment design (verify editions).

**R17-005** — **Should.** Use the 2019 manual (§5.3), or its successors, as the primary reference for new *ishibadate* and *nuki*-frame designs. Use the 2004 manual for comparison and for retrofit precedent. Record which edition and which element data were used.

## 6. Structural behaviour: representative data

The values in this section are **order-of-magnitude illustrations** drawn from the general research literature. They are not design values. Design values must be taken from the manuals and notifications, or from tests on the actual details.

### 6.1 *Nuki* joints: embedment (*merikomi*) theory

When a frame racks by an angle θ, the *nuki* rotates relative to the post. It presses into the post at two diagonally opposite corners of the mortise, and the post presses into the *nuki*. The resisting moment M(θ) comes from:
1. **Embedment of wood perpendicular to grain.** Embedment stiffness depends on the modulus perpendicular to grain (roughly 1/25–1/50 of the longitudinal modulus for softwoods), the loaded area, and the **end-distance effect**: fibres beyond the loaded area are dragged down and contribute stiffness.
2. **Friction** between wedges, *nuki* and mortise faces.
3. **Initial slip** due to play in the mortise, which is reduced by tight wedging.

**Inayama Masahiro** (稲山正弘, later professor at the University of Tokyo) developed a widely used theory of embedment of wood perpendicular to grain, including the end-distance effect, and applied it to *nuki* and other traditional joints. It became the basis of later design formulas and of the element data in the manuals. Researchers such as the Kyoto/Ritsumeikan group extended it to cyclic behaviour and to wedged through-*nuki*, with test-based models of the moment–rotation skeleton curve.

**Typical characteristics:**
- M–θ curve with **low initial stiffness**, a gradual increase, and continuing resistance up to **θ ≈ 1/10 rad** or more.
- No sudden failure until the post splits at the mortise or the *nuki* is crushed or split.
- **Pinched hysteresis loops**: permanent embedment leaves gaps, so on reloading the joint slips before re-engaging.
- The dissipated energy per cycle is moderate and decreases in repeated cycles.

**Order of magnitude:**
- For house-scale *nuki* (e.g. ~30 × 120 mm in 120 mm posts, softwood), published tests and calculations show resisting moments in the range of roughly **0.5–2 kN·m per joint** over drifts of about 1/30–1/10 rad. Heavier and harder-wood *nuki* give more.
- A bay 1 ken wide and one storey high with three *nuki* levels thus resists a shear force of only a few kN at large drift. That is equivalent to a small fraction of a braced wall.

**Consequence:** *nuki* alone give a frame **deformation capacity and a safety net**, not stiffness. Stiffness comes from earth walls, *sashigamoi* and board walls.

### 6.2 Earth walls (土塗り壁)

- **Initial behaviour:** stiff until cracking (roughly 1/120–1/60 rad). Cracks form along the frame and diagonally.
- **After cracking:** the panel continues to resist through bearing against the frame and friction, with slowly decreasing strength up to large drifts (1/30–1/15 rad and beyond).
- **Capacity:** increases with thickness and with a proper *nakanuri* coat. In legal terms: wall factor 0.5 (Order table) up to 1.5 (Notification 1100 for thick walls meeting specified conditions), i.e. about 1–3 kN/m allowable short-term shear.
- **Tested capacity:** research tests of thick earth walls report maximum capacities that can be several times the allowable values, with the peak typically at large drift. The allowable values are set conservatively.
- **Energy dissipation:** good in the first large cycles (cracking and friction), reducing in later cycles.
- **Variation:** strongly dependent on the clay, the straw, the workmanship and the drying. Regional clays differ.

**R17-006** — **Should.** Where earth walls are credited in calculation, specify the clay (or require tests), the *komai* details, the *nuki* layout and the thickness of each coat. Have the plasterer confirm them in writing.
*Why:* The wall factor applies only to the specified construction. A thin-coated wall on sparse lath is a 0.5 wall at best.

### 6.3 *Sashigamoi* frames

- The deep lintel (300–450 mm) with keyed or pegged joints gives much higher rotational stiffness and moment capacity than *nuki*, several times that of a *nuki* joint (depending on depth, joint type and pegs).
- **Failure modes:**
  - splitting of the post at the joint;
  - shear of pegs (*komisen*);
  - pull-out;
  - bending fracture of the post below the *sashigamoi* (short-column effect, → Ch. 10 §11.5).
- The frames of the 2008 Kantō-type test house illustrate the post-bending risk.

### 6.4 *Ishibadate* base sliding

- **Friction:** wood on stone has a friction coefficient of the order of 0.3–0.5 (dry), lower if wet or with smooth dressed stone.
- **Below the threshold** the base acts as fixed.
- **Above the threshold** the base shear is limited to roughly μ × weight, and relative sliding occurs, which can be several centimetres or more depending on the ground motion.
- **Uplift and rocking** of individual posts occur when the frame racks and when vertical ground motion is strong.
- **Design approaches in the manuals:**
  1. design the upper structure for the full demand as if fixed, and check the sliding displacement for safety (stones large enough, no restraint);
  2. or use sliding to limit the demand, with appropriate checks.
- Dowels (§2.1) change the behaviour toward pinned bases.

**R17-007** — **Must.** An *ishibadate* design that relies on sliding must show that the predicted sliding displacement is accommodated: stone size, clearances to adjacent structures, flexible service connections, and no local restraint.

### 6.5 Comparison with other systems

| System | Initial stiffness | Drift at peak strength | Behaviour after peak | Typical legal design basis |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Traditional *nuki* + earth wall frame (*ishibadate* or sill) | Low–moderate | ~1/30–1/15 rad (walls) and beyond (*nuki*) | Gradual degradation; large deformation capacity; tends to return | Limit strength calculation; wall-quantity route with earth-wall factors |
| *Sashigamoi* frame | Moderate | ~1/30–1/20 rad | Post splitting/bending risk | As above |
| Conventional post-and-beam with braces and hardware (在来軸組構法) | High | ~1/60–1/30 rad | Brace buckling or connector failure, then steep drop | Wall-quantity route; allowable stress (grey book) |
| Conventional with structural panels (plywood) | High | ~1/50–1/30 rad | Nail failure; gradual to moderate drop | Same |
| Platform frame "2×4" (枠組壁工法) | Very high | ~1/60–1/30 rad | Nail and sheathing failure | Separate notification (枠組壁工法告示) |

(Representative, from the general literature; individual tests vary.)

## 7. Fire regulations

### 7.1 Fire zones

| Zone | Japanese | Main consequences for traditional houses (paraphrase) |
|---|---|---|
| **Fire zone** | 防火地域 | Most buildings must be fire-resistive or quasi-fire-resistive. Exposed-timber traditional houses are very difficult to build |
| **Quasi-fire zone** | 準防火地域 | For timber houses: external walls and eaves soffits within the "area at risk of fire spread" (延焼のおそれのある部分, measured from boundaries and road centre lines) must be of **fire-preventive construction** (防火構造); openings in that zone need fire-rated windows and doors (防火設備) |
| **Art. 22 zone** | 法22条区域 | Roofs must meet performance for resistance to flying sparks: in practice **thatch, bark and shingle roofs are not allowed for new buildings** in these zones (with limited exceptions); certain external wall requirements |
| Outside these zones | — | Fewer restrictions; thatch possible |

### 7.2 Earth walls and fire

- Traditional earth walls are fire-resistant in themselves.
- **Notification 1359 of 2000** (防火構造の構造方法) lists earth-plastered wall constructions among the methods for fire-preventive construction, with minimum plaster thicknesses (commonly cited: ≥ 30 mm for certain configurations, with higher values or additional conditions for others). It also addresses the gaps where the earth meets posts in *shinkabe* walls (exposed-frame walls).
- The details have been amended several times (most recently at least 2016). Check the current text.
- **Eaves soffits** (軒裏) with exposed rafters and boards are a common problem in quasi-fire zones. Current notifications include methods for exposed soffits with specified board thickness and blocking between rafters (面戸板 *mendoita*) (verify).

**R17-008** — **Must.** In fire zones, quasi-fire zones and Art. 22 zones, check the fire requirements for roof, external walls, eaves soffits and openings **before** fixing the design of exposed timber, *shōji*, wooden sash, thatch or shingles. Traditional expressions are often still possible within the listed methods, or under preservation-district ordinances (§3.3), but not by default.

## 8. Durability, long-life housing and warranties

### 8.1 Traditional strengths

- Traditional houses are designed for **inspection and repair** (→ Ch. 16): exposed members, open underfloor, replaceable post bases, dismountable joints.
- Earth walls buffer humidity.
- Durable species are used at the base.

### 8.2 Long-life housing certification (長期優良住宅)

- Certification under the 2008 Act gives tax and loan benefits.
- **Criteria:**
  - durability (劣化対策);
  - seismic performance;
  - maintenance;
  - energy performance (raised in 2022 to a level equivalent to ZEH insulation, 断熱等性能等級5, and primary energy grade 6: verify);
  - floor area;
  - maintenance plan.
- **Seismic criterion:** seismic grade 2 or higher, or grade 3 when the wall-quantity route is used (since 2022; verify). For buildings designed by limit strength calculation, a limit on the safety-limit storey drift; the value commonly cited for timber is **1/40 rad** (verify). This is much stricter than the drifts at which traditional frames are usually designed (1/30–1/15). It is a practical obstacle to certifying *nuki*-frame houses as long-life housing.
- **Durability criteria** (e.g. underfloor height ≥ 330 mm, inspection openings, treatment within 1 m of ground, roof-space ventilation) are generally compatible with traditional construction.

**R17-009** — **Should.** If long-life certification or a seismic grade under the Housing Quality Assurance Act is desired, determine early whether the traditional frame can meet the drift criteria. Otherwise inform the client that the house will be built to the minimum legal standard (with the traditional frame's large deformation capacity) without certification.

### 8.3 Defect warranty

New houses in Japan are subject to a mandatory **10-year defect liability** for structural parts and rainwater penetration (Housing Quality Assurance Act, 1999), secured by insurance or deposit. Warranty insurers apply their own design and construction standards (e.g. inspections of foundations and frames).

**R17-010** — **Should.** Check the warranty insurer's standards for the base detail (*ishibadate*, dowels) and for earth walls and exposed timber before the design is fixed. Some insurers require specific details or additional inspections for non-standard construction.

## 9. Energy regulations

- **From April 2025**, all new buildings, including houses, must comply with the energy efficiency standard under the Building Energy Efficiency Act:
  - insulation performance grade 4 (断熱等性能等級4);
  - primary energy consumption grade 4.
- Stricter levels (ZEH level) are planned for **2030** (as announced; verify).

**Tension with traditional construction:**
- A 60–70 mm earth wall has low thermal resistance. Earth conductivity is of the order of 0.6–1 W/m·K, so the wall's U-value is several W/m²K, far from what grade 4 requires in most regions.
- Single glazing, *shōji*, draughty *tategu*, open underfloors and exposed-rafter roofs add to this.
- Exposed-frame (*shinkabe*) walls leave no cavity for insulation.

**Climate-adapted housing (気候風土適応住宅).**
- The energy rules provide a special category for houses built with traditional methods suited to the local climate, e.g. earth walls, exposed frames, thick board walls.
- Under **MLIT Notification No. 786 of 2019** (令和元年国土交通省告示第786号), or criteria set by the local administrative agency, qualifying houses are **exempt from the envelope (insulation) standard**, and the primary-energy standard is applied in a relaxed form.
- The conditions are lists of required and optional features.

(Verify the current notification and local criteria.)

**Common strategies when full compliance is needed:**
- Insulate the **roof** (above the roof boards, preserving exposed rafters inside).
- Insulate the **floor** (under floorboards, between joists).
- Use **external insulation** on the outer side of an earth wall (大壁 on the outside, *shinkabe* inside) with a ventilated cladding.
- Use double-glazed timber windows outside the *shōji* line (a double-layer façade).
- Check **condensation risk** carefully. Earth walls are vapour-open, and adding a vapour-closed layer on the cold side traps moisture.

**R17-011** — **Must.** For a new traditional house after April 2025, determine at the concept stage whether it will comply with the energy standard by calculation or qualify as climate-adapted housing. The choice determines wall build-up, glazing and roof section.

**R17-012** — **Should.** When insulating earth-walled or exposed-frame buildings, keep all layers vapour-open toward the outside (diffusion-open membranes, wood-fibre or similar insulation). Avoid polyethylene vapour barriers on the inside of earth walls unless a hygrothermal assessment supports them.
*Why:* Earth and timber buffer and release moisture. A closed layer traps it against the timber.

## 10. Precut (CNC) joinery versus hand-cut joinery

### 10.1 What precut is

**Precut** (プレカット) means cutting joints in a factory with automated machines driven by CAD/CAM data.
- It spread in Japan from the 1970s–1980s.
- By the late 2010s it accounted for the great majority (commonly reported as **over 90%**) of the post-and-beam house frames built in Japan (verify current figure).
- Standard lines process square sections, typically 105 and 120 mm widths, in kiln-dried solid timber or glulam.
- They cut a standard repertoire of joints with rotating cutters, finishing with drilled holes for hardware.

### 10.2 Which traditional joints are reproduced

| Joint | Precut status (representative) |
|---|---|
| 腰掛け鎌継ぎ *koshikake-kama-tsugi* (sill, plates) | Standard |
| 腰掛け蟻継ぎ *koshikake-ari-tsugi* | Standard |
| 大入れ蟻掛け *ōire-arikake* (beam to beam) | Standard |
| 蟻掛け / 兜蟻 | Standard or common |
| 渡りあご *watari-ago* | Common |
| 短ほぞ *tanhozo* (post ends) | Standard (with hardware) |
| 長ほぞ + 込み栓穴 *nagahozo-komisen* | Available on many lines |
| 傾ぎ大入れ短ほぞ (girder into *tōshibashira*) | Standard |
| 追掛け大栓継ぎ *okkake-daisen-tsugi* | Available at some plants with special machines |
| 金輪継ぎ *kanawa-tsugi* | Available at some plants |
| 台持ち継ぎ *daimochi-tsugi* | Available at some plants |
| 雇いほぞ, 竿車知 *sao-shachi*, 貫穴 *nuki-ana* | Limited; often roughed by machine and finished by hand |
| Log beams (丸太梁), curved members, scribed joints to stones (*hikaritsuke*) | Generally not; hand work (some plants handle logs) |
| Bracket sets, hip rafters, *sori* | Specialist CNC in some temple workshops; generally hand work |

### 10.3 What is lost with precut

1. **Reading the individual timber.** A hand-cutting *tōryō* chooses where each piece goes according to its grain, curvature, knots and heart position (→ Ch. 03). Precut treats timber as a uniform section.
2. **Fit and *kigoroshi*.** Hand-cut joints are fitted tight and pre-compressed (*kigoroshi*, → Ch. 06). Machine joints are cut with clearances to allow assembly without fitting (typically a fraction of a millimetre to about 1 mm). They rely on hardware for tightness.
3. **Internal corners.** Rotating cutters leave **rounded internal corners** (radius equal to the cutter radius). The mating piece must be relieved, or a gap is left. Hand-cut joints have sharp internal corners.
4. **Long, complex and multi-piece joints.** Joints needing long sliding assembly, keys and wedges, or three-dimensional geometry are either unavailable or approximated.
5. **Transmission of skill.** Marking (*sumitsuke*) and cutting are where carpenters learn geometry and the reading of timber. Where all joinery is precut, these skills are lost from the workforce (→ Ch. 04).
6. **Moisture regime.** Precut assumes kiln-dried timber of stable dimension. Hand-cut traditional frames often use air-dried timber and plan for shrinkage (*mashijime*, → Ch. 15).

### 10.4 What is gained

- Speed, low cost, and accuracy of repetitive joints.
- Consistent quality with less skilled labour.
- Direct link to structural calculation and hardware schedules.

**R17-013** — **Should.** Where precut is used for a traditional-style frame, specify the joints individually, not the "standard set". Confirm the plant's capabilities for *okkake-daisen*, *kanawa*, *nagahozo-komisen* and *nuki-ana*. Plan for hand finishing of internal corners and of all joints to be exposed. Scribing to stones and log work remain hand work.

**R17-014** — **Should.** Do not mix precut clearances with traditional pin-and-wedge tightening without checking. A loose machine-cut tenon pinned with a *komisen* is not equivalent to a hand-fitted *nagahozo-komisen*.

## 11. Practical rules for designers

The following rules summarise how to approach a traditional building under current Japanese law. **All are subject to the disclaimer at the head of this chapter.**

**R17-015** — **Must.** Engage a licensed architect (建築士) of the appropriate class, and for calculation routes a structural engineer experienced in traditional frames, from the concept stage. The *tōryō* is a member of the design team from the beginning, not a contractor brought in afterwards.

**R17-016** — **Must.** Determine the legal route first (specification route, limit strength calculation, dowel exception, heritage exemption), and then design the frame to that route. Do not design a traditional frame and then look for a route.
*Why:* The route decides the base detail, the walls, the joints, the post sizes and the schedule.

**R17-017** — **Must.** Check the current text of the Act, the Enforcement Order and the notifications at the start of each project. Do not rely on this book, earlier projects or memory for article contents.

**R17-018** — **Should.** Discuss the project with the building confirmation authority (特定行政庁 or designated confirmation body) at an early stage, especially for *ishibadate*, heritage exemptions, fire-zone solutions and climate-adapted housing.

**R17-019** — **Should.** Keep the lateral system of each storey uniform in type (all deformation-type or all stiff type) and balanced. If the specification route forces stiff elements, redesign the whole storey as a stiff system rather than mixing (→ R10-067, R10-068).

**R17-020** — **Should.** In quasi-fire zones, resolve the eaves soffit, external wall and opening requirements before detailing exposed timber (R17-008).

**R17-021** — **Should.** Size posts for the post-2025 rules (loads including insulation, solar panels and heavy tiles). In earth-walled tiled houses expect 4–5 sun (120–150 mm) posts. Avoid mortise concentrations at the edges of hanging walls (→ R10-066).

**R17-022** — **Should.** Use sill-based construction (*dodai* on a footing) with *nuki* and earth walls when the project cannot bear the time and cost of the limit strength calculation route and its review. Reserve *ishibadate* for projects where the route has been agreed and budgeted (→ R10-036).

**R17-023** — **Must.** If *ishibadate* relies on sliding, the site layout must accommodate it: stone size, clearance to boundaries and other structures, flexible services, and no rigid connections to steps, verandas or annexes on separate foundations (R17-007).

**R17-024** — **Should.** Where the Notification 1460 tables require hardware at post tops or bases, prefer details that remain inspectable and compatible with the joint: concealed plates in slots, bolts with visible nuts in hidden faces. Avoid hardware that splits the timber when it shrinks (e.g. long rows of closely spaced nails near end grain).

**R17-025** — **Should.** Credit earth walls only where the construction meets the specified conditions (R17-006). Keep records (photographs of *komai*, coat thicknesses) for inspection.

**R17-026** — **Should.** Include *mashijime* (re-wedging and re-tightening) in the contract and schedule, with dates and access (→ R15-048). Structural calculations for *nuki* frames assume tight joints.

**R17-027** — **Should.** When retrofitting existing traditional buildings, use the seismic assessment methods for traditional frames (limit strength based, e.g. from the 2004 and 2019 manuals). General retrofit methods calibrated for conventional houses can undervalue *nuki* frames and prescribe stiff panels unnecessarily.

**R17-028** — **Should.** For *machiya* and historic houses, investigate whether an Art. 3(1)(iii) ordinance, a preservation district (Art. 85-3) or other special regime applies before proposing alterations that would trigger full compliance.
*Why:* A large alteration or change of use can require the whole building to be brought up to current standards. The special regimes are designed to avoid this for historic buildings.

**R17-029** — **Should.** Establish the energy compliance strategy (calculation or climate-adapted housing) at concept stage (R17-011), and keep wall build-ups vapour-open (R17-012).

**R17-030** — **Should.** Document the design basis — route, manuals, element data, assumptions about sliding, joints and walls — in a form that future repairers can read, and keep a copy with the *itazu* and the *munafuda* record (→ R15-059).

**R17-031** — **Should.** Communicate to the client the performance philosophy of the traditional frame: large deformations in a major earthquake, visible cracking of earth walls, re-wedging and re-plastering after the event, but a high margin against collapse. Contrast this with the stiff behaviour of conventional houses. Record the client's informed acceptance.
*Why:* Damage expectations differ from those of conventional houses. Clients must not mistake cracked earth walls and loose wedges after an earthquake for failure.

**R17-032** — **May.** Where the law and the budget do not allow a fully traditional frame, a hybrid can preserve much of the craft and character: a precut or conventional structural core with hand-cut exposed members, earth or board walls as secondary elements, and traditional *zōsaku*. It must be designed honestly as the system it actually is (→ R10-037).
