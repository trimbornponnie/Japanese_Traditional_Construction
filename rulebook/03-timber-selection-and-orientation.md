# Chapter 03 — Timber: Species, Selection and Orientation (木材の選定と木使い / *mokuzai no sentei to kizukai*)

**Scope.** This chapter sets out the rules by which a Japanese carpenter chooses a species for a given member, follows the timber from the standing tree to the finished stick, and places each piece in the building: which end goes down, which face is shown, which face gets the relief kerf, and which way a curved beam is turned. It covers species properties, felling and seasoning, sawing patterns, heartwood and sapwood, moisture and shrinkage, the orientation customs (*kiomote/kiura*, *moto/sue*, *se/hara*), the oral teachings of the Hōryūji master carpenter Nishioka Tsunekazu, commercial grading, and the defects to reject. Units, marking and the *sashigane* are in → Ch. 02. Tools and the workshop sequence (*kidori*, *kizami*) are in → Ch. 04. How timber movement shapes joint design and *kigoroshi* is in → Ch. 06. Sill and post details are in → Ch. 10.

## 1. Why species and orientation matter

A Japanese timber frame depends on its wood in three ways that metal-fastened framing does not:

1. **The joints *are* the wood.** Tenons, splice tongues, wedges and pins carry the load through bearing, friction and wood-on-wood shear (→ Ch. 06). Their strength depends on the species, how straight the grain runs, whether knots are present, and how dry the wood is.
2. **Much of the structure is left exposed** (*shiraki*, 白木, bare planed wood). The same stick has to be structurally sound and good-looking, so grading for appearance and grading for strength overlap.
3. **The wood keeps moving after assembly.** Shrinkage, cupping, twisting and creep go on for years. Traditional practice places each piece so that its movement tightens the joints, or at least does no harm. Most of the orientation rules in this chapter come from that aim.

**R03-001** — **Must.** Choose the species for a member by its function first (bearing, bending, rot exposure, wear, show), and only then by appearance and cost.
*Why:* an attractive but perishable wood in a sill, or a weak wood in a long-span beam, fails whatever it looks like.

**R03-002** — **Should.** Before *kidori* (木取り, allocating stock to members; → Ch. 04 §11), read every piece for moisture, heartwood proportion, grain slope, knots, twist, crown and bark side, and record the decisions on the stock or on the *itazu* (板図, the plan drawn on a board; → Ch. 02).
*Why:* orientation cannot be fixed once the joints are cut.

## 2. Wood as the carpenter sees it: anatomy, directions and terms

### 2.1 The three directions

| Direction | Japanese | Movement (green → oven-dry, typical softwood) | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Longitudinal (along the fibres) | 繊維方向 *sen'i hōkō* | ≈ 0.1–0.3 % | Negligible, except in reaction wood and juvenile core wood |
| Radial (pith → bark) | 半径方向 *hankei hōkō* / 柾目方向 | ≈ 2–5 % | About half the tangential value |
| Tangential (along the rings) | 接線方向 *sessen hōkō* / 板目方向 | ≈ 5–10 % | Largest. This causes cupping and checking |

The tangential-to-radial ratio is roughly **2 : 1** in most Japanese conifers. This one fact explains why flat-sawn boards cup, why boxed-heart posts check, and why quarter-sawn stock is valued for fittings.

### 2.2 Terms used throughout

| Term | Japanese | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Pith / heart | 髄 *zui*, 心 (芯) *shin* | The centre of the stem |
| Heartwood | 赤身 *akami* (also 心材 *shinzai*) | Inner, dead, extractive-rich wood. Usually darker and more durable |
| Sapwood | 白太 *shirata* (also 辺材 *henzai*) | Outer, formerly living wood. Pale, perishable, attractive to insects |
| Earlywood / latewood | 早材 *sōzai* (春材) / 晩材 *banzai* (夏材) | The soft light band and the hard dark band of each growth ring |
| Growth ring | 年輪 *nenrin* | Ring count per sun is a traditional index of quality; tight rings (目が詰んだ *me ga tsunda*) are prized |
| Butt end | 元口 *motokuchi*, 元 *moto* | The end that was toward the root |
| Top end | 末口 *suekuchi*, 末 *sue* | The end that was toward the crown |
| Bark side | 木表 *kiomote* | The face of a sawn piece that was nearer the bark |
| Pith side | 木裏 *kiura* | The face nearer the pith |
| Crown / back | 背 *se* | The convex side of a naturally bowed piece |
| Belly | 腹 *hara* | The concave side |
| Grain with / against | 順目 *naraime* / 逆目 *sakame* | Planing direction relative to the grain slope |
| End grain | 木口 *koguchi* | Cross-section face |
| Edge grain face / flat grain face | 柾目面 *masame-men* / 板目面 *itame-men* | Faces cut radially / tangentially |

**R03-003** — **Must.** Every carpenter and supplier on a project uses these terms in the same sense. In particular *moto/sue* and *kiomote/kiura* are marked on the stock at *kidori* (e.g. an arrow toward *sue*, and a symbol on the *kiomote*) and the marks survive until the piece is cut.
*Why:* once a piece has been planed square, its original orientation can often only be read from the end grain, and after end-cutting even that is lost.

### 3.2 Species entries

#### Hinoki (檜, *hinoki*)
- **Properties.** Light but stiff and strong for its weight. Straight, fine grain. Low movement. The heartwood resists decay well. It planes to a satiny sheen (艶 *tsuya*) that no finish improves on. Its fragrance lasts for decades.
- **Traditional uses.** Posts, beams and bracket sets (*kumimono*, → Ch. 12) of shrines and temples; *shinmei-zukuri* shrines; best-quality house posts, sills and *keta*; bath fittings; hinoki bark (*hiwada*, 檜皮) for roofing (*hiwadabuki*, 檜皮葺).
- **Why.** In Japanese experience hinoki's long-term performance is unequalled. A widely repeated finding from wood-science research on aged Hōryūji timber (associated with Kohara Jirō, 小原二郎) is that hinoki's stiffness and strength *rise* for roughly the first 100–300 years after felling and then decline only slowly. Treat the exact curve as reported rather than settled; the reported figures vary between retellings.
- **Sources of supply.** Kiso (木曽) hinoki is the benchmark. The Kiso forests were protected under the Owari domain (tradition: "one tree, one head", 木一本首一つ *ki ippon kubi hitotsu*), and the *Jingū Birin* (神宮備林) reserve supplies Ise. Other named regional supplies include Owase (尾鷲), Yoshino (吉野) and Tōnō (東濃).

#### Sugi (杉, *sugi*)
- **Properties.** Soft, light, easy to work. Straight-grained in plantation stock. Wide rings in fast-grown timber. Heartwood colour varies from pink-red to near black (黒芯 *kuroshin*: the black heart holds more water and dries slowly). Surfaces dent easily. Sapwood rots quickly.
- **Traditional uses.** Rafters (*taruki*, 垂木), roof boarding (*noji-ita*, 野地板), ceiling boards (*tenjō-ita*, 天井板; figured *nakamoku* 中杢 boards are prized), wall boards, posts in ordinary houses (especially Kantō and Tōhoku), *kōshi* lattices and *tategu*. Also *yakisugi* (→ Ch. 04 §13) and *kitayama-maruta* (北山丸太, polished sugi logs) for *sukiya* alcove posts (→ Ch. 13).
- **Why.** Sugi is abundant, straight and long, easy to split and plane, light to lift, and handsome as boards. Its low stiffness keeps it out of long-span heavy beams unless the section is generous.
- **Named sources.** Yoshino, Akita (天然秋田杉, the old-growth benchmark), Kitayama, Obi (飫肥, resinous and prized for ship planking), Yanase (魚梁瀬).

#### Keyaki (欅, *keyaki*)
- **Properties.** Heavy, hard, strong, ring-porous. Coarse, handsome figure. Very high movement and a tendency to twist unless thoroughly seasoned. Heartwood durability is good. Hard on edges.
- **Traditional uses.** Main posts and ornamental members of Edo-period (and some Muromachi) temples and gates. Large timbers for halls such as the Higashi Honganji *Goeidō*. Carved elements (*kaerumata*, *kibana*). Heavy thresholds and sills at gates. The great central post (*daikokubashira*, 大黒柱) and doorway front beam (*agari-gamachi*, 上がり框) of wealthy minka. Temple *shikii*. Drum bodies. *Tamamoku* veneer and panels.
- **Why.** Strength, wear resistance and display. Its movement means it must be seasoned for years (large keyaki posts traditionally sat for many years before use) and must not be combined rigidly with low-movement softwoods in the same wide panel.
- **Caution.** Keyaki cut and assembled green twists visibly. A keyaki post that is visibly twisting while it dries should not be used for a long, exposed member.

#### Matsu: akamatsu and kuromatsu (松, *matsu*)
- **Properties.** High bending strength and toughness. Rich in resin, especially the heartwood of old slow-grown trees (*jimatsu* 地松, domestic pine, as opposed to imported Douglas fir). Durability in intermittently wet exposure is only medium, but pine kept permanently waterlogged lasts very long, which is why it was used for piles (*matsukui*, 松杭). The sapwood is prone to blue stain (青変 *aohen*) and insect attack.
- **Traditional uses.** Roof and floor beams, especially the naturally curved, round or half-round beams of minka (丸太梁 *maruta-bari*, 牛梁 *ushibari*, 太鼓梁 *taiko-bari*, sawn on two faces only) and *kōjō-bari*. Purlins. Sleepers. Foundation piles. Bridge members.
- **Why.** Pine combines bending strength, stiffness and toughness with the ability to span and carry heavy roof and snow loads in round form, which saves sawing. Its natural curvature was used deliberately: crown up (→ §10). Resin gives some resistance to moisture cycling.
- **Current note.** Domestic pine of beam size is scarce because of pine wilt disease (松枯れ *matsugare*) since the later 20th century. Beimatsu is now the usual substitute.

#### Hiba / asunaro (檜葉, 翌檜)
- **Properties.** Heartwood contains hinokitiol and related compounds. It has one of the highest resistances to decay and termites of any Japanese timber. Workable, stable, strongly scented.
- **Traditional uses.** Sills (*dodai*, 土台), floor sleepers and posts in wet locations, bathrooms, and shrines and temples in Tōhoku and Hokkaidō. Aomori hiba is the principal source. The Chūsonji *Konjikidō* (中尊寺金色堂) is often stated to be built of hiba. Asunaro is one of the "Five Trees of Kiso" (木曽五木 *Kiso goboku*: hinoki, sawara, asunaro, nezuko, kōyamaki).
- **Why.** Durability where the building meets the ground.

#### Kuri (栗, *kuri*)
- **Properties.** Tannin-rich, very rot-resistant heartwood. Hard and heavy. Splits easily along the grain. Checks on drying. Stains blue-black in contact with iron and water.
- **Traditional uses.** *Dodai* sills, floor-post footings (*tsuka*), sleepers, *ōbiki* (大引) in humid regions, railway sleepers (historical). Chōna-textured boards and posts in *sukiya* (栗の名栗 *kuri no naguri*). Excavated Jōmon posts (e.g. the large posts at Sannai-Maruyama) are chestnut.
- **Why.** Rot resistance at ground level; texture for rustic display.
- **Caution.** Splitting. Pre-bore for nails and pins, and keep pin holes away from ends (→ Ch. 09).

#### Tsuga (栂, *tsuga*)
- **Properties.** Hard for a conifer, dense, very fine and straight grain, pale with a faint pink-brown tone, good stability. It can splinter along the latewood.
- **Traditional uses.** High-quality posts and fittings (*kamoi*, *shikii*, *nageshi*) in Kansai residences and *sukiya*. A house finished throughout in tsuga (*tsuga-bushin*, 栂普請) was historically regarded as a luxury.
- **Why.** Fine grain, wear resistance on sliding surfaces, stability.

#### Momi (樅, *momi*)
- **Properties.** White, odourless, easy to work. Low durability.
- **Uses.** Ceiling boards, interior boards, formwork, *sotoba* grave tablets, coffins, packing, and wedding and ritual objects (being odourless). Not for exposed exterior or ground-contact members.

#### Kaya (榧, *kaya*)
- **Properties.** Elastic, water-resistant, fragrant, fine-grained, golden.
- **Uses.** Best-quality *go* and *shōgi* boards (its best-known use), baths and buckets, boat parts, occasionally small shrine members and carvings. Rare and expensive in large sizes.

#### Kusu (楠・樟, *kusu*)
- **Properties.** Aromatic camphor. Resists insects. Interlocked grain, so it tends to twist and is difficult to plane cleanly, but it carves well.
- **Uses.** Asuka-period Buddhist sculpture (many early images are camphor), transom carvings (*ranma*), chests, temple ornament, boat building. Seldom used as a straight structural member.

#### Kiri (桐, *kiri*)
- **Properties.** Lightest domestic timber. Very low movement. Good insulator. Low strength.
- **Uses.** Chests (*kiri-dansu*), storage boxes, *geta*, and sometimes ceiling boards and fittings in *sukiya*. Not structural.

#### Sawara (椹, *sawara*)
- **Properties.** Like a lighter, softer, less-scented hinoki. Splits cleanly. Water-resistant.
- **Uses.** Tubs and buckets (*oke*), rice tubs, bath fittings, ceiling boards. Split shingles (*kokera-ita*, 柿板) for *kokerabuki* roofs; hinoki and sugi shingles are also used. Sawara is one of the Five Trees of Kiso.

#### Kōyamaki (高野槇, *kōyamaki*)
- **Properties.** Resists water and rot. Easy to work.
- **Uses.** Baths and tubs. Ancient coffins (Kofun period). Posts and members in wet positions. Reputedly also bridge piers. One of the Five Trees of Kiso.

#### Kashi and nara (樫・楢, oaks)
- **Properties.** Very hard, heavy, tough and elastic. Kashi is the hardest commonly available domestic wood. Nara shows silver-ray figure (*torafu*) when quarter-sawn.
- **Uses in construction.** Draw pins (*komisen*, 込み栓), keys (*shachisen*, 車知栓), wedges (*kusabi*, 楔), splines and false tenons (*yatoi*, 雇い), dowels (*dabo*, 太枘) (→ Ch. 09). Wear strips inlaid in *shikii* grooves (*umegashi*, 埋樫). Rollers and skids.
- **Uses in tools.** Plane bodies (*kanna-dai*: shirakashi is the classic wood, akagashi is used too), mallet heads, *gennō* handles, chisel handles, and the soles of marking gauges (→ Ch. 04).
- **Why.** A pin or wedge must be harder and stiffer than the timbers it joins, so that it crushes into them rather than being crushed. It must also be tough enough not to shear across the grain.

#### Sakura (桜, *sakura*)
- **Properties.** Fine, even texture. Stable. Planes and carves superbly.
- **Uses.** Woodblocks (*hangi*), *tokobashira* with the bark left on (皮付き *kawatsuki*), *tokogamachi* (床框, alcove front rail), fittings, furniture.

#### Imported timbers in modern practice
- **Beihiba (米ヒバ).** Hiba-like durability. Widely used for *dodai* and wet-area members, and as a hinoki substitute in shrine work.
- **Beimatsu (米松, Douglas fir).** Long, strong, stiff beams with high bending capacity. It is the default heavy beam in modern *zairai* framing, and is also used in restoration where domestic pine of adequate size is unobtainable. Resinous; watch for resin bleed on *keshō* surfaces.
- **Beisugi (米杉, western red cedar).** Exterior siding, fences and shingles. Very stable and durable, but too weak for structure.
- **Beitsuga (米栂, western hemlock).** Cheap posts and joists. **Not** durable: never in sills or ground contact without treatment.
- **Taiwan hinoki (台湾檜) and benihi (紅檜).** Available historically in huge old-growth dimensions. Used for major 20th-century shrine and temple works where Japanese hinoki of adequate size was lacking; the Yakushiji reconstructions directed by Nishioka Tsunekazu are the best-known case. Taiwan ended large-scale logging of these old-growth forests around the early 1990s, so new supply is essentially gone. Treat the exact year as approximate.

**R03-004** — **Must.** Do not use non-durable species (momi, beitsuga, spruce, sapwood of any species) in ground-contact, splash-zone or sill positions unless they are preservative-treated and the treatment is permitted for the work.
*Why:* decay begins at the sill and spreads into posts through end grain.

**R03-005** — **Should.** When a traditional specification names a domestic species that cannot be obtained, substitute only with a species of equal or better durability *and* stiffness for that position. Beihiba for hiba or hinoki sills and beimatsu for pine beams are the accepted substitutions. Record the substitution.
*Why:* restorers and later repairers (→ Ch. 16) need to know what is in the building.

## 4. Classic assignments of species to members

The table below states the traditional first choice. Regional practice varies. The Kantō, where sugi was abundant, used sugi for posts in ordinary houses. Kansai sukiya favoured tsuga and hinoki. In Tōhoku, hiba was the default durable timber.

| Member | First choice | Common alternatives | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| Shrine/temple posts (*hashira*) | Hinoki | Keyaki (Edo temples), Taiwan hinoki (20th c.) | Durability, stiffness, straightness, appearance |
| Temple ornamental members (*kaerumata*, *kibana*, carved *ranma*) | Keyaki | Kusu, hinoki | Carving quality, hardness, figure |
| Heavy sills and gate thresholds | Keyaki, kuri | Hiba | Wear and rot resistance |
| *Dodai* (foundation sill) | Hinoki heartwood, hiba, kuri | Beihiba, treated timber | Rot and termite resistance |
| House posts, ordinary | Sugi, hinoki | Beitsuga (modern, low grade) | Availability, straightness |
| *Daikokubashira* (central post) | Keyaki | Hinoki, matsu | Strength, display |
| Long-span beams, minka roof beams (*ushibari*, *maruta-bari*) | Akamatsu, kuromatsu | Beimatsu, sugi (deeper section) | Bending strength, natural curvature |
| *Keta* (wall plate/eaves beam) | Matsu, hinoki | Sugi, beimatsu | Bending, bearing |
| Rafters (*taruki*) | Sugi | Hinoki (shrines), matsu | Light, straight, long |
| Roof boarding (*noji-ita*) | Sugi | Matsu, momi | Cheap, light, easy to nail |
| Ceiling boards | Sugi (*nakamoku*, *masame*) | Hinoki, momi, sawara, kiri | Appearance, lightness |
| *Shikii*, *kamoi* | Hinoki, tsuga, matsu | Sugi (akami), sakura | Stability, wear |
| *Tokobashira* (alcove post) | Kitayama sugi log, hinoki, keyaki, sakura, kuri, *shitan* etc. | — | Aesthetic choice (→ Ch. 13) |
| *Tategu* (sliding doors, shōji frames) | Sugi (*akami masame*), hinoki | Beisugi, spruce | Stability, lightness, straight grain |
| Pins, wedges, keys | Kashi | Keyaki, nara, bamboo (for small pins) | Hardness > timbers joined |
| Wet-area members, baths | Hinoki, hiba, kōyamaki, sawara | Beihiba | Water resistance |
| Exterior cladding | Sugi (incl. *yakisugi*) | Beisugi, hinoki | Durability of charred or heartwood surface |
| Piles under water | Matsu | — | Durable when permanently saturated |

**R03-006** — **Should.** Shrine and temple posts, and exposed structure of first-rank buildings, are hinoki heartwood unless the design tradition specifies otherwise (e.g. keyaki posts in Edo-period temple halls).

**R03-007** — **Should.** Use keyaki for carved and ornamental temple members and for heavy thresholds. Do not use it for thin members that must stay straight unless it has been seasoned for years.
*Why:* high movement and twist.

**R03-008** — **Should.** Use pine for minka-type long-span and naturally curved beams (*ushibari*, *maruta-bari*, *taiko-bari*), set crown up (→ R03-041).
*Why:* bending strength and toughness, and the ability to use a naturally bent log with minimal sawing.

**R03-009** — **Should.** Use kuri, hiba or hinoki heartwood (or beihiba) for *dodai*, *tsuka* footings and sleepers.
*Why:* rot and termite resistance at the zone of greatest moisture.

**R03-010** — **Should.** Use sugi for rafters, roof boarding, ceilings and ordinary boards.
*Why:* light, straight, abundant, easy to work, attractive as boards.

**R03-011** — **Must.** Make pins, keys and wedges from a wood distinctly harder than the members they join: kashi is standard, and keyaki or nara are acceptable. They must be straight-grained, quarter-split or rived rather than sawn across the grain, and dry (drier than the members, → Ch. 09).
*Why:* a soft pin crushes and loosens. A cross-grained pin shears. A wet pin shrinks loose.

**R03-012** — **Should.** Avoid mixing species with very different movement in one rigidly joined panel or glued-up surface (e.g. keyaki frame with a sugi panel fixed on all sides). Where different species meet, provide for their relative movement: float panels in grooves, and leave expansion in grooved joints.

## 5. From tree to member

### 5.1 Selecting the standing tree: "buy the mountain"

The Hōryūji carpenters' oral tradition (→ §11) teaches that the timber for a temple should be bought as a stand of trees on a mountain, seen growing, not as logs in a yard: 「堂塔建立の用材は木を買わず山を買え」 (*dōtō konryū no yōzai wa ki o kawazu yama o kae*, "for timber to build halls and pagodas, do not buy trees, buy the mountain"). The point is practical. From the living stand the master can see each tree's aspect (which side faced south), its slope, its lean, its spiral, and the soil. These tell him how the timber will behave.

**R03-013** — **Should.** For first-rank work, select trees standing, and record for each tree its aspect (north/south face), its position on the slope, its lean and any visible spiral, so the members can later be placed "as they grew" (→ R03-049).

### 5.2 Felling season

Traditional practice across Japan is to fell in the **dormant season**, roughly from the autumn equinox (秋の彼岸) to before the spring flush. Many regions say autumn to midwinter (about September/October to February). The reasons given:

- Sap flow and water content in the sapwood are at their lowest, so the wood dries faster and checks less.
- Stored starches are lower (the claim is debated), which reduces attraction to powder-post beetles and staining fungi.
- Insects and fungi are inactive during early drying.
- Farm labour was available and snow let logs be skidded.

**Lunar-phase felling** (新月伐採 *shingetsu bassai*, felling on the waning moon or new moon of winter) is presented by some modern suppliers as traditional, partly under the influence of the Central European *Mondholz* movement since the 1990s–2000s. Treat it as **tradition and lore**: controlled studies of a lunar effect on wood properties are inconclusive or contested. The saying 「木六竹八塀十郎」 (*ki roku, take hachi, hei jūrō*: "trees in the sixth, bamboo in the eighth, walls in the tenth [month]") also survives. How its months map onto the old lunar calendar, and so onto felling practice, is interpreted differently by different writers, so do not cite it as a precise rule.

**R03-014** — **Should.** Fell in the dormant season (autumn to late winter).
*Why:* lower moisture and less biological attack during early seasoning. Lunar timing within that season **may** be followed as a custom, but it is not a substitute for proper seasoning.

### 5.3 Leaf seasoning (葉枯らし, *hagarashi*)

After felling, the tree is left **lying in the forest with its crown and branches intact**, typically with the top pointing downhill, for roughly **three months or more** (practice ranges from about 1 to 6 months). The leaves go on transpiring and draw water out of the stem. Sugi in particular dries markedly: black-hearted sugi, whose heartwood holds a lot of water, benefits most. Prefectural forestry trials report substantial moisture reduction. They also report that drying is slow in the coldest months and that timing matters, with late-summer to autumn felling often drying fastest in the field.

Benefits cited: reduced weight for extraction; more uniform colour (the heartwood of sugi lightens toward a better red); less checking and warp in later drying; less need for high-temperature kiln drying.

**R03-015** — **Should.** Leaf-season sugi and hinoki destined for exposed or high-grade members, leaving the crown on for at least about three months, before bucking.

### 5.4 Bucking and transport

- **Bucking** (玉切り *tamagiri*) is cutting the stem into log lengths (玉 *tama*). Lengths are chosen for the planned members plus trimming allowance: traditional lengths are multiples of the *shaku* and *ken*, e.g. 13 *shaku* (≈ 3.94 m) and 20 *shaku* (≈ 6.06 m) for posts and *keta* (→ Ch. 02).
- Historically logs were moved by timber slides (修羅 *shura*), rafting (筏流し *ikada-nagashi*) and floating. Long soaking leached sap and was believed to reduce later checking and insect attack. The *soma* (杣, fellers) and *kobiki* (木挽, sawyers) were separate trades from the *daiku* (→ Ch. 04 §10).

**R03-016** — **Should.** Buck so that knots, sweep (curvature) and defects fall where they can be cut away or placed harmlessly. Allow at least about 1 *sun* (≈ 30 mm) of trimming length per end on logs, and more where end checking is expected.

### 5.5 From splitting to sawing: historical note

In ancient Japan large timbers were converted mainly by **splitting** (打ち割り *uchiwari*) with wedges and axes, then dressed with the *chōna* (adze) and *yariganna* (spear plane) (→ Ch. 04). This is why ancient work favoured easily split, straight-grained species (hinoki, sugi). The two-man frame rip saw *ōga* (大鋸) appeared in about the 14th–15th centuries, and the one-man broad rip saw *maebiki-ōga* (前挽大鋸) spread from about the 15th–16th centuries. Together they made sawn boards and posts from less straight-grained logs routine. Dates are approximate, and specialists differ on the precise chronology.

**R03-018** — **Should.** Take *shihō-masa* and *nihō-masa* posts only from logs large enough that the pith lies well outside the post, typically where the log diameter is at least about 2–3 times the post width. Show the *masame* faces to the principal room.

## 6. Heartwood and sapwood, boxed heart and heart-free, and the relief kerf

### 6.1 *Akami* and *shirata*

- **Akami (赤身, heartwood)** has extractives and blocked vessels or tracheids. It is more decay-resistant and less attractive to insects. It is the preferred part for durability-critical members.
- **Shirata (白太, sapwood)** is pale and absorbent, with low decay resistance. Powder-post beetles (*hiratakikuimushi*, ヒラタキクイムシ) attack starch-rich sapwood of hardwoods and some softwoods. In sugi the sapwood is conspicuously white against the red heart. A board or post showing both is called *genpei* (源平, "red and white", after the Minamoto/Taira colours), a lower appearance grade for high-grade work although some sukiya designers seek it deliberately.

**R03-019** — **Must.** *Dodai*, sleepers, footings, members in the splash zone, and exterior members without roof protection are *akami* throughout, or at minimum have no *shirata* on the faces exposed to wetting.
*Why:* sapwood rots first, and decay spreads from it into the heart.

**R03-020** — **Should.** Where some *shirata* must be used, turn it to the dry, ventilated, inspectable side, and keep it out of joint zones (tenons, mortise walls). Never put *shirata* in a tenon at the base of a post.

### 6.2 Boxed heart (*shinmochi*) vs heart-free (*shinsari*)

A boxed-heart post (心持ち材) contains the pith. As it dries, the tangential shrinkage (≈ 2 × radial) cannot be accommodated around the complete ring, so the post **checks radially**, opening one or more V-cracks from the surface toward the pith (干割れ *hiware*, drying checks). A heart-free piece (心去り材) can shrink more freely, so it checks less, but it cups or distorts according to where it was cut.

Traditional ordinary-house posts are *shinmochi* (one post per small log). High-grade exposed posts in *shoin* and *sukiya* rooms are often *shinsari*, cut from large logs (*nihō-masa*, *shihō-masa*). Shrine and temple posts of large diameter are *shinmochi* or *shinsari* depending on log size. Ancient practice with huge logs sometimes split them into quarters (四つ割り *yotsuwari*) to produce several heart-free posts.

**R03-021** — **Should.** For exposed high-grade posts, prefer *shinsari*. Where *shinmochi* posts are used exposed, control checking with a *sewari* (§6.3) or with adequate drying, and accept that checks will occur.

**R03-022** — **Should.** In boxed-heart beams, place the member so that the principal check, which follows the widest face, runs on a side face near the neutral axis and not on the top or bottom face near joints. Where a *sewari* is used on a beam (uncommon), it goes on the top face, where it is hidden.

### 6.3 The relief kerf (背割り, *sewari*)

A *sewari* is a single saw kerf run along the length of a boxed-heart post, on one face, **to the depth of the pith** (about half the section). It gives the post a planned opening: the drying stress is released at that one kerf, which opens into a V, instead of in random checks on the show faces. After the post has dried, or at erection, wedges or slips (*ya*, 矢 / *kusabi*) may be driven into the kerf to hold it open evenly. On modern kiln-dried posts the kerf is often cut before drying, and it opens during drying.

```
     Plan of a 4-sun boxed-heart post with sewari (hidden face = back)

            show face (mitsuke, 見付け) — toward the room
          +----------------------+
          |                      |
          |          o  pith     |
          |          |           |
          |          |  kerf     |   kerf depth ≈ to the pith
          +----------|-----------+
               back face (mikaeshi, 見返し) — into the wall
```

**Which face gets the *sewari*: the rule.** The kerf goes on the face that will be **hidden** (見え隠れ *miekakure*): the face buried in a wall (in *ōkabe* construction), the face against a wall (in *shinkabe*), or the face toward a closet or a lesser room. It never goes on the face presented to the principal room or the *toko*. The best-looking face (fewest knots, best grain) is chosen first as the show face (*mitsuke*, 見付け); the *sewari* then goes on the opposite face (*mikaeshi*, 見返し), or on a hidden side face where the opposite face is also visible.

Additional placement considerations:
- **Keep the kerf away from the faces receiving the principal joints**, where practicable. As the kerf opens, the faces either side of it bow slightly. A mortise for a *nuki*, *sashimono* or *kamoi* cut across the kerf face has one wall weakened by the kerf. If the only hidden face must take a mortise, accept it, but centre the kerf so it passes through the mortise and does not split one of its walls.
- **Corner posts and free-standing posts** have no hidden face. Either use *shinsari* stock, accept natural checks, or cut the *sewari* on the face least seen (e.g. toward the exterior wall under cladding) and fill it (*umeki*, 埋木) with a matching slip after drying.
- **Round posts** in shrine and temple work are not given a *sewari* as a rule (large-diameter hinoki is often *shinsari* or allowed to check naturally). Treat this as a general observation, not a fixed rule.
- **Tokobashira** (alcove posts) take the *sewari* on the face against the alcove wall.

**R03-023** — **Must.** Place the *sewari* on a hidden face, never on the show face (*mitsuke*) of a post.
*Why:* the kerf opens by several millimetres and is the post's permanent weak-looking side.

**R03-024** — **Should.** Cut the *sewari* to the pith (≈ half the post width) over the full length of the post, from *moto* to *sue*. Some carpenters stop the kerf a short distance short of the ends; this is a school variant. Wedge it open evenly after drying if the kerf closes unevenly or twists.
*Why:* a kerf that stops short of the pith does not relieve the stress, and the post checks anyway on another face.

**R03-025** — **Should.** Orient the *sewari* face so that it does not coincide with the wall of a principal mortise. If unavoidable, the kerf should pass through the mortise, not alongside it.

**R03-026** — **May.** Kiln-dried posts with a pre-cut *sewari* may be used as received, but check that the kerf has opened straight. A kerf that spirals along the post indicates spiral grain (→ §13); such a post should not be used where it will twist a wall or a *kamoi*.

## 7. Moisture, drying and shrinkage

### 7.1 Definitions

- **Moisture content (含水率 *gansui-ritsu*, MC)** = mass of water / oven-dry mass of wood × 100 %. Green sugi heartwood can exceed 100–200 %; black-heart sugi is notoriously wet.
- **Fibre saturation point (繊維飽和点, FSP)** ≈ 25–30 %. Shrinkage begins only below this.
- **Equilibrium moisture content (平衡含水率, EMC)**: the MC the wood settles at in its environment. Outdoors under cover in much of Japan this is about **14–17 %**, averaging about 15 %. Heated interiors in winter are about **8–12 %**, and lower near floor heating.

### 7.2 Shrinkage coefficients

Representative **average shrinkage per 1 % change in MC** (below FSP), as used in Japanese timber engineering literature. These are approximate values: individual sources differ by ±20–30 %.

| Species | Tangential (%/%MC) | Radial (%/%MC) | Longitudinal (%/%MC) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sugi | ≈ 0.25 | ≈ 0.10 | ≈ 0.01 |
| Hinoki | ≈ 0.23 | ≈ 0.12 | ≈ 0.01 |
| Akamatsu | ≈ 0.28–0.30 | ≈ 0.14–0.17 | ≈ 0.01 |
| Keyaki | ≈ 0.28–0.30 | ≈ 0.17–0.19 | ≈ 0.01 |
| Mizunara | ≈ 0.35 | ≈ 0.18 | ≈ 0.01 |
| Kiri | ≈ 0.20 | ≈ 0.08 | small |

**Worked example.** A sugi post of 4 *sun* (≈ 120 mm) is installed at 25 % MC and dries in service to 15 %, a change of 10 %:
- Tangential: 0.25 × 10 = 2.5 % → ≈ 3.0 mm
- Radial: 0.10 × 10 = 1.0 % → ≈ 1.2 mm

A 4-*sun* mortise in that post, cut when wet across the tangential direction, will therefore become about 3 mm narrower. A 1-*sun* (30 mm) *nuki* passing through a mortise that runs tangentially will see its fit tighten, because the mortise walls shrink onto it, while the *nuki* itself shrinks in its own thickness. This is why the orientation of mortises relative to the rings, and the moisture of both parts, decide whether a joint tightens or loosens with time (→ Ch. 06, *kigoroshi* and allowances).

### 7.3 Drying methods

| Method | Japanese | Practice | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Leaf seasoning | 葉枯らし *hagarashi* | Tree felled and left with crown (§5.3) | First stage; reduces sapwood and black-heart moisture |
| Water seasoning | 水中貯木 *suichū choboku* | Logs stored in ponds or rivers | Historical. Leaches sap; prevents checking and staining while logs wait |
| Air drying | 天然乾燥 *tennen kansō* | Stickered stacks (桟積み *sanzumi*) under cover, sticks (桟木 *sangi*) in vertical alignment, ends sealed | Slow. Preserves colour, oils and scent |
| Low-temperature kiln | 低温乾燥 | ≈ 40–60 °C | Closer to air-dried quality |
| Medium/high-temperature kiln | 中温・高温乾燥 | ≈ 80–120 °C (incl. *kōon setto* 高温セット for surface set) | Fast. Can cause internal checks (内部割れ *naibu ware*, honeycomb), loss of scent and oils, and colour change |

**Traditional rules of thumb for air drying** (to be treated as rough guides):
- Boards: "one year per *sun* of thickness" is often heard, comparable to the Western "one year per inch".
- Posts and beams: at least one to several years under cover. Large keyaki: many years.
- Stickers at about 45–60 cm centres (about 1.5–2 *shaku*), aligned vertically so the load passes straight down. Stack on level bearers clear of the ground.

**Modern targets** (Japanese Agricultural Standard, JAS, for sawn lumber; consult the current edition):
- Structural sawn lumber: grades **D15, D20, D25** (MC ≤ 15 %, ≤ 20 %, ≤ 25 %).
- Finishing and joinery lumber: typically **≤ 15 %**, or ≤ 18 % for some classes (treat the class names as approximate).
- Practitioners of traditional joinery commonly aim at **≤ 20 %** for structural members at cutting, and **≤ 15 %** for *keshō* members, fittings and *tategu*, with *tategu* and *kumiko* stock lower still (≈ 10–12 %).

**R03-027** — **Must.** Know the MC of every structural member at the time of *kizami*. Use a meter, or oven-dry samples for critical work, and record it.
*Why:* joint allowances and *kigoroshi* depend on it (→ Ch. 06).

**R03-028** — **Should.** Structural members for traditional joinery should be at or below about 20 % MC at cutting, and exposed finish members and fittings at or below about 15 %. *Tategu*, *kumiko* and panels should be drier still, seasoned to the EMC of the room they will serve.

**R03-029** — **Should.** Prefer air-dried or low-temperature-dried timber for members carrying pinned or wedged joints (*komisen*, *shachisen*, *okkake-daisen*). Reject high-temperature-dried stock that shows internal checking at cross-cuts.
*Why:* internal honeycomb checks weaken tenons, pin holes and splice tongues invisibly.

**R03-030** — **Must.** Pins, wedges and keys must be drier than the members they fix (≈ 10–12 %). *Nuki* should be as dry as, or drier than, the posts they pass through.
*Why:* a pin that shrinks after driving loosens. A post that shrinks onto a dry *nuki* tightens the frame.

**R03-031** — **Should.** When timber must be used partially green (common in historical minka and still in some regional practice), design and orient joints so that shrinkage *tightens* them: mortise walls shrinking onto tenons, wedged joints re-driven after the first season (増し締め *mashijime*, → Ch. 09, Ch. 15). Allow visible gaps to form at shoulders rather than inside the load path.

**R03-032** — **Should.** Seal the end grain (木口 *koguchi*) of logs and freshly cut members with paper, wax or end-sealer during drying and storage.
*Why:* end grain loses moisture 10–15 times faster than side grain, and end checks can run into tenon zones.

## 8. Orientation rules I: bark side and pith side (*kiomote / kiura*)

### 8.1 How a flat-sawn board moves

Because tangential shrinkage exceeds radial shrinkage, and because the rings on the bark side of a flat-sawn board are longer and more tangential than those on the pith side, **a flat-sawn board cups as it dries so that the bark side (*kiomote*) becomes concave and the pith side (*kiura*) becomes convex.** The rings try to straighten out. Carpenters say "wood warps toward its *kiomote*" (木表側に反る *kiomote-gawa ni soru*).

```
     End view of a flat-sawn board, before and after drying

     kiomote (bark side)          kiomote — becomes CONCAVE
     ______________              \______________/
    (  ( ( (o) ) )  )    →        \            /
     ‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾                ‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾
     kiura (pith side)            kiura — becomes CONVEX
```

Surface character differs too:
- The **kiomote** surface generally planes cleaner and wears more evenly. It is regarded as the "good" face (表 *omote*, front).
- The **kiura** surface, especially in flat-sawn sugi and pine, is prone to *mebore* (目ぼれ): the hard latewood lifts from the soft earlywood as ridges or splinters with wear and weathering. It also tends to raise splinters (*sasakure*, ささくれ).

### 8.2 The general rule and its applications

**General rule:** *show the kiomote*. Boards, panels and linings are fixed with the bark side toward the viewer or the user, except where a specific reason (below) reverses it.

| Element | Face up / toward the room | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Floor boards (*yuka-ita*, *enkō-ita* 縁甲板) | **Kiomote up** | Better wearing surface; avoids *mebore* splinters underfoot. Cupping (edges rising) is restrained by nailing and tongue-and-groove |
| Ceiling boards (*tenjō-ita*) | **Kiomote down (to the room)** | Shows the better face |
| Wall panelling (*hame-ita* 羽目板) | **Kiomote to the room** | Same |
| Shelf boards (*tana-ita*), *toko-ita* (床板, alcove floor board) | **Kiomote up** | Same |
| *Shikii* (敷居, grooved sill for sliding doors) | **Kiomote up**; grooves ploughed in the kiomote | See §8.3 |
| *Kamoi* (鴨居, grooved head for sliding doors) | **Kiomote down**; grooves ploughed in the kiomote | See §8.3 |
| Open verandah boards (*nure-en* 濡れ縁, fully exposed) | **Variant:** some carpenters lay kiura up so that the board crowns and sheds water; others keep kiomote up for wear | School and regional variant; state which on the drawings |
| Exterior horizontal cladding | Kiomote out (usual) | Better weathering face; fixings resist cupping |

### 8.3 *Shikii* and *kamoi*: the grooved face

The rule taught in Japanese carpentry, and stated in Japanese licensing examination material, is:

> **Grooves in *shikii* and *kamoi* are cut in the *kiomote*.** The *shikii* is laid kiura down (kiomote up), and the *kamoi* is fixed kiura up (kiomote down).

**Reason as usually given:** if the grooves were in the *kiura*, the drying member would become convex on the grooved side. The grooved face would crown, and the running surfaces and the ridges between the grooves (*hibata*, 樋端) would distort in the direction that tightens the sliding panels, so the doors bind. With the grooves in the *kiomote*, any cupping works away from the doors. A second reason is surface quality: the kiomote gives a smoother, harder-wearing running surface, with no *mebore* in the grooves.

In practice both members are quarter-sawn or *oimasa* where possible, which reduces cupping altogether, and the rule then governs the residual movement. Hard wear strips (*umegashi*, 埋樫) or modern sliding tape (敷居すべり *shikii-suberi*) may be inlaid in the *shikii* grooves.

**R03-033** — **Must.** Cut the grooves of *shikii* and *kamoi* in the *kiomote* face: *shikii* kiomote up, *kamoi* kiomote down.
*Why:* cupping on drying then does not pinch the sliding fittings, and the running surfaces are the better-wearing face.

**R03-034** — **Should.** Make *shikii* and *kamoi* from quarter-sawn or rift-sawn (*masame*/*oimasa*), well-seasoned stock (≤ 15 % MC).

**R03-035** — **Should.** Fix boards (floors, ceilings, wall linings, shelves) kiomote to the visible or used side, unless a documented regional variant applies (e.g. exposed verandah boards).

**R03-036** — **Should.** Where flat-sawn boards are edge-jointed into a wider panel (e.g. a *toko-ita*, a door panel), keep all boards kiomote on the same side. The alternating arrangement used in some Western furniture practice is **not** the Japanese norm for show faces, because it mixes *kiomote* and *kiura* surfaces on the show side.

**R03-037** — **Should.** On posts cut off-centre (*shinsari*), the face nearer the bark is the *kiomote* face. Where one face must be shown and the choice is otherwise equal, show the *kiomote* face (better surface, fewer splinters).

## 9. Orientation rules II: butt and top (*moto / sue*), and the inverted-post taboo

### 9.1 Posts: *moto* down

**R03-038** — **Must.** Stand every post with its butt (*moto*, root end) down and its top (*sue*) up, as the tree grew.
*Why (traditional):* a post stood upside down (逆柱 *sakabashira*, "inverted post") is held to bring misfortune: family discord, house noises ("the post groans at night"), fire, decline of the house. This is one of the most widely known building taboos in Japan. *Why (technical):* the butt end is usually the larger, denser and more durable end, with more heartwood and less juvenile wood. It belongs at the bottom, where bearing, rot and splashing act. The taper also reads correctly to the eye, and grain slope and knots run the way the eye expects.

**Identifying *moto* and *sue*:**
- The *moto* end has the larger diameter (on round stock) and usually wider heartwood.
- Knots slope **upward** from the pith toward the surface, since branches grow up and out. On a sawn face, the pointed tip of an elongated knot (the "tail" of the branch trace) points toward the *sue*.
- The growth-ring pattern (*itame* "cathedral" figure) on a flat-sawn face forms arches or V-shapes whose apex (point) generally points toward the *sue*. It is a guide, not infallible, because local grain deviations exist.
- Bark scale pattern (on round stock) and lean of branch stubs.
- A *moto/sue* mark made at *kidori* (R03-003).

**The deliberate exception: Nikkō Tōshōgū Yōmeimon.** Of the twelve columns of the Yōmeimon (陽明門) gate at Nikkō Tōshōgū, one (on the rear, north side, usually described as the second from the west) has its carved *guri* (屈輪) pattern oriented upside down relative to the others. It is known as the "evil-averting inverted post" (魔除けの逆柱 *mayoke no sakabashira*). The explanation usually given is that a building is complete only at the moment its decline begins, so leaving it deliberately "incomplete" wards off misfortune. Note that the inversion is of the **carved pattern**. Whether the timber itself was stood *sue*-down is not what the tradition asserts. The case shows that the taboo was well known, since the inversion only means something because *sakabashira* is otherwise forbidden. There are similar anecdotes of deliberately "unfinished" elements at other sites.

**R03-039** — **Must.** Never invert a post by accident, including when reusing timbers from a dismantled building (→ Ch. 16). When the *moto/sue* of a reused post cannot be determined from end grain, knots or old joint positions, record the uncertainty and decide on technical grounds (larger section and denser end down).

### 9.2 Horizontal members: which way the *moto* faces

For beams, *keta*, sills and purlins there is no single national rule for the compass direction of the *moto*. Several recognised customs exist:

1. **"As the tree grew" along the building's sequence.** All members in one line run in the same direction, *moto* to *sue*, like one continuous tree lying down. At a splice the *sue* end of one member meets the *moto* end of the next (元末 *moto-sue*). Joining *moto* to *moto* or *sue* to *sue* is avoided. This is the most widely taught principle.
2. **Moto toward the "upper" side** (上手 *kamite*): toward the more important end of the building, the principal room or *toko*, the front, or (in some regional customs) east or south, toward the sunrise or the sun. Customs differ by region and by school. Some carpenters speak of the ridge beam's *moto* toward the east. These are local customs, and this book does not assert any one as national.
3. **Technical placement.** The larger *moto* end is placed over the support with the greater load or the longer cantilever, or at the end where the most section is cut away by joints.

### 9.3 *Moto* and *sue* at splices

Custom 1 combines with the logic of the female joint. The **female / lower piece** (女木 *megi*, also 下木 *shitaki*: the piece placed first, which receives) of a splice such as *okkake-daisen-tsugi* or *koshikake-kama-tsugi* is commonly cut on the **moto end** of its member. The **male / upper piece** (男木 *ogi*, 上木 *uwaki*) is cut on the **sue end** of the next member. This gives:

```
   Direction of the run  ─────────────────────────────►

   [moto ..... member A ..... sue]=ogi ▷◁ megi=[moto ..... member B ..... sue]=ogi ▷◁ ...
                                    splice                      (continues)

   Every member runs moto→sue in the same direction; each splice joins
   the sue (male, laid on top) of one member to the moto (female, laid first) of the next.
```

*Why:* the female part loses the most section (housings and seats cut into it), so it belongs on the thicker *moto* end. The male part, which is dropped onto it, comes from the smaller *sue*. Erection proceeds in one direction along the line, each new member lowered onto the one already placed. This is **one common teaching**. Some joints (e.g. *okkake-daisen*, whose two halves are congruent) and some schools state it differently, and the placement of splices relative to supports (→ Ch. 07) takes precedence over *moto/sue* preference.

**R03-040** — **Should.** In a line of spliced horizontal members, run all members in the same *moto→sue* direction, and cut the female (lower) half of each splice on the *moto* end and the male (upper) half on the *sue* end. Do not splice *moto* to *moto* or *sue* to *sue* without a structural reason.
*Why:* consistent grain flow, section matched to the cut-away, and a single erection direction.

**R03-041** — **Should.** Record on the *itazu* the chosen *moto* direction for each line of *keta*, beams and sills (e.g. "*moto* toward east" or "*moto* toward the *toko* side"), following the house or regional custom, so every member of the line is cut and erected consistently.

**R03-042** — **Should.** Rafters (*taruki*) are laid with the *moto* at the eaves (down-slope) and the *sue* toward the ridge. *Hanegi* (cantilever levers) and other sloping members follow the same "*moto* down" logic.
*Why:* the heavier, stronger end sits at the eaves, where cantilever bending and weathering are greatest, and the member lies "as it grew".

**R03-043** — **Should.** Vertical members other than posts (*tsuka* struts, *kamoi*-hangers, *tsukabashira*) also stand *moto* down.

## 10. Orientation rules III: crown and belly (*se / hara*), curved beams and twist

### 10.1 Crown up

Most sawn beams and *keta* have some natural bow along their length. The convex side is the **back** (背 *se*), and its highest point is the **crown** or "pass" (峠 *tōge*). The concave side is the **belly** (腹 *hara*).

**R03-044** — **Must.** Set every horizontal spanning member (beam, *keta*, *sashimono*, *kamoi*, *nageshi*, purlin) with its crown (*se*) **upward**.
*Why:* gravity loads and creep then straighten the member toward level, instead of adding sag to an existing bow. A beam set belly-up sags visibly and pinches the sliding doors beneath (*kamoi*), and it loosens joints at its ends.

**R03-045** — **Should.** Locate the *tōge* near mid-span. When cutting a bowed beam to length, trim so that the crown lies between the supports, not over one support. Mark the *tōge* on the stock at *kidori*.

**R03-046** — **Should.** Naturally curved logs for minka beams (*ushibari*, *maruta-bari*, *hanegi*) are chosen for their curve and placed **crown up**, arching over the span or rising toward the ridge, with the *moto* at the lower or bearing end as the curve allows. The carpenter lays out the joints on the curved log with reference lines (芯墨 *shinzumi*, center lines, and *mizu-zumi* 水墨, level lines) snapped on flattened faces (→ Ch. 02, Ch. 10).
*Why:* the arch shape carries load efficiently, and the curve raises the beam's middle to give headroom and room for the roof structure.

### 10.2 Lateral bow and twist

- **Lateral bow** (曲がり *magari*, sideways) in *keta* and beams is placed so that it bows **outward** (toward the eaves), or in the direction where the roof load or the adjacent structure will restrain it. Treat this as common practice with variations.
- **Twist** (ねじれ *nejire*) and **spiral grain** (旋回木理 *senkai mokuri*): a member that will twist as it dries is either rejected for exposed and critical positions, or placed where the frame restrains it. In the Hōryūji tradition (§11), members with opposite twists are paired so that they counteract each other.

**R03-047** — **Should.** Pair members of opposite twist tendency across a joint or within one frame line (e.g. a right-spiral post with a left-spiral post at the two ends of a *nuki* run), so that their movements restrain each other.
*Why:* this is "building with the wood's character" (木の癖組み *ki no kusegumi*, → §11).

**R03-048** — **Should.** Do not use strongly spiral-grained members as exposed *keshō* posts that carry *kamoi* or *nageshi*, or as *tategu* stiles. Moderate spiral grain may be accepted in hidden posts restrained by *nuki* and walls.
*Why:* twisting pulls horizontal fittings out of plane and opens joints.

## 11. The Hōryūji oral tradition (Nishioka Tsunekazu)

Nishioka Tsunekazu (西岡常一, 1908–1995) was the last master carpenter (*tōryō*) of the hereditary Hōryūji temple-carpenter line. He led the Shōwa dismantling repairs of Hōryūji, the reconstruction of the Hōrinji three-storey pagoda, and the reconstruction of the Yakushiji Kondō and West Pagoda. He transmitted the **oral teachings of the Hōryūji carpenters** (法隆寺宮大工口伝 *Hōryūji miyadaiku kuden*) in several books and interviews. The best known are 『木に学べ——法隆寺・薬師寺の美』 (*Ki ni manabe*, "Learn from the trees", Shōgakukan, 1988) and 『木のいのち木のこころ』 (*Ki no inochi, ki no kokoro*, "Life of the tree, heart of the tree", with his pupil Ogawa Mitsuo 小川三夫 and the writer Shiono Yonematsu 塩野米松, 1990s). The wording of the teachings varies slightly between editions and retellings. The forms below are the widely quoted ones. They are presented as **oral tradition as reported by Nishioka**, not as documents of fixed antiquity.

| Teaching (Japanese) | Rōmaji | Meaning and practical content |
|---|---|---|
| 堂塔建立の用材は木を買わず山を買え | *dōtō konryū no yōzai wa ki o kawazu yama o kae* | "For the timber of halls and pagodas, do not buy trees, buy the mountain." Buy standing timber from one mountain, so that the master can see each tree's growth conditions and all the timbers share a common origin and character (→ R03-013) |
| 木は生育の方位のままに使え | *ki wa seiiku no hōi no mama ni tsukae* | "Use the trees in the orientation in which they grew." Trees from the south slope go on the south side of the building, north-slope trees on the north. Within a tree, the side that faced the sun faces south. The rationale: a tree has grown adapted to its exposure (density, knots, reaction to wind and sun), and it will perform best under the same exposure |
| 堂塔の木組みは木の癖組み | *dōtō no kigumi wa ki no kusegumi* | "The framing of halls and pagodas is the combining of the timbers' characters." Framing is not only assembling pieces to dimension. It is combining timbers according to their individual tendencies (twist, bow, hardness), e.g. pairing right- and left-twisting timbers so they hold each other (→ R03-047). Some versions add 「寸法で組まず木の癖で組め」 ("do not frame by dimension, frame by the wood's character") |
| 木の癖組みは工人たちの心組み | *ki no kusegumi wa kōnin-tachi no kokorogumi* | "Combining the timbers' characters is combining the craftsmen's hearts." Each craftsman also has his character, and the team must be combined as the timbers are |
| 工人たちの心組みは匠長が工人への思いやり | *kōnin-tachi no kokorogumi wa shōchō ga kōnin e no omoiyari* | "Combining the craftsmen's hearts depends on the master's consideration for them" |
| 百工あれば百念あり、これを一つに統ぶる… | *hyakkō areba hyakunen ari…* | "A hundred workmen, a hundred minds." Uniting them is the master's capacity (wording varies) |

Related sayings attributed to Nishioka in the same books include the view that a hinoki of 1,000 years' growth should serve 1,000 years in the building, and a strong preference for **splitting and adze-dressing** (as in ancient times) over sawing and machine planing, on the grounds that split timber follows the fibre and resists weathering better. He revived the *yariganna* for the Yakushiji work (→ Ch. 04).

**R03-049** — **Should (for shrine and temple work; May for other work).** Where the source of timber is known, place members according to their growth aspect: south-grown timber on the south, and within a member, the sun-facing side of the tree toward the south or the exterior.
*Why:* this is the traditional expectation, and it is believed to match the timber's adaptation to exposure. Its measurable effect is debated, but it costs nothing if recorded at *kidori*.

**R03-050** — **Should.** Treat timbers as individuals: record each major member's twist, bow and hardness, and assign members so that their tendencies cancel or are restrained within the frame (*kusegumi*).
*Why:* a frame of "average" dimensioned sticks placed without regard to character will distort as the individual members move.

**R03-051** — **May.** For restoration of ancient buildings, dress new members by splitting and *chōna*/*yariganna* finish where the original evidence shows such working (→ Ch. 04, Ch. 16).

## 12. Grading and appearance

### 12.1 Knot grades (節 *fushi*)

Market grading of *keshō* timber in Japan is by the knots visible on the faces. The terms are standard. Exact limits differ between JAS classes, regions and suppliers, so the figures are indicative only.

| Grade | Japanese | Typical meaning (indicative) |
|---|---|---|
| Clear | 無節 *mubushi* | No knots visible on the graded face(s) |
| Superior small-knot | 上小節 *jōkobushi* | Only small sound knots, typically ≤ ≈ 10 mm diameter, few and widely spaced (e.g. ≥ ≈ 30 cm apart, as one common reading) |
| Small-knot | 小節 *kobushi* | Small knots, typically ≤ ≈ 20 mm, more frequent |
| Common / "first grade" | 一等 *ittō* (also 特一等 *tokuittō*, special first) | Structural-appearance commercial grade. Knots of moderate size permitted, including some dead knots. Usual for hidden members and ordinary structure |

The grade is stated per face: e.g. *ippō mubushi* (一方無節, one face clear), *nihō mubushi* (二方無節), *shihō mubushi* (四方無節, all four faces clear). A post for a *shinkabe* room with two faces shown (e.g. *nihō jōkobushi*) is priced accordingly.

**Knot types:** live (sound, intergrown) knot 生き節 *ikibushi*; dead knot 死に節 *shinibushi*; loose or falling knot 抜け節 *nukebushi*; decayed knot 腐れ節 *kusarebushi*.

### 12.2 Grain grades

- **四方柾 *shihō-masa*:** all four faces edge-grained. The highest grade for square posts (→ §5.6).
- **二方柾 *nihō-masa*:** two opposite faces edge-grained.
- **柾目 *masame*, 追柾 *oimasa*, 板目 *itame*, 中杢 *nakamoku*, 杢 *moku*** (figure: *tamamoku* 玉杢, *uzuramoku* 鶉杢, *sasamoku* 笹杢, *jorinmoku* 如鱗杢 etc.).
- **Ring density** (年輪の詰み) is commonly stated as "rings per *sun*". Old-growth Kiso hinoki and Akita sugi with very fine rings command premiums.

### 12.3 *Yakumono*, *keshōzai*, *nomono*

| Term | Japanese | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Premium finish stock | 役物 *yakumono* | Clear or small-knot, selected grain, graded for appearance (posts, *kamoi*, *nageshi*, *tokobashira*, ceiling boards). Priced per piece |
| Exposed member | 化粧材 *keshōzai* | Any member that will be visible in the finished building. Planed, protected, graded for face |
| Hidden member | 野物 *nomono* | Hidden structure: roof members above the ceiling (*noyane*, 野屋根), joists under floors, members buried in walls. Graded for strength, not appearance. May be rough-sawn or adze-dressed |
| Common stock | 並材 *namizai* | Ordinary unselected grade |

**R03-052** — **Must.** Grade and allocate every member as *keshō* or *nomono* before *kidori*. *Keshō* members must meet the appearance grade specified for each *visible face*. *Nomono* must meet the structural requirements only.
*Why:* using a *yakumono* where it is hidden wastes the most valuable stock. Using a *nomono* where it is exposed spoils the room.

**R03-053** — **Should.** In a room, keep the grade consistent across the visible members of the same kind: all posts *jōkobushi*, or all *mubushi*. Place the highest-grade face toward the *toko* and the principal viewpoint.

**R03-054** — **Should.** For structural grading of hidden members, follow the current JAS visual or machine stress grading (→ Ch. 17). The old market term *ittō* is not a structural grade under modern regulations.

## 13. Defects and acceptance

### 13.1 Defect catalogue

| Defect | Japanese | Description | Acceptance |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ring shake | 目廻り *memawari* | Separation along a growth ring, often from wind stress or felling impact. Common in some sugi | **Reject** for beams, splice members and any member with joints near the shake. It may pass in short hidden blocking |
| Included bark | 入り皮 *irikawa* | Bark enclosed within the wood (from a healed wound or fork) | Reject in joints and *keshō* faces. Acceptable in hidden members away from joints if small |
| Decay | 腐れ *kusare* | Fungal decay; soft, discoloured | **Reject** always (except in dismantled members repaired per → Ch. 16) |
| Insect damage | 虫食い *mushikui* | Beetle or termite galleries; powder-post in sapwood | Reject if active or structural. Old inactive surface damage in hidden *nomono* may be accepted after inspection |
| Spiral grain | 旋回木理 *senkai mokuri* (ねじれ木) | Fibres spiral around the stem | Reject for *keshō* posts carrying fittings, for *tategu*, and for long beams. Moderate spiral acceptable in hidden, restrained posts (→ R03-048) |
| Slope of grain | 目切れ *megire* / 繊維傾斜 | Fibres run out of the face | Limit in bending members. Structural grades limit slope, typically to around 1 in 12 or flatter for higher grades (check the current standard). Reject run-out at tenons and splice tongues |
| Reaction wood | あて *ate* (also written 陽疾 or 当て) | Compression wood in conifers, on the underside of leaning stems and branches. Dense, dark, brittle; very large longitudinal shrinkage | **Reject** for long members and *keshō*. It bows and checks unpredictably and planes badly |
| Checks and splits | 割れ *ware*: 干割れ *hiware* (drying checks), 胴割れ *dōware* (lengthwise splits on the side), 木口割れ *koguchi-ware* (end checks) | Separations along the grain | Surface drying checks on *shinmochi* posts are normal and acceptable if not through. Reject through-splits and end checks that enter tenon or splice zones |
| Heart shake / pith check | 芯割れ *shin-ware* | Radial cracks from the pith | Reject if they reach a joint |
| Resin pocket | 脂壺 *yanitsubo* | Pocket of resin | Reject on *keshō* faces. Acceptable hidden |
| Blue stain | 青変 *aohen* | Fungal staining of sapwood (pine) | Cosmetic. Reject for *keshō* |
| Wane | 丸身 *marumi* | Bark edge or missing corner | Acceptable within limits on *nomono*. Must be planed out on *keshō* |
| Warp: bow, spring, cup, twist | 反り *sori*, 曲がり *magari*, 幅反り *haba-zori*, ねじれ *nejire* | Distortion | Within dressing allowance, or placed per §10 |
| Black heart | 黒心 *kuroshin* | Dark, wet heartwood of sugi | Not a defect in strength, but dries slowly and irregularly. Colour grade issue for *keshō* |

### 13.2 Knots and joints

Knots are the most frequent reason to reject or re-orient a member. A knot concentrates grain deviation around itself, has different shrinkage, and is weak in tension and in shear along the grain.

**R03-055** — **Must.** No knot (live or dead) within a tenon (*hozo*), a splice tongue or hook (*kama*, *ari*, *okkake* faces), the walls of a mortise, or the bearing seat (*koshikake*) of a splice.
*Why:* these zones carry concentrated shear and bearing. A knot there is a pre-formed failure.

**R03-056** — **Must.** No knot, check or grain run-out within the end distance of a pin hole (*komisen*, *shachisen*), i.e. between the hole and the end of the tenon or member.
*Why:* the pin splits out the end grain in relish failure (→ Ch. 09).

**R03-057** — **Should.** Keep large knots (≥ about 1/4 of the face width) at least about one member depth away from any joint, and out of the tension zone (lower third) of beams near mid-span. Shift the member lengthwise, turn it over, or swap it with another at *kidori* to achieve this.

**R03-058** — **Should.** On *keshō* posts, turn the best face to the principal view (→ R03-053), then check that the joints fall in clear wood. If the choice conflicts, joint soundness wins and the post is reallocated.

**R03-059** — **Must.** Reject *memawari* (ring shake) in any beam, *keta*, *sashimono* or member receiving splices.
*Why:* ring shake splits the member into two concentric beams with no shear connection, and the joints pull out along the shake.

**R03-060** — **Must.** Reject members with active decay or insect infestation, and members with *ate* (reaction wood) for long spans and *keshō* positions.

**R03-061** — **Should.** Inspect end grain after every cross-cut in *kizami*. Defects revealed (internal checks, shake, black heart, decay) may require re-allocating the member before further work is invested.

## 14. Summary checklist

Before *kidori* and *sumitsuke*, for each member:

1. Species appropriate to function and exposure (R03-001, R03-004, R03-006 – R03-012).
2. MC measured and within target (R03-027 – R03-030).
3. *Akami/shirata* distribution acceptable for the position (R03-019, R03-020).
4. *Moto/sue* marked, and the post will stand *moto* down (R03-003, R03-038, R03-039).
5. Line direction and splice orientation decided (R03-040, R03-041).
6. *Kiomote/kiura* marked, and boards, *shikii* and *kamoi* oriented (R03-033 – R03-037).
7. Crown (*tōge*) marked and placed up, near mid-span (R03-044 – R03-046).
8. Twist and spiral noted; paired or rejected (R03-047, R03-048).
9. *Sewari* on the hidden face, to the pith, clear of principal mortise walls (R03-023 – R03-026).
10. Show face graded; joints fall in clear wood (R03-052 – R03-058).
11. Growth aspect recorded where known (R03-049, R03-050).
12. Defects checked and end grain inspected (R03-059 – R03-061).
