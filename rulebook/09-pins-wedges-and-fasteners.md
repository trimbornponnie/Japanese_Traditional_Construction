# Chapter 09 — Pins, Wedges, Keys and Fasteners (栓・楔・車知・金物 / sen, kusabi, shachi, kanamono)

**Scope.** This chapter covers the **secondary elements that lock joints**: wooden pins, wedges, keys, dowels and butterfly keys, and the procedure of wedging and re-tightening a frame. It then covers **metal**: hand-forged nails, staples, straps, decorative hardware and modern connectors, together with adhesives and **rope lashings**. The joints these elements serve are catalogued in → Ch. 07 (splices) and → Ch. 08 (connections), and the principles of joinery in → Ch. 06. The assembly sequence is in → Ch. 15, and repair and conservation in → Ch. 16. The modern regulatory treatment of connectors (required hardware, capacities) is in → Ch. 17.

Dimensions are **representative**. Where a figure is uncertain it is marked "c." or "approx." or given as a range.

## 0. Principles of locking elements

The Japanese frame is assembled from **joints that locate** (tenons, housings, cogs) and **elements that lock** them (pins, wedges, keys). The locking elements are small, cheap, replaceable, and **harder than the members they lock**. Most of them can be **driven out**, which makes the frame demountable and repairable (→ Ch. 16).

**Species for locking elements.** The usual choice is **kashi** (樫, evergreen oaks: 白樫 *shirakashi*, *Quercus myrsinifolia*, and 赤樫 *akagashi*, *Q. acuta*). **Keyaki** (欅, *Zelkova serrata*) is used for large pins and keys where kashi is not available in size. Seasoned hinoki or hiba is used for light wedges in soft frames and where a softer wedge that crushes before the member is wanted. Chestnut (栗, *kuri*) is used in regional minka work. Hard, straight, **riven** (割り, not sawn) stock with the grain along the length is always preferred.

**R09-001** — (Must) Pins, keys, wedges and dowels are made from **straight-grained, riven, well-seasoned hardwood**, with the **grain running along the element's length** (the direction in which it is driven).
*Why:* riven stock follows the grain, so there is no run-out to cause breakage while driving. Sawn stock with short grain snaps under the mallet or shears in service.

**R09-002** — (Should) Locking elements are **drier than the members** they lock, or at least equally dry.
*Why:* a wet pin or wedge shrinks after driving and the joint loosens. A dry one in a slightly moister member is gripped harder as the member shrinks around it.

**R09-003** — (Should) Locking elements are **left accessible and, where appropriate, slightly proud** so they can be re-driven (増し締め, *mashi-jime*, §7) and later driven out for repair. Do not glue structural pins or wedges.

## 1. Draw-pin — 込み栓 (komisen)

### Draw-pin — 込み栓 (komisen)
- **Class:** pin (栓, *sen*) through a tenon
- **Typical use:** long tenons of posts into sills and beams (長ほぞ差し込み栓打ち, → Ch. 08 §2.2), sashimono and tie-beam tenons into posts (込栓打ち, → Ch. 08 §4.4), roof struts at gable ends, and some splices (e.g. as the pins of 腰掛け蟻 or 竿 splices in some traditions, → Ch. 07).
- **Load behaviour:** the pin works in **double shear and bending** across the two cheeks of the mortised member, and **bears on the tenon** toward its end. It resists **withdrawal**, and with draw it also pre-compresses the shoulder. It does not stiffen the joint in rotation significantly.
- **Forms:**
  - **Square pin (角込み栓, *kaku-komisen*)**: traditional, driven into a square hole chopped through both pieces. Its flat faces bear over a larger area than a round pin of the same size.
  - **Round pin (丸込み栓, *maru-komisen*)**: bored hole (auger 錐, or machine drill in modern precut), faster to make. Round hardwood pins are sold in standard diameters.
- **Proportions:**

| Member size | Square pin | Round pin | Pin centre from shoulder (into receiving member) | Min. relish beyond pin in tenon |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 105 mm (3.5 sun) | 15 mm sq. (5 bu) | 15 mm dia. | 40–50 mm | ≥ 30–40 mm |
| 120 mm (4 sun) | 15–18 mm sq. (5–6 bu) | 15–18 mm dia. | 45–55 mm | ≥ 36–45 mm |
| 150 mm (5 sun) | 18–21 mm sq. (6–7 bu) | 18–21 mm dia. | 55–70 mm | ≥ 45–50 mm |

  Pin length is the member width plus c. 5–15 mm, so that it can be trimmed or left proud for re-driving.
- **Draw (引き, *hiki*):** the hole in the tenon is offset **toward the shoulder** by **c. 1–2 mm** relative to the hole in the receiving member. Some carpenters use c. 2–3 mm in softwood frames. When the pin is driven, it enters the receiving member's hole, meets the offset tenon hole, and **pulls the tenon in** until the shoulder closes.
- **Taper:** the square pin is made with a slight **overall taper** (roughly 0.5–1 mm over its length) and a short **pointed or chamfered tip** (先を細く). The tip then finds the offset hole, and the pin tightens progressively as it is driven.
- **Orientation:** the pin is driven **across the receiving member's width**, perpendicular to the tenon's width, so that it passes through **both cheeks and the tenon**. It is driven from the face that remains accessible (usually the inside of a building or the face that will not be plastered). For a horizontal member entering a post, the pin runs horizontally through the post's cheeks, **never vertically along the post's grain** (→ R08-063). A square pin is normally driven with its faces parallel to the member faces. Some carpenters turn it 45° ("diamond") so a corner leads into the offset hole. This is a variant.
- **Marking:** mark the pin hole centre on the receiving member from the shoulder line. Dry-fit, mark through onto the tenon (or measure), withdraw, and **mark the tenon hole's centre 1–2 mm nearer the shoulder**.
- **Cutting sequence:** (1) Bore or chop the hole through the receiving member, from both faces to meet in the middle. (2) Bore or chop the tenon hole with the draw. (3) Ease the shoulder-side edge of the tenon hole slightly. (4) Make the pins.
- **Assembly:** assemble, check plumb or level, then drive the pin with a hammer (玄能, *gennō*) from one face. A properly drawn joint **audibly tightens** in the last few blows. Trim flush or leave slightly proud.
- **Rules:**
  **R09-004** — (Must) The draw of a komisen is **toward the shoulder** and **c. 1–2 mm** (max. c. 3 mm in soft species). No draw leaves a loose joint. Too much draw splits the tenon end or breaks the pin.
  **R09-005** — (Must) The **wood beyond the pin in the tenon (relish) is at least 2 × the pin width**, and the pin hole is at least **1.5 × the pin width from the receiving member's face**.
  *Why:* the draw puts the tenon end in tension toward the shoulder, and the wood between the hole and the face of the receiving member takes the pin's reaction. Either can shear out.
  **R09-006** — (Should) The pin is **harder than the tenon** (kashi pins in hinoki, sugi or matsu members). Use pins of the same species only for members of kashi or keyaki themselves.
  **R09-007** — (Should) Where two pins are needed (deep tenons), they are **staggered and spaced at least c. 3 × the pin width** apart, never in one line along the grain of the tenon.
  *Why:* two pins in line along the grain split the tenon between them.
  **R09-008** — (Should) Holes are made **square to the member face**, from both faces toward the middle for long holes, so the pin does not wander out through a cheek.
- **Common errors:** drawing the wrong way. Holes bored oversize. Softwood or sawn-grain pins that snap. Pins driven before the frame is plumbed, which locks errors in. The tenon end split because the relish was too short. Pins glued in.
- **Variants:** komisen through splices (→ Ch. 07). Komisen combined with a hanasen on the same through-tenon. Modern **steel or resin pins** tested as substitutes. These are not traditional and should be treated as a separate, engineered joint.

## 2. Flat tapered key — 車知栓 (shachisen)

### Flat tapered key — 車知栓 (shachisen), also 車知 (shachi)
- **Class:** key (flat, tapered, driven across a joint)
- **Typical use:** **sao-shachi** connections (竿車知, → Ch. 08 §4.3), where a long tongue passes through a post or into another member. The **sao-shachi splice** (竿車知継ぎ, → Ch. 07) of sills and plates. **Shachi mitres** (車知留め / 三方留め, → Ch. 08 §5.1) in fine work. Some types of bracket-arm and tie connections.
- **Load behaviour:** the key sits in a slot cut **partly in each of the two pieces** being joined. Because the two halves of the slot are offset by the draw, driving the tapered key forces the pieces together along the joint's axis. The key works in **shear and bearing** across its width. Relative to a round pin, its broad flat faces spread the bearing over a longer length of grain.
- **Geometry and proportions (representative):**
  - **section:** thickness c. 1/3–1/4 of the key's width. In building work c. **12–18 mm thick × 30–45 mm wide**. In furniture and fittings much smaller (one published furniture example is c. 6 × 14 mm).
  - **taper:** c. **1:10 to 1:30** along the length, i.e. the width increases by a few mm over the key's length. Steeper tapers are used for short keys in big members, shallower tapers for long keys in fine work. Sources vary.
  - **length:** passes through the member (depth or width) with c. 10–20 mm projecting at the head for driving and later removal.
  - **slot:** in many forms the slot is cut **obliquely** (skewed) relative to the joint axis, so the key is driven **diagonally to the grain** of the member it passes through. This makes the pull stronger and prevents the key from sliding out along the grain.
- **Orientation rules:**
  **R09-009** — (Must) Shachisen are **used in pairs driven from opposite faces** (one from above and one from below, or from the two sides), so that the pull is balanced and the tongue is not cocked.
  **R09-010** — (Must) The two halves of each slot are offset by the draw (c. 1–2 mm), **in the direction that closes the joint**. Verify the direction on the dry fit before cutting the final slot.
  **R09-011** — (Should) The key's **grain runs along its length** (R09-001), and its broad faces bear on **long grain** of the members, i.e. the key width is set along the member's length.
  **R09-012** — (Should) Slots are located at least c. **1.5 × the key width** from any end grain (tongue tip, member end) and from the post face.
- **Marking and cutting:** dry-fit the joint. Mark the slot through the female member onto the tongue. Withdraw, and cut the tongue's half-slot with the draw. Chop the slots with a narrow chisel and pare to fit the key's taper exactly.
- **Assembly:** insert the tongue. Start both keys by hand. Drive them alternately with light blows until the shoulders close, then give a final firm blow to each. Do not trim the heads flush where re-driving is foreseen.
- **Common errors:** keys driven from one side only. The slot wider than the key, so it pinches only at one corner. Keys of softwood. Keys driven so hard that the tongue splits between the slot and the tip.
- **Variants:** a single shachi in small work. Shachi combined with a dowel. Shachi mitres in furniture.

## 3. Nose pin — 鼻栓 (hanasen)

### Nose pin — 鼻栓 (hanasen)
- **Class:** pin through the **projecting end of a through-tenon**, outside the receiving member
- **Typical use:** through-tenons of sashigamoi, tie beams and floor ties (足固め) projecting beyond a post (→ Ch. 08 §2.9). Sill-to-sill joints. Projecting bracket arms and cantilevered beams (出し梁). Storehouse (蔵) frames. Gates and fences.
- **Load behaviour:** the pin bears against the **outer face** of the receiving member and locks the tenon against withdrawal. Relative to a komisen, the pin does not weaken the receiving member (no hole through it), and it can be knocked out to take the joint apart.
- **Proportions:**
  - pin 15–21 mm square or round (as komisen), or a **tapered flat pin** in heavier work
  - tenon projection beyond the receiving member ≥ c. 2.5–3 × the pin width, plus the pin: c. **60–90 mm** in 4-sun work
  - pin centre c. 1–1.5 × the pin width out from the member face, with a **draw of c. 1 mm toward the member**
- **Orientation:** the pin passes through the tenon's **thickness** (the short dimension) where possible, perpendicular to the tenon's width, so it bears on the long dimension of the tenon section. For a horizontal tenon with its width vertical, the pin is therefore horizontal. Where the tenon is flat (width horizontal), the pin is vertical, driven from above. Practice varies with the tenon's orientation: the rule is **pin across the thin dimension**.
- **Rules:**
  **R09-013** — (Must) The hanasen hole is offset **toward the receiving member** (draw), so that driving the pin pulls the shoulder tight.
  **R09-014** — (Should) The projecting tenon end ("nose", 鼻 *hana*) is at least **2 × the pin width beyond the pin hole**, and is chamfered. Where it is exposed to weather, it is cut with a slight downward slope or capped.
  *Why:* the nose's end grain takes the pin's reaction in shear, and exposed end grain is the first place rot begins.
- **Common errors:** projection too short, so the nose shears off. A pin hole that is not drawn. The pin driven through the receiving member instead of just outside it.
- **Variants:** hanasen combined with an internal komisen. Paired hanasen from both faces on wide tenons. Tapered flat hanasen that act like a shachi.

## 4. Wedges — 楔 (kusabi)

### 4.1 General rules for wedges
- **Species:** kashi for structural wedges. Keyaki or kuri as alternatives. Seasoned hinoki, hiba or sugi for non-structural packing wedges, or where a wedge that crushes before the member is wanted (e.g. in soft nuki frames, some carpenters prefer wedges of the same species or slightly harder than the nuki).
- **Grain:** along the wedge's length, i.e. in the direction of driving (R09-001). The broad faces of the wedge are cut **along the grain** (flat-sawn or quartersawn faces). Wedges with grain across or diagonal to their length snap when struck.
- **Taper (representative):** c. **1:8 to 1:15** for wedges driven beside a nuki or tenon, and c. 1:10 for kerf wedges (割楔). **Shallower tapers** (e.g. 1:15–1:20) hold better against backing out but must be driven further. **Steeper tapers** (1:5–1:8) are used for short wedges in kerfs and in jigoku-hozo. Values vary between schools. The principle is that a shallower taper gives more holding friction and more force per blow.
- **Moisture:** dry (R09-002). Some carpenters soak the *tip* of a kerf wedge briefly so it slides in, and then it tightens as it dries. Practice varies, and a fully wet wedge shrinks loose.

**R09-015** — (Must) A wedge is oriented so that its **spreading force acts on end grain of the mortised member** (the top and bottom of a post mortise, the ends of a sill mortise), never on long-grain cheeks (→ R08-022, R08-064).
*Why:* long-grain cheeks split like firewood under a wedge. End-grain walls crush slightly and hold.

**R09-016** — (Should) Wedges are **driven in opposing pairs** wherever the gap can be reached from both sides. A pair of wedges driven toward each other gives a **parallel-faced** packing that fills the gap evenly, whereas a single wedge tilts the member.

### 4.2 Kerf wedges in through-tenons — 割楔 (wari-kusabi)
- **Use:** through-tenons and stepped through-tenons (通し枘 / 小根枘割楔締め, → Ch. 08 §2.6, §2.8).
- **Geometry:** one or two saw kerfs in the tenon, c. 2/3–3/4 of the tenon length, positioned c. 1/4–1/6 of the tenon width from its edges (two kerfs) or on the centreline (one kerf). The mortise exit is **flared** by c. 2–5 mm per end.
- **Wedge size:** width = tenon thickness. Length c. 2/3 of the tenon length plus the head. Head thickness = kerf width + the flare to be taken up (typically 3–6 mm in building work).
- **Rules:**
  **R09-017** — (Must) The kerfs are placed so the tenon **spreads along the length of the mortise** (for a horizontal member in a post, the kerfs are horizontal and the tenon spreads up and down).
  **R09-018** — (Should) A small **stop hole** is bored at the bottom of each kerf in brittle or short-grained stock, and kerfs stop at least c. 1/4 of the tenon length short of the shoulder.
  **R09-019** — (Should) Kerf wedges are **driven alternately** (when two) so that the tenon spreads evenly, and they are driven only after the member's shoulder is fully home.

### 4.3 Hidden wedges — 地獄枘 (jigoku-hozo)
See → Ch. 08 §2.10. The wedges are short (c. 1/2 of the tenon length) and are **started in the kerfs before assembly**. The mortise bottom drives them home as the tenon enters. The flare of the mortise, the wedge thickness and the kerf depth must be calculated and tested on an offcut.

**R09-020** — (Must) **Test jigoku-hozo wedges on an offcut** of the same species and moisture content before final assembly. The joint cannot be adjusted or removed afterwards.

### 4.4 Nuki wedges — 貫楔 (nuki-kusabi)
- **Use:** through-ties (通し貫, → Ch. 08 §4.1)
- **Geometry:** the nuki hole is taller than the nuki by the wedge allowance (c. 10–20 mm in wall nuki). The wedges are **as wide as the nuki is thick**, c. 1/2 of the post width long each (so a pair meets near the post centre), with a taper of c. 1:8–1:15.
- **Placement:** into the gap on **one side** of the nuki (above or below, depending on the carpenter's rule and the level; → R08-057), **one wedge from each face** of the post.
- **Rules:**
  **R09-021** — (Must) Structural nuki are wedged with **a pair of wedges driven from opposite faces** of each post (→ R08-056).
  **R09-022** — (Should) Nuki wedges are driven **only after the frame is plumbed**, and tightened **progressively and symmetrically** along the wall and across the building (→ §7).
  **R09-023** — (Should) Nuki wedges are **not glued and not cut flush with the post** until after the first re-tightening (§7). Leave c. 5–10 mm of head proud where it will later be concealed by the wall finish.
- **Common errors:** wedges driven from one side only. Wedges too steep (they pop out). Wedges of soft or cross-grained wood that crush to nothing. Wedges driven hard at one post before the neighbouring posts are set, which pushes the frame out of plumb.

### 4.5 Other wedges
- **Wedges beside a tenon** (横楔, *yoko-kusabi*): driven between a tenon and the end of an over-long mortise to tighten a sashimono or a floor tie. They are used where kerfs are impractical. The same end-grain rule applies (R09-015).
- **Levelling and packing wedges** (飼い物, *kaimono*; 飼い楔): used during tatemae to set sills and plates to level on foundations. These are temporary and are replaced by permanent packing or mortar (→ Ch. 15).
- **Wedges in splices**: e.g. the central wedge of the 金輪継ぎ (*kanawa-tsugi*) and the 尻挟み継ぎ (*shiribasami-tsugi*), which draw the two halves of a splice together (→ Ch. 07).

## 5. Large splice pins — 大栓 (daisen)

### Large pin — 大栓 (daisen)
- **Class:** pin (large) through a splice
- **Typical use:** the **追掛け大栓継ぎ (*okkake-daisen-tsugi*)** of plates, girts and beams (→ Ch. 07), and other long scarf splices in heavy members that must resist tension and bending.
- **Load behaviour:** the pins pass **horizontally from the side face** through the lapped portion of the splice (through the interlocking tongues and grooves, 目違い). They lock the two halves against sliding apart along the splice's sloped sliding plane (the draw of the okkake), and so prevent the splice from opening in tension or bending.
- **Proportions (representative):**
  - **size:** c. 15–24 mm square (5 bu–8 bu). For 120–150 mm members, 18–21 mm (6–7 bu) is typical.
  - **number:** commonly **two**, sometimes **four** in long laps or deep members. The number varies by lap length, which is commonly c. 2.5–3 × the member depth, and by school.
  - **position:** spaced along the lap, clear of the hooked ends and of the 目違い roots, at least c. 2 × the pin width from any end grain
- **Square vs round:** traditionally **square**, driven into chopped holes. Round pins in bored holes are common in modern work. Square pins bear better. Round pins are easier to make accurately.
- **Rules:**
  **R09-024** — (Should) Daisen are driven **after** the splice has been drawn fully home (the okkake sliding plane taken up by driving the pieces along it), and they **are not relied upon to draw** the splice. Their holes are bored straight through with no draw, or only minimal draw.
  *Why:* the okkake's own sloped sliding plane (c. 1:10) does the drawing. Adding a large draw to the pins overstresses the short grain around them.
  **R09-025** — (Should) Where the daisen are driven from alternate faces, the heads are then visible on both faces. Where the pins may be re-driven, leave the heads accessible (not buried in a wall).

## 6. Dowels, loose tongues and butterfly keys

### 6.1 Dowel — 太枘 (dabo) / だぼ
- **Class:** dowel (locating pin, not driven across a joint but **set half in each of two members** stacked on each other)
- **Typical use:**
  - **bracket sets:** bearing blocks (斗 / 枡, *masu*) on arms (肘木, *hijiki*) and on posts or the daiwa (→ Ch. 08 §5.10, → Ch. 12)
  - **stacked members:** plates on post tops (where no tenon is used), daiwa on posts, and log walls of the 校倉 (*azekura*) type
  - **post to stone:** on some ancient foundation stones (礎石, *soseki*) a small socket or projecting seat (造り出し, *tsukuridashi*) locates the post. Where a socket exists, a dabo or the post's own short tenon enters it. This is a feature of some ancient and temple foundations. Most ishibadate posts have no dowel and rely on the scribed fit (光付け) and weight (→ Ch. 10).
  - **splices and daimochi-tsugi** (→ Ch. 07): dabo lock the lapped faces
- **Proportions (representative):** square, c. **1/5–1/4 of the smaller member's width** (15–30 mm in house and small temple work, larger in great halls), c. **1–2 sun long** (30–60 mm), i.e. half in each member. Round dabo of similar size are used in modern work.
- **Species:** kashi or keyaki.
- **Rules:**
  **R09-026** — (Must) Dabo holes are **slightly deeper than half the dabo** in each member (a total clearance of c. 2–3 mm), so the members **bear on each other, not on the dabo**.
  **R09-027** — (Should) Every block in a bracket set is located by dabo (or tenon) (→ R08-081). Place dabo on the member's **centreline**, clear of any halving or slot, and at least 1.5 × the dabo size from end grain.
  **R09-028** — (Should) Square dabo are oriented with their faces parallel to the member faces. In a block (masu), the dabo hole is **not** placed in line with a slot (斗の口) where it would leave short grain.

### 6.2 Loose tenons, tongues and keys — 雇い (yatoi)
雇い (*yatoi*, "hired") is the general prefix for a **separate piece inserted to join two members** that could otherwise carry integral joinery. Forms:
- **雇い枘 (yatoi-hozo)**: loose tenon (→ Ch. 08 §2.13)
- **雇い竿 (yatoi-zao)**: loose through-tongue in yatoi-zao-shachi (→ Ch. 08 §4.3)
- **雇い実 (yatoi-zane)**: loose tongue (spline) between board edges (→ Ch. 08 §7, → Ch. 14)
- **雇い目違い**: a loose alignment key

**R09-029** — (Must) A yatoi is of **equal or harder species** than the members, with its grain **along the direction of the force** it carries. A loose tongue between board edges has its grain *across* the joint (i.e. short in the tongue's length and long across the joint), so that it does not snap along the seam. (For loose tenons and tongues in frames, the grain runs along the tenon.)

### 6.3 Butterfly key — 千切り / 契り (chigiri)
- **Class:** key (inlaid)
- **Typical use:** **arresting a crack (割れ止め)** at the end of a thick board or post. **Joining two boards edge to edge** (tokonoma boards, tabletops, wide panels). **Repair** of split members, including in conservation (→ Ch. 16). It is also decorative, often in a contrasting species.
- **Geometry:** a double-dovetail ("bow-tie" or hourglass) key, let into a matching recess that spans the crack or joint.
- **Proportions (representative):**
  - length (across the crack) c. **3–4 × the waist width**. The heads are c. 2–3 × the waist width. The waist is c. **1/3–1/2 of the head width**.
  - flare of the dovetail sides c. 1:5–1:8
  - **depth** c. **1/3 to 1/2 of the board thickness** (typical practical depths c. 10–15 mm in 30–60 mm boards). Where a chigiri is let into both faces, stagger the positions or keep each at ≤ 1/3.
  - common sizes in furniture and interior boards: c. 50–100 mm long
- **Grain:** the chigiri's **grain runs along its length**, i.e. **across the crack or joint**. This is the direction in which it carries tension.
- **Species:** harder than the board (kashi, keyaki, or dense tropical woods such as ebony and rosewood in decorative work), and dry.
- **Rules:**
  **R09-030** — (Must) A chigiri's grain runs **across the crack** (along the key's length), and the key is **drier than the board**.
  **R09-031** — (Should) The chigiri is placed with its **waist exactly on the crack line**. Several are spaced along a long crack, and one is set at the **crack tip**, or just beyond it, to stop propagation.
  **R09-032** — (Should) The chigiri is fitted tight at the heads (the pull faces) and **not forced at the waist**. It is glued (in fittings and furniture) or left dry and held by its fit (in structural members, where it must remain removable).
- **Common errors:** grain along the crack (the key splits in two). A key set too shallow in a thick board, which does not restrain the crack below. A key set in a still-drying board, which opens a new crack beside it.
- **Variants:** round-ended chigiri. Multi-waisted keys. **独鈷 (*tokko*)**-shaped keys, a regional name for similar double-headed keys (uncertain; → Ch. 08 §5.8).

## 7. Wedging the frame: tatemae and re-tightening

The locking elements are **not all driven at once**. The frame is assembled, **plumbed and aligned**, and only then locked. This section summarises the sequence. The full tatemae is in → Ch. 15.

### 7.1 Sequence at tatemae (representative, for a nuki-and-sashimono frame)
1. **Sills and floor ties** are set, levelled with packing wedges, and their joints closed. In ishibadate frames the posts are stood on the stones and the floor ties (足固め) are inserted.
2. **Posts** are stood in order, bay by bay, each dropped onto its sill tenon or stone.
3. **Nuki are threaded** through the posts as they are stood, lowest level first, and spliced inside the posts. The wedges are set **loosely (仮締め, *karijime*)**, just enough to hold the posts.
4. **Sashimono (sashigamoi, tie beams)** are inserted. Sao-shachi keys are started but not driven home. Wedged tenons are entered but not wedged.
5. **Plates, girts and beams** are set on the post tops and their tenons engaged. Splices are closed and daisen driven where the splice is fully drawn.
6. **Plumbing and alignment (建て入れ直し, *tateire-naoshi*)**: every post is brought to plumb with a plumb bob (下げ振り, *sagefuri*) against the marked centrelines. The frame is pulled with ropes and props, or with pull devices (in modern work, turnbuckle wires). Diagonals are checked.
7. **Final locking (本締め, *honjime*)**: komisen are driven at post feet and tops. Shachisen are driven home. Kerf wedges are driven. **Nuki wedges** are driven in pairs, working **symmetrically** from the centre of the building outward, or bay by bay around the frame, re-checking plumb as the work proceeds.
8. **The roof frame** is raised, and the ridge is set (上棟, *jōtō*; → Ch. 15). The frame is re-checked for plumb after the ridge is set and the rafters are laid, because the roof load shifts the frame.

**R09-033** — (Must) **No structural pin, key or wedge is driven home until the frame (or the part being locked) is plumb and aligned.**
*Why:* locking elements lock errors too. A frame pinned out of plumb cannot be corrected without drawing the pins.

**R09-034** — (Should) Wedging and keying are done **symmetrically and progressively**, alternating between opposite sides of the building and between opposing wedges of each pair.

### 7.2 Re-tightening — 増し締め (mashi-jime)
Green or partly seasoned members shrink across the grain after assembly, especially in the first one or two years. Nuki become loose in their holes, wedges back out, and shachi and komisen joints open slightly.

- **When:** after the roof covering (tiles or thatch) and the walls are on, and again after the first dry season or first year. Traditional carpenters return to re-drive the wedges before the walls are finished where possible, and in any case at the first maintenance visit.
- **How:** re-drive nuki wedges in pairs. Re-drive shachisen. Where a wedge has bottomed, add a thin shim wedge (差し楔) or replace the wedge with a thicker one. Re-drive komisen only where they have been left proud and the draw has not been used up.
- **Recording:** note in the building records which wedges were re-driven and when (→ Ch. 16).

**R09-035** — (Should) Plan for **at least one re-tightening** of all accessible wedges and keys after the first dry season. Leave wedge heads proud, and where walls conceal the wedges, **leave access** (e.g. a removable board or an unplastered strip) at key posts, or do the re-tightening before the finish coat of the walls.

## 8. Hand-forged nails — 和釘 (wa-kugi)

### 8.1 Character
Traditional Japanese nails are **hand-forged** from low-carbon iron (historically iron smelted from iron sand in the *tatara* process, 和鉄 *wa-tetsu*). The shank is **square in section and tapers** to a point. The **head is formed by the smith** in several patterns, and the name of the nail comes from the head. Wa-kugi are **thick and long** relative to modern wire nails, and their square shank gives a large surface area and good withdrawal resistance. Old wa-kugi in temple buildings have in many cases survived centuries with limited corrosion. This is attributed to the purity and slag structure of tatara iron and to the protection of the nail inside the wood. Reproduction wa-kugi for the restoration of major temples have been made by specialist smiths in recent decades (e.g. for the reconstruction work at Yakushi-ji). Specific attributions are → Ch. 01 / Ch. 16.

### 8.2 Types (by head form)

| Type | Japanese | Reading | Head | Typical use |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Rolled-head nail (large head)** | 巻頭釘 | *makigashira-kugi* | head beaten flat and wide (like a fan) and **rolled over** | exposed nailing where the head is meant to be seen: boards, gates, fittings. It is the decorative choice. |
| **Rolled-head nail (small head)** | 頭巻釘 | *atamamaki-kugi* | as above, but the head is **smaller** | the same uses where the head should be **less conspicuous** |
| **Folded-head nail** | 皆折釘 (also written 替折釘, 階折釘) | *kaiore-kugi* | square shank, the head simply **bent over** and hammered tight | wall boards, fences (e.g. 木賊張り), dog-fences (犬矢来), 駒寄せ, charred-board (焼板) walls. A general-purpose nail. |
| **Double-pointed nail** | 合釘 | *aikugi* | none: **pointed at both ends** | **hidden edge-joining** of boards (the nail is driven into one board edge and the next board is driven onto it). Used in floors, ceilings, doors and fittings. |
| **Hook or L-nail** | 折釘 | *orekugi* | shank bent at a right angle to form a hook | hanging (lanterns, blinds, scrolls), eaves hooks |
| **Hidden-staple nail** | 目鎹釘 | *mekasugai-kugi* | small staple-like or angled head designed to be hidden | secret fixing of floorboards (e.g. 縁甲板 *enkōita*) to joists from the side or back, so that no nail shows on the face (sukiya floors). Hanging ceiling boards and kamoi. |

Other named forms exist (e.g. nails with square heads, rivet-like 鋲 *byō* for decorative work, and regional names). **Nomenclature varies between smiths and regions**, and the table covers the forms most commonly listed by makers of traditional hardware.

**Sizes:** designated in sun (寸) of length, from c. **1 sun (30 mm) to over 1 shaku (300 mm)** for large temple nails. Common house sizes are 1.5–3 sun (45–90 mm).

### 8.3 Use rules

**R09-036** — (Should) Wa-kugi are driven into a **pre-bored pilot hole** (下穴, *shitaana*) made with a square awl (四つ目錐 *yotsume-giri*) or a gimlet (三つ目錐), c. 2/3 of the shank's depth into the receiving piece. Hard species and thin boards near their ends are always pre-bored.
*Why:* the thick square shank acts as a wedge and splits the wood if driven without a pilot.

**R09-037** — (Should) Orient the **square shank's faces** so that the wedge action of the taper is **across the grain as little as possible**: drive with one face parallel to the grain of the board.

**R09-038** — (Must) In traditional structural frames, **nails are never the primary structural connection** of posts, beams and plates. They fix boards, rafters, battens and fittings. Rafters are nailed to plates (→ Ch. 08 §8.1). Nuki-to-lath (komai) ties are lashed or nailed.

### 8.4 Modern wire nails
Modern nails (丸釘 *marukugi*, N-nails, CN-nails, galvanised or stainless steel) are used in modern frames for sheathing, brace plates and connectors, according to specified schedules (→ Ch. 17). In restoration they are **not** used as visible replacements for wa-kugi in listed buildings. Where hidden, the conservation authority's guidance governs (→ Ch. 16).

## 9. Other metal: staples, straps, bolts, decorative hardware

### 9.1 Staples — 鎹 (kasugai)
- **Form:** a bar of iron (or steel) with **pointed legs** bent at each end, driven across a joint to hold two members together.
- **Types:**
  - **両手鎹 (ryōte-kasugai)**: both legs in the **same plane** (the common U-shaped staple)
  - **手違い鎹 (techigai-kasugai)**: the legs are **twisted at 90°** to each other, so one leg enters one member's face with the grain and the other enters the other member across a corner. Used where the two members' faces are at right angles or where the grain directions differ.
  - **片手鎹 (katate-kasugai)**: a form with a leg at one end only, the other end being flat with a nail hole or otherwise fixed. Forms vary.
- **Sizes:** bar c. 6–9 mm (square or round), c. 90–150 mm between legs, with legs c. 30–50 mm (representative).
- **Use:** roof struts to beams and purlins (→ Ch. 08 §8.3), temporary and secondary ties, scaffolding and falsework. In traditional buildings they are used in roof spaces and concealed locations, **not** on exposed finished faces.
- **Rules:**
  **R09-039** — (Should) Kasugai are driven with **each leg across the grain** of its member (the leg's flat face parallel to the grain) and at least **c. 1.5 × the leg length from end grain**. On hard species, pre-bore.

### 9.2 Straps, stirrups and bolts (modern)

| Hardware | Japanese | Description | Use |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Iron band / strap** | 帯金 (*obigane*), 短冊金物 (*tanzaku kanamono*) | flat strap nailed or bolted across a joint | historic: binding splits and heavy joints in large buildings. Modern: tension connections across girts and splices. |
| **Stirrup / box strap** | 箱金物 (*hako kanamono*) | U-shaped or box strap wrapping a member | modern: girt-to-through-post connections, beam hangers |
| **Hagoita bolt** | 羽子板ボルト (*hagoita bolt*) | a flat plate (shaped like a battledore, 羽子板) with a bolt at one end. The plate is bolted to one member and the bolt passes through the other. | modern: **beam-to-plate, girt-to-post and beam-to-beam** pull-out restraint (→ Ch. 08 §3.2, §3.4). Bolt typically M12. |
| **Hold-down** | ホールダウン金物 | a heavy bracket bolted to the post and anchored to the foundation (or to the post below) | modern: **anchoring posts against uplift**, graded by capacity (→ Ch. 17) |
| **Anchor bolt** | アンカーボルト | a bolt cast into the concrete foundation and passing through the sill | modern: **sill to foundation**, typically M12, at maximum spacings set by the governing specification and near every corner and splice (→ Ch. 08 R08-076, → Ch. 17) |
| **Corner plates, L-plates, T-plates, brace plates** | かど金物, L型, T型, 筋交いプレート | pressed steel plates with nails or screws | modern: post-end and brace-end connections |

**R09-040** — (Should) In modern frames, **metal hardware supplements, and does not replace, a correctly cut shiguchi**. The hardware must be placed so that it does not cut into the joint, and bolt holes must not pass through tenons, dovetail necks or pin holes.

### 9.3 Decorative hardware — 飾り金具 (kazari-kanagu)
Decorative metal fittings in temples, shrines, palaces and shoin interiors protect end grain and cover nail heads, and are an integral part of the architectural order (→ Ch. 12, → Ch. 14):
- **八双金物 (hassō kanamono)**: tapering decorative straps on **gate and door leaves** and on the ends of members, historically also functioning as straps binding the boards of the leaf
- **釘隠し (kugikakushi)**: **nail-head covers**, most familiarly on the **nageshi (長押)** of shoin rooms, over the nails that fix the nageshi to the posts. Designs include the six-lobed 六葉 (*rokuyō*) and family crests and flower motifs.
- **Rafter-end and hip-end caps (垂木先金物, 隅木金物)**: protect end grain from weather
- **乳金物 (chi-kanamono)**: stud-like bosses on gate leaves
- **Materials:** copper, gilt copper (金銅 *kondō*), brass, iron (sometimes lacquered or blackened), and silver in the highest-grade interiors

**R09-041** — (Should) Decorative hardware is fixed with **its own pins or nails of compatible metal** (copper nails for copper fittings), and is removable for repair and re-gilding. In restoration, **original fittings are recorded, conserved and reused**. Replicas are marked as such (→ Ch. 16).

### 9.4 Corrosion, tannin reactions and compatibility

- **Iron and tannin-rich woods:** oaks (kashi, nara), **chestnut (kuri)** and, to a lesser degree, keyaki are **rich in tannins**. In contact with iron and moisture they produce **blue-black iron-tannate stains** and **accelerate the corrosion** of the iron. The wood around a corroding nail is degraded ("nail sickness"). Sugi and hinoki are acidic enough to corrode iron in wet conditions too, though they are less tannic.
- **Consequences:** stained finish surfaces, loosened nails, split members around expanding rust, and the loss of the hardware's strength in hidden locations.
- **Mitigation:** keep iron **dry** (inside the envelope, above damp). Use hot-dip galvanised or **stainless** steel for modern hardware in contact with oak or chestnut and in exposed or damp positions. Use copper or bronze for decorative fittings and exterior nails. Isolate dissimilar metals (e.g. copper roofing and iron fixings) to avoid galvanic corrosion.

**R09-042** — (Should) **Avoid bare iron in contact with kuri, kashi, keyaki or other tannin-rich species** in damp or exposed positions. Where iron is unavoidable, use galvanised or stainless steel, or isolate it.

### 9.5 When metal is acceptable — traditional practice and conservation
In **traditional practice**, metal was used for:
- nails for boards, rafters and fittings
- staples in roof spaces and temporary works
- straps and bands on gates, very large members and repairs
- decorative and protective fittings

Metal was **not** the primary means of connecting the structural frame. In **modern construction** under the Building Standard Law, specified metal connectors are generally **required** at post ends, braces, beams and sills, except where a "traditional method" design is verified by engineering (→ Ch. 17).

In **conservation** of historic buildings, practice balances **authenticity**, **reversibility** and **safety**:

**R09-043** — (Must) In the repair of historic buildings, any added metal reinforcement is **reversible** (removable without further damage to original material), **minimal**, **recorded** (drawings, photographs, repair report), and **compatible** (non-corroding, non-staining). Original members are not cut to fit modern hardware where an alternative exists.

**R09-044** — (Should) Where possible, **repair joints with wood** (new pins, wedges, keys, chigiri, yatoi tenons, 根継ぎ *netsugi* splices, → Ch. 07, → Ch. 16) before introducing metal. Where metal is necessary, place it **concealed or clearly distinguishable** according to the conservation authority's policy.

## 10. Adhesives

Three traditional adhesives are important:

| Adhesive | Japanese | Material | Properties | Traditional use |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Rice paste** | 続飯 (*sokui*) | cooked rice **kneaded and worked** (traditionally with a spatula on a board) into a stiff paste | good initial tack, reversible with water, **weak in moisture**, can be attacked by insects and mould | paper on shōji and fusuma, veneers, light fittings (tategu), inlays, small parts of fine joinery, positioning pieces |
| **Hide or animal glue** | 膠 (*nikawa*) | collagen glue from hides or bones, applied hot | strong, reversible with heat and moisture, **creeps and fails in damp** | furniture and cabinetry (指物 *sashimono* in the furniture sense), musical instruments, the binder for pigments and gesso in painted architecture |
| **Lacquer** | 漆 (*urushi*); as adhesive 麦漆 (*mugi-urushi*, lacquer mixed with wheat flour) or 刻苧 (*kokuso*, lacquer with fibres as filler) | the sap of *Toxicodendron vernicifluum*, cured in humidity | **very durable** and water-resistant once cured, difficult to reverse | lacquerware repair and joinery, fine fittings, filling and bonding in lacquered architecture |

**Why structural joints are dry (unglued):**
1. **Movement:** large timbers shrink and swell by several millimetres across the grain. Glue lines between members of different grain orientation fail, or the members crack.
2. **Adjustment:** frames are plumbed after assembly and re-tightened after drying (§7). Glue would prevent both.
3. **Repair:** Japanese buildings are designed to be **dismantled and repaired** (解体修理, → Ch. 16). Glued joints cannot be taken apart without destruction.
4. **Durability:** traditional glues are weak in the damp conditions of a building's structure.

**R09-045** — (Must) **Structural joints of the timber frame are not glued.** Adhesives are confined to fittings, furniture, paper coverings, veneers and decorative work.

**R09-046** — (Should) In restoration, **modern synthetic adhesives and consolidants (epoxy etc.) are used only where the conservation authority accepts them**, and not where a wood repair is feasible, because they are not reversible.

## 11. Rope and lashing

### 11.1 Where lashing is used
In **minka roofs of the sasu (叉首 / 扠首) type** and in **gasshō-zukuri** (合掌造り) of Shirakawa-gō and Gokayama, the roof structure above the tie beams is assembled **largely with lashings rather than carpentry joints** (→ Ch. 11, → Ch. 13):
- **sasu (principal rafters)** are paired, crossed or butted at the apex, and **lashed** together, with the ridge member held in the crotch.
- **horizontal members** (purlin-like poles, variously called 屋中 *yanaka*, 母屋, and others regionally) are **lashed** across the sasu.
- **common rafters and battens** (垂木, and the 小舞 *komai* or bamboo battens that carry the thatch) are **lashed** to the purlins.
- the **sasu feet** are pointed and seated in sockets or notches in the ends of the tie beams (叉首尻, *sasujiri*). They are held by their own thrust and weight rather than by lashing, and forms vary regionally.

### 11.2 Materials
- **藁縄 (wara-nawa)**: rice-straw rope, the most common. It is cheap and renewable, and loses strength over time, especially if damp.
- **ネソ (neso)**: young stems of **witch hazel (マンサク, *mansaku*, *Hamamelis japonica*)**, c. 4 years old, cut green. The fibres are **twisted and loosened while green** to make them pliable. They are wound around the joint and the end is tucked in. As they dry, **they shrink and tighten**. In Shirakawa-gō neso is the characteristic binding of the gasshō roof and is reported to last until the next re-thatching (on the order of decades).
- **藤蔓 (fuji-zuru)**: wisteria vine, used green. It is strong and flexible and shrinks as it dries.
- **Other vines and fibres** in regional practice: kudzu, hemp rope (麻縄), and split bamboo ties (竹).

### 11.3 Rules for lashing

**R09-047** — (Should) Green plant bindings (neso, fuji-zuru) are **applied green and allowed to dry in place**, so that they **tighten by shrinkage**. They are never applied dry and forced.

**R09-048** — (Should) Lashings are wound **across the joint diagonally in both directions** (a crossed lashing) where two members cross, and **finished by tucking or twisting the end back under the turns**. Knots that can loosen are avoided on structural bindings.

**R09-049** — (Should) Rope and plant lashings are **inspected and renewed at each re-thatching**, when they are exposed. Traditional communities do this collectively (結, *yui*; → Ch. 13, → Ch. 16).

**R09-050** — (Should) Lashed roof frames depend on a **dry, smoky environment**: the smoke of the hearth (囲炉裏, *irori*) dries the roof space and deters insects. Where the hearth is no longer used, **increase inspection frequency** and provide ventilation, because straw lashings and thatch decay faster.

**R09-051** — (Should) In the conservation of lashed roofs, **use the traditional material** (straw rope, neso, fuji) wherever it is available. Where synthetic rope or wire is substituted for safety or supply reasons, record it and keep it out of sight as far as the conservation policy requires.
